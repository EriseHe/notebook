## 2.1 Regular and Singular Perturbations

Assume throughout that

$$
0 \le \varepsilon \ll 1.
$$

---

## 2.2 Example 1: Regular Perturbation

Consider

$$
x^2 + 2\varepsilon x - 1 = 0.
$$

Assume a regular perturbation expansion

$$
x(\varepsilon)
=
x_0 + \varepsilon x_1 + \varepsilon^2 x_2 + \cdots.
$$

This is a **regular perturbation** expansion: the solution is expanded in integer powers of $\varepsilon$.

The two branches behave as

$$
x(\varepsilon)
\sim
\pm 1 - \varepsilon \pm \frac{\varepsilon^2}{2} + \cdots.
$$

---

## 2.3 Example 2: Singular Perturbation

Consider

$$
\varepsilon x^2 + 2x - 1 = 0.
$$

A regular expansion is not sufficient for all solution branches. One possibility is to rescale the variable.

Let

$$
X = \varepsilon x.
$$

Then

$$
x = \frac{X}{\varepsilon},
$$

and the equation becomes

$$
X^2 + 2X - \varepsilon = 0.
$$

Alternatively, use a singular perturbation expansion of the form

$$
x
=
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}x_{-1}
+
x_0
+
\varepsilon x_1
+
\cdots.
$$

Thus the solution may contain a leading term of order $1/\varepsilon$, rather than order $1$.

---

## 2.4 Example 3: Failure of an Integer-Power Expansion

Consider

$$
(1-\varepsilon)x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0.
$$

At $\varepsilon = 0$,

$$
x_0^2 - 2x_0 + 1 = 0,
$$

so

$$
x_0 = 1
$$

is a **double root**.

### 2.4.1 Attempt 1: Ordinary perturbation series

One might try

$$
x(\varepsilon)
=
x_0
+
\varepsilon x_1
+
\varepsilon^2 x_2
+
\cdots.
$$

But this approach **fails**.

From the exact solution,

$$
x(\varepsilon)
=
\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}
\pm
\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{1-\varepsilon},
$$

and hence

$$
x(\varepsilon)
\sim
1 \pm \sqrt{\varepsilon} + O(\varepsilon).
$$

The leading correction is therefore of order $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ rather than $\varepsilon$.

---

## 2.5 Generalized Perturbation Ansatz

Try instead

$$
x
\sim
x_0
+
\varepsilon^\alpha x_\alpha
+
\varepsilon x_1
+
\cdots,
$$

where

$$
0 < \alpha < 1.
$$

Substitute this into

$$
(1-\varepsilon)x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0.
$$

Then

$$
(1-\varepsilon)
\left(
x_0+\varepsilon^\alpha x_\alpha+\varepsilon x_1
\right)^2
-
2
\left(
x_0+\varepsilon^\alpha x_\alpha+\varepsilon x_1
\right)
+
1
\sim 0.
$$

Expanding and grouping powers of $\varepsilon$ gives

$$
x_0^2 - 2x_0 + 1
$$

$$
+ \varepsilon^\alpha
\left(
2x_0x_\alpha - 2x_\alpha
\right)
$$

$$
+ \varepsilon
\left(
-x_0^2 + 2x_0x_1 - 2x_1
\right)
$$

$$
+ \varepsilon^{2\alpha}x_\alpha^2
+\cdots
\sim 0.
$$

---

### 2.5.1 Order $O(1)$

We obtain

$$
x_0^2 - 2x_0 + 1 = 0,
$$

so

$$
\boxed{x_0 = 1}.
$$

---

### 2.5.2 Order $O(\varepsilon^\alpha)$

We obtain

$$
2x_\alpha(x_0-1)=0.
$$

Since $x_0=1$,

$$
2x_\alpha(x_0-1)=0
$$

is satisfied for **all values of $\alpha$**.

Therefore, the $O(\varepsilon^\alpha)$ equation does not determine $\alpha$.

---

## 2.6 Higher-Order Balance

We need to look at the higher-order terms.

The two relevant orders are

$$
\varepsilon
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\varepsilon^{2\alpha}.
$$

For these terms to balance,

$$
2\alpha = 1,
$$

so

$$
\boxed{\alpha = \frac12}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\varepsilon^{2\alpha} = \varepsilon.
$$

At order $O(\varepsilon)$,

$$
-x_0^2
+
2x_0x_1
-
2x_1
+
x_\alpha^2
=
0.
$$

Using $x_0=1$,

$$
-1 + 2x_1 - 2x_1 + x_\alpha^2 = 0,
$$

hence

$$
x_\alpha^2 = 1.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{x_\alpha = \pm 1}.
$$

Since $\alpha=\frac12$,

$$
\boxed{
x(\varepsilon)
\sim
1 \pm \sqrt{\varepsilon} + \cdots
}.
$$

---

## 2.7 Main Takeaway

A typical outcome in singular perturbation problems is that we may need to consider a **higher-order balance to determine the leading-order perturbation**.

In particular, the correction does not necessarily occur at an integer power of $\varepsilon$.

Here, the double root at $\varepsilon=0$ causes the usual expansion

$$
x=x_0+\varepsilon x_1+\cdots
$$

to fail, and the correct leading perturbation instead scales as

$$
\varepsilon^{1/2}.
$$




$$
\begin{align} 
&\underbrace{ f(\epsilon)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}a_{n} \epsilon^{n} }_{ \text{power series} } \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{ f(\epsilon) \sim \sum^{N}_{n=0} a_{n}\delta_{n}(\epsilon) }_{  \text{asymtotic series} }
\end{align}
$$
- $N$ is finite ($N=1$ or $N=2$ is  )
- no notion of continuity as $N\to \infty$. In fact, typically the symtotpic series diverges as $N \to \infty$
- exceptionally accurate as $\epsilon>0$

What qualifies as asymptotic function sequence $\delta_{0}(\epsilon), \delta_{1}(\epsilon),\dots,\delta_{N}(\epsilon)$

$$
\lim_{ \epsilon \to 0 } \frac{\delta_{n+1}(\epsilon)}{\delta_{n}(\epsilon)} = 0
$$
well ordered expansion.

examples:
$$
\begin{align}
\delta_{0}=1, \delta_{1}=\epsilon, \delta_{2}=\epsilon^{2} \\
\delta_{0}=\frac{1}{2}, \delta_{1}=1, \delta_{2}=\epsilon\\ 
\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{2}, \delta_{1}=\sqrt{ \epsilon }, \delta_{2}=\epsilon\\

\end{align}
$$
Compute $a_{n}$:

$$
a_{0} = \lim_{ \epsilon \to \infty }  \frac{f(\epsilon)}{\delta_{0}(\epsilon)}
$$

$$
\begin{align}
\lim_{ \epsilon \to \infty }  \frac{f(\epsilon)}{\delta_{0}(\epsilon)}  & = \sum^{N}_{n=0} a_{n} \frac{\delta_{n}(\epsilon)}{\delta_{0}(\epsilon)} \\
 & =a_{0}
\end{align}
$$
$$
a_{n+1}=\lim_{ \epsilon \to \infty } \frac{ \delta(\epsilon) - \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{k}\delta_{k}(\epsilon)}{\delta_{n+1}(\epsilon)}
$$
Remark.

**The expansion is successful if not all of the $a_{n}$ are zero, and none are infinite.**

The same function $f(\epsilon)$ has many different asymptotic expansions.

Examples. 

$$
\begin{align}
e^{\epsilon} \sim  & 1 + \epsilon + \frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2} \\ &  1 + \sin\epsilon + \frac{6}{12} \sin ^{2}(\epsilon)
\end{align}
$$
but the coefficients $a_n$ are uniquely determined once $\delta_0,\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_n$ have been chosen.


Historically this was often used to approximate integrals

Examples.
$$
\text{eft}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{ \pi }} \int_{0}^{x} e^{-s^{2}} ds\quad \text{where} \quad \text{erf}(\infty)= 1
$$
What to do if $x$ is large but finite? Taylor series can be integrated term by term for finite $X$, but convergence is very slow. 

Much better results by using integration by parts.


$$
\text{erf(x)} = 1 - \underbrace{ \frac{2}{\sqrt{ \pi }}\int_{x}^{\infty} e^{-s^{2}}ds }_{ \text{erfc}(x) }
$$
Then we have

$$\begin{align}
\operatorname{erfc}(x)
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}
\int_{x^2}^{\infty} e^{-q}\frac{dq}{\sqrt{q}} \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}
\left(
\left.-\frac{e^{-q}}{\sqrt{q}}\right|_{x^2}^{\infty}
-\frac{1}{2}\int_{x^2}^{\infty} e^{-q}\frac{dq}{q^{3/2}}
\right) \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{e^{-x^2}}{x}
- \text{h.o.t.}
\end{align}$$

So we have

$$
\operatorname{erf}(x)
\sim
1- \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{e^{-x^2}}{x},
\qquad x\to\infty.
$$



### Good News? About Linear ODEs

For $y(x)$, consider

$$
(1-\epsilon)y''-2y'+y=0.
$$

Solve by trying
$$
y=e^{\lambda x}.
$$

Then

$$\begin{align}
y' &= \lambda e^{\lambda x}=\lambda y, \\
y'' &= \lambda^2 e^{\lambda x}=\lambda^2 y.
\end{align}$$

Substituting into the ODE gives

$$
(1-\epsilon)\lambda^2-2\lambda+1=0.
$$

This is the problem we looked at in the first lecture!
