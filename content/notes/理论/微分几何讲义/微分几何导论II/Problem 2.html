Problem 2

1 Problem 2

Statement. Let \(M\) be a smooth \(n\)-manifold, let \(X\) be a vector field on \(M\), and let \(\varphi \in \Lambda(M)\). Prove Cartan’s magic formula \[ L_X \varphi = i_X d\varphi + d(i_X\varphi). \]

1.1 Big picture / idea

This identity says that the Lie derivative of a differential form can be split into two simpler operations:

  1. first differentiate the form by \(d\), then insert \(X\) by \(i_X\)
  2. or first insert \(X\), then differentiate

and the sum of those two is exactly the infinitesimal change of the form along the flow of \(X\).

So the geometric idea is:

  • \(L_X\) measures how the form changes as we move along the flow generated by \(X\)
  • \(i_X\) plugs the vector field \(X\) into the form
  • \(d\) measures infinitesimal variation of the form

Cartan’s formula says these three operations are not independent. They are tied together by a very rigid algebraic identity.

The cleanest proof is to define \[ D := i_X d + d i_X \] and show that \(D\) and \(L_X\) are the same derivation on the whole exterior algebra.

1.2 Step 1: define the operator

Set \[ D := i_X d + d i_X. \]

We will prove that \[ D\varphi = L_X\varphi \] for every differential form \(\varphi\).

1.3 Step 2: show that \(D\) is a derivation of degree \(0\)

Let \(\alpha \in \Lambda^r(M)\) and \(\beta \in \Lambda^s(M)\). Then \[ \begin{aligned} D(\alpha \wedge \beta) &= i_X d(\alpha \wedge \beta) + d\bigl(i_X(\alpha \wedge \beta)\bigr). \end{aligned} \]

Now use the product rules for \(d\) and \(i_X\): \[ d(\alpha \wedge \beta)=d\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^r \alpha \wedge d\beta \] and \[ i_X(\alpha \wedge \beta)=i_X\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^r \alpha \wedge i_X\beta. \]

Substitute these in: \[ \begin{aligned} D(\alpha \wedge \beta) &= i_X\bigl(d\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^r \alpha \wedge d\beta\bigr) + d\bigl(i_X\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^r \alpha \wedge i_X\beta\bigr). \end{aligned} \]

Now expand the first term: \[ \begin{aligned} i_X\bigl(d\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^r \alpha \wedge d\beta\bigr) &= i_X(d\alpha \wedge \beta) + (-1)^r i_X(\alpha \wedge d\beta) \\ &= i_X d\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^{r+1} d\alpha \wedge i_X\beta \\ &\qquad + (-1)^r i_X\alpha \wedge d\beta + \alpha \wedge i_X d\beta. \end{aligned} \]

Now expand the second term: \[ \begin{aligned} d\bigl(i_X\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^r \alpha \wedge i_X\beta\bigr) &= d(i_X\alpha \wedge \beta) + (-1)^r d(\alpha \wedge i_X\beta) \\ &= d(i_X\alpha)\wedge \beta + (-1)^{r-1} i_X\alpha \wedge d\beta \\ &\qquad + (-1)^r d\alpha \wedge i_X\beta + \alpha \wedge d(i_X\beta). \end{aligned} \]

Add the two expansions: \[ \begin{aligned} D(\alpha \wedge \beta) &= \bigl(i_X d\alpha + d(i_X\alpha)\bigr)\wedge \beta + \alpha \wedge \bigl(i_X d\beta + d(i_X\beta)\bigr) \\ &\qquad + \Bigl((-1)^{r+1}+(-1)^r\Bigr)d\alpha \wedge i_X\beta \\ &\qquad + \Bigl((-1)^r+(-1)^{r-1}\Bigr)i_X\alpha \wedge d\beta. \end{aligned} \]

But \[ (-1)^{r+1}+(-1)^r = 0, \qquad (-1)^r+(-1)^{r-1}=0, \] so the mixed terms cancel. Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} D(\alpha \wedge \beta) &= \bigl(i_X d\alpha + d(i_X\alpha)\bigr)\wedge \beta + \alpha \wedge \bigl(i_X d\beta + d(i_X\beta)\bigr) \\ &= D\alpha \wedge \beta + \alpha \wedge D\beta. \end{aligned} \]

So \(D\) is a derivation of degree \(0\).

1.4 Step 3: recall that \(L_X\) is also a derivation of degree \(0\)

For the Lie derivative of forms, we have \[ L_X(\alpha \wedge \beta)=L_X\alpha \wedge \beta + \alpha \wedge L_X\beta. \]

So \(L_X\) is also a derivation of degree \(0\).

This is the key structural point: both \(D\) and \(L_X\) are derivations on the exterior algebra. So to prove they are equal everywhere, it is enough to check them on generators.

1.5 Step 4: check equality on functions

Let \(f \in C^\infty(M)=\Lambda^0(M)\). Then \[ \begin{aligned} Df &= i_X(df) + d(i_X f) \\ &= i_X(df) + d(0) \\ &= df(X) \\ &= Xf. \end{aligned} \]

But the Lie derivative of a function is just differentiation along \(X\), so \[ L_X f = Xf. \]

Hence \[ Df=L_Xf \] for all functions \(f\).

1.6 Step 5: check equality on \(1\)-forms

Let \(\alpha\) be a \(1\)-form, and let \(Y\) be any vector field. We compute \((D\alpha)(Y)\).

By definition, \[ \begin{aligned} (D\alpha)(Y) &= \bigl(i_X d\alpha + d(i_X\alpha)\bigr)(Y) \\ &= (i_X d\alpha)(Y) + d(i_X\alpha)(Y) \\ &= d\alpha(X,Y) + Y\bigl(\alpha(X)\bigr). \end{aligned} \]

Now use the formula for \(d\alpha\) on two vector fields: \[ d\alpha(X,Y)=X(\alpha(Y)) - Y(\alpha(X)) - \alpha([X,Y]). \]

Substitute: \[ \begin{aligned} (D\alpha)(Y) &= X(\alpha(Y)) - Y(\alpha(X)) - \alpha([X,Y]) + Y(\alpha(X)) \\ &= X(\alpha(Y)) - \alpha([X,Y]). \end{aligned} \]

Now compute the Lie derivative of \(\alpha\) on \(Y\): \[ (L_X\alpha)(Y)=X(\alpha(Y))-\alpha(L_XY). \]

Since \[ L_XY=[X,Y], \] we get \[ \begin{aligned} (L_X\alpha)(Y) &= X(\alpha(Y))-\alpha([X,Y]). \end{aligned} \]

Therefore \[ (D\alpha)(Y)=(L_X\alpha)(Y) \] for every vector field \(Y\), hence \[ D\alpha=L_X\alpha \] for every \(1\)-form \(\alpha\).

1.7 Step 6: conclude for all forms

Every differential form is built from functions and \(1\)-forms using wedge products and addition. Locally, any \(r\)-form can be written as \[ \varphi=\sum_{i_1<\cdots<i_r} \varphi_{i_1\cdots i_r}\, dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_r}. \]

Since both \(D\) and \(L_X\) are degree-\(0\) derivations, and since they agree on functions and on \(1\)-forms, they must agree on all differential forms.

Therefore \[ \boxed{L_X\varphi = i_X d\varphi + d(i_X\varphi).} \]

1.8 Final comment on why this proof works

The point is not brute-force coordinate computation. The real idea is that the exterior algebra is generated by low-degree objects:

  • \(0\)-forms = functions
  • \(1\)-forms

Once two derivations agree on these generators, they agree on everything. So the proof reduces a statement about all forms of all degrees to a computation on just:

  • functions
  • \(1\)-forms

That is the conceptual core of Cartan’s formula.
