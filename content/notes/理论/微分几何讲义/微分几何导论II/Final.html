1 Problem 2

Statement. Let \(M\) be a smooth manifold, let \(X\) be a vector field on \(M\), and let \(\varphi \in \Lambda(M)\). Prove Cartan’s magic formula \[ L_X \varphi = i_X d\varphi + d(i_X\varphi). \]

1.1 Idea

Define \[ D := i_X d + d i_X. \] We will prove that \(D=L_X\) on all differential forms.

The strategy is:

  1. show \(D\) is a derivation of degree \(0\) on \(\Lambda(M)\);
  2. show \(L_X\) is also a derivation of degree \(0\);
  3. check that \(D\) and \(L_X\) agree on functions and on \(1\)-forms;
  4. conclude they agree on all forms, since differential forms are generated by functions and \(1\)-forms.

Boothby uses exactly the operators \(L_X\), \(i_X\), and \(d\) in this differential-form setting.

1.2 Step 1: \(D=i_Xd+d,i_X\) is a derivation of degree \(0\)

Let \(\alpha \in \Lambda^r(M)\) and \(\beta \in \Lambda^s(M)\). Then \[ \begin{aligned} D(\alpha \wedge \beta) &= i_X d(\alpha \wedge \beta)+d\bigl(i_X(\alpha \wedge \beta)\bigr) \ &= i_X\bigl(d\alpha \wedge \beta+(-1)^r \alpha \wedge d\beta\bigr) +d\bigl((i_X\alpha)\wedge \beta+(-1)^r \alpha \wedge i_X\beta\bigr). \end{aligned} \]

So \(D\) is a derivation of degree \(0\).

1.3 Step 2: \(L_X\) is also a derivation of degree \(0\)

For differential forms, the Lie derivative satisfies \[ L_X(\alpha \wedge \beta)=L_X\alpha \wedge \beta+\alpha \wedge L_X\beta. \] So \(L_X\) is also a degree-\(0\) derivation. This is one of the standard properties Boothby records for \(L_X\).

1.4 Step 3: check equality on functions

Let \(f \in C^\infty(M)=\Lambda^0(M)\). Then \[ \begin{aligned} Df &= i_X(df)+d(i_X f) \ &= i_X(df)+d(0) \ &= df(X) \ &= Xf \ &= L_X f. \end{aligned} \]

So \(D=L_X\) on \(0\)-forms.

1.5 Step 4: check equality on \(1\)-forms

Let \(\alpha\) be a \(1\)-form. We evaluate both sides on an arbitrary vector field \(Y\).

First, \[ \begin{aligned} (D\alpha)(Y) &= \bigl(i_X d\alpha + d(i_X\alpha)\bigr)(Y) \ &= d\alpha(X,Y)+Y\bigl(\alpha(X)\bigr). \end{aligned} \]

Now use the definition of \(d\alpha\) on two vector fields: \[ d\alpha(X,Y)=X(\alpha(Y))-Y(\alpha(X))-\alpha([X,Y]). \] Hence \[ \begin{aligned} (D\alpha)(Y) &= X(\alpha(Y))-Y(\alpha(X))-\alpha([X,Y])+Y(\alpha(X)) \ &= X(\alpha(Y))-\alpha([X,Y]). \end{aligned} \]

On the other hand, the Lie derivative of a \(1\)-form satisfies \[ (L_X\alpha)(Y)=X(\alpha(Y))-\alpha([X,Y]). \] Therefore \[ (D\alpha)(Y)=(L_X\alpha)(Y) \qquad \text{for every vector field }Y. \] So \[ D\alpha=L_X\alpha \] for every \(1\)-form \(\alpha\).

1.6 Step 5: conclude for all forms

Every differential form is locally a finite sum of terms of the form \[ f, dx^{i_1}\wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{i_r}, \] so the exterior algebra is generated by functions and \(1\)-forms. Since both \(D\) and \(L_X\) are degree-\(0\) derivations and they agree on functions and \(1\)-forms, they agree on all of \(\Lambda(M)\).

Therefore, \[ \boxed{L_X\varphi=i_Xd\varphi+d(i_X\varphi)} \] for every \(\varphi \in \Lambda(M)\).

2 Problem 3

Statement. Let \(M\) be a manifold with smooth boundary \(\partial M\). Let \(g\) be a Riemannian metric smooth on \(M\) such that \(\partial M\) is strictly convex. Prove that for \((x,v)\in \partial_+SM\setminus \partial_0SM\), the geodesic \(\gamma_{x,v}(t)\) stays in \(M\) for sufficiently small \(t>0\).

2.1 Idea

Near a boundary point, Boothby models \(M\) on the half-space \[ H^n={(x^1,\dots,x^n)\in \mathbb{R}^n : x^n\ge 0}, \] with the boundary corresponding to \(x^n=0\). So the last coordinate plays the role of a boundary defining function. If the initial velocity points strictly inward and is not tangent to the boundary, then the last coordinate has positive derivative at \(t=0\). That alone forces the geodesic to move into the interior for small positive time. The final problem statement is exactly this claim.

2.2 Step 1: choose a boundary chart

Since \(M\) is a smooth manifold with boundary, there exists a coordinate neighborhood \((U,\varphi)\) of \(x\) such that \[ \varphi(U)\subset H^n, \qquad \varphi(x)=0, \] and \[ \varphi(U\cap \partial M)=\varphi(U)\cap {x^n=0}. \] Thus, in these local coordinates, points of \(M\) correspond to points with \(x^n\ge 0\), boundary points correspond to \(x^n=0\), and interior points correspond to \(x^n>0\). This is exactly Boothby’s local model for a manifold with boundary.

Define the local boundary defining function \[ \rho := x^n \circ \varphi. \] Then, on \(U\), \[ U\cap M = {\rho \ge 0}, \qquad U\cap \partial M = {\rho = 0}, \] and \(d\rho \neq 0\) on \(\partial M\).

2.3 Step 2: look at the boundary defining function along the geodesic

Let \[ \gamma(t):=\gamma_{x,v}(t) \] be the geodesic with \[ \gamma(0)=x, \qquad \dot\gamma(0)=v. \] Since geodesics are smooth, the function \[ h(t):=\rho(\gamma(t)) \] is smooth for \(|t|\) small.

At \(t=0\) we have \[ h(0)=\rho(x)=0. \]

Differentiate: \[ \begin{aligned} h'(0) &= \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\rho(\gamma(t)) \ &= d\rho_x(\dot\gamma(0)) \ &= d\rho_x(v). \end{aligned} \]

Now \((x,v)\in \partial_+SM\setminus \partial_0SM\) means that \(x\in \partial M\), the vector \(v\) is not tangent to \(\partial M\), and it points strictly inward. In the coordinates above, this is exactly the statement \[ d\rho_x(v)>0. \] Equivalently, \[ h'(0)>0. \]

2.4 Step 3: conclude positivity for small positive time

Since \(h'\) is continuous and \(h'(0)>0\), there exists \(\varepsilon>0\) such that \[ h'(t)>\frac{1}{2}h'(0)>0 \qquad \text{for }0\le t\le \varepsilon. \]

Therefore, for every \(t\in (0,\varepsilon]\), \[ \begin{aligned} h(t)-h(0) &=\int_0^t h'(s),ds \ &> \int_0^t \frac{1}{2}h'(0),ds \ &= \frac{t}{2}h'(0) \ &> 0. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(h(0)=0\), this gives \[ h(t)>0 \qquad \text{for }0<t\le \varepsilon. \] That is, \[ \rho(\gamma(t))>0 \qquad \text{for }0<t\le \varepsilon. \]

But \(\rho>0\) means the point lies in the interior of \(M\). Hence \[ \gamma_{x,v}(t)\in \operatorname{Int}(M)\subset M \qquad \text{for all sufficiently small }t>0. \]

Therefore, \[ \boxed{\gamma_{x,v}(t)\text{ stays in }M\text{ for sufficiently small }t>0.} \]

2.5 Remark on strict convexity

For this particular statement, the strict convexity hypothesis is actually stronger than necessary. Since \((x,v)\notin \partial_0SM\), the first derivative \(h'(0)\) already tells us that the geodesic immediately enters the interior.

Strict convexity becomes important in the tangent case \((x,v)\in \partial_0SM\), where one studies the second derivative of \(h(t)\) at \(t=0\) instead of the first.
