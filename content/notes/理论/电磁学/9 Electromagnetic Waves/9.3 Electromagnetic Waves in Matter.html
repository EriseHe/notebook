9.3 Electromagnetic Waves in Matter

9.3.1 Big picture

In vacuum, Maxwell’s equations gave us waves traveling at speed \(c\).
In matter, the fields interact with the medium through polarization and magnetization, so the wave still propagates, but with a modified speed

\[ v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}}=\frac{c}{n}. \]

The main goals of this section are:

  • derive wave propagation in a linear homogeneous medium,
  • use electromagnetic boundary conditions to determine what happens at an interface,
  • recover the laws of reflection and refraction,
  • derive amplitude and energy relations for reflected and transmitted waves,
  • understand the special role of polarization, including Brewster’s angle.

9.3.1 Propagation in Linear Media

9.3.1.1 Maxwell equations in matter

Inside matter, but in regions with no free charge and no free current, Maxwell’s equations are

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla\cdot \mathbf D &= 0, \\ \nabla\cdot \mathbf B &= 0, \\ \nabla\times \mathbf E &= -\frac{\partial \mathbf B}{\partial t}, \\ \nabla\times \mathbf H &= \frac{\partial \mathbf D}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \]

If the medium is linear,

\[ \mathbf D=\epsilon \mathbf E, \qquad \mathbf H=\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf B, \]

and homogeneous so that \(\epsilon\) and \(\mu\) are constants, then these become

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla\cdot \mathbf E &= 0, \\ \nabla\cdot \mathbf B &= 0, \\ \nabla\times \mathbf E &= -\frac{\partial \mathbf B}{\partial t}, \\ \nabla\times \mathbf B &= \mu\epsilon \frac{\partial \mathbf E}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \]

These are exactly the vacuum equations with the replacement

\[ \mu_0\epsilon_0 \longrightarrow \mu\epsilon. \]

So all the vacuum-wave machinery carries over with this substitution.

9.3.1.2 Wave speed and index of refraction

From the same curl-of-curl derivation used in vacuum,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^2 \mathbf E}{\partial t^2}, \qquad \nabla^2\mathbf B=\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^2 \mathbf B}{\partial t^2}} \]

so the wave speed is

\[ \boxed{ v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}}=\frac{c}{n}} \]

with index of refraction

\[ \boxed{ n\equiv \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\mu}{\epsilon_0\mu_0}} }. \]

For most media, \(\mu\approx \mu_0\), so

\[ \boxed{n\approx \sqrt{\epsilon_r}}. \]

That is why light usually travels more slowly in matter than in vacuum.

9.3.1.3 Plane-wave relations in matter

All the plane-wave structure from vacuum survives, with \(c\to v\), \(\epsilon_0\to\epsilon\), \(\mu_0\to\mu\).

For a monochromatic plane wave:

  • \(\omega=kv\),
  • the amplitude of \(\mathbf B\) is \((1/v)\) times the amplitude of \(\mathbf E\),
  • \(\mathbf E\), \(\mathbf B\), and \(\hat{\mathbf k}\) are mutually perpendicular.

So for amplitudes,

\[ \boxed{B_0=\frac{E_0}{v}}. \]

9.3.1.4 Energy density, Poynting vector, and intensity

The energy density is

\[ \boxed{ u=\frac12\left(\epsilon E^2+\frac{1}{\mu}B^2\right)} \]

and the Poynting vector is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf S=\frac{1}{\mu}(\mathbf E\times \mathbf B)}. \]

For a monochromatic plane wave, the intensity is

\[ \boxed{ I=\frac12\,\epsilon v E_0^2 }. \]

So the practical transcription from vacuum is very simple:

\[ \epsilon_0 \to \epsilon,\qquad \mu_0 \to \mu,\qquad c \to v. \]

9.3.1.5 Key conceptual point

The mathematics looks simple, but physically this is subtle: the wave polarizes and magnetizes the medium, and the induced microscopic dipoles generate fields that combine with the original wave into one new wave with the same frequency but a different speed.

9.3.2 Reflection and Transmission at Normal Incidence

9.3.2.1 Setup

Let the interface be the \(xy\)-plane.
A wave in medium 1 travels in the \(+z\) direction toward medium 2, polarized in the \(x\) direction.

The incident wave is

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\mathbf E}_I(z,t) &= \tilde E_{0I} e^{i(k_1 z-\omega t)}\hat{\mathbf x},\\ \tilde{\mathbf B}_I(z,t) &= \frac{1}{v_1}\tilde E_{0I} e^{i(k_1 z-\omega t)}\hat{\mathbf y}. \end{aligned} \]

The reflected wave is

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\mathbf E}_R(z,t) &= \tilde E_{0R} e^{i(-k_1 z-\omega t)}\hat{\mathbf x},\\ \tilde{\mathbf B}_R(z,t) &= -\frac{1}{v_1}\tilde E_{0R} e^{i(-k_1 z-\omega t)}\hat{\mathbf y}. \end{aligned} \]

The minus sign in \(\tilde{\mathbf B}_R\) is essential: it makes \(\mathbf E\times \mathbf B\) point in the reflected propagation direction.

The transmitted wave is

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\mathbf E}_T(z,t) &= \tilde E_{0T} e^{i(k_2 z-\omega t)}\hat{\mathbf x},\\ \tilde{\mathbf B}_T(z,t) &= \frac{1}{v_2}\tilde E_{0T} e^{i(k_2 z-\omega t)}\hat{\mathbf y}. \end{aligned} \]

9.3.2.2 Boundary conditions

At the interface \(z=0\), the fields on the left must match those on the right.

For linear media, the boundary conditions are

\[ \begin{aligned} \epsilon_1 E_{1}^{\perp} &= \epsilon_2 E_{2}^{\perp}, \\ B_{1}^{\perp} &= B_{2}^{\perp}, \\ E_{1}^{\parallel} &= E_{2}^{\parallel}, \\ \frac{1}{\mu_1}B_{1}^{\parallel} &= \frac{1}{\mu_2}B_{2}^{\parallel}. \end{aligned} \]

In the present geometry there are no perpendicular components, so only the tangential conditions matter.

From continuity of tangential \(\mathbf E\),

\[ \boxed{\tilde E_{0I}+\tilde E_{0R}=\tilde E_{0T}} \]

and from continuity of tangential \(\mathbf H=\mathbf B/\mu\),

\[ \frac{1}{\mu_1}\left(\frac{1}{v_1}\tilde E_{0I}-\frac{1}{v_1}\tilde E_{0R}\right) = \frac{1}{\mu_2}\left(\frac{1}{v_2}\tilde E_{0T}\right). \]

Define

\[ \boxed{ \beta\equiv \frac{\mu_1 v_1}{\mu_2 v_2} =\frac{\mu_1 n_2}{\mu_2 n_1}} \]

so the second condition becomes

\[ \boxed{\tilde E_{0I}-\tilde E_{0R}=\beta \tilde E_{0T}}. \]

9.3.2.3 Solving for amplitudes

Now solve the two equations

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde E_{0I}+\tilde E_{0R} &= \tilde E_{0T},\\ \tilde E_{0I}-\tilde E_{0R} &= \beta \tilde E_{0T}. \end{aligned} \]

A clean textbook-style elimination is

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde E_{0I}+\tilde E_{0R} &= \tilde E_{0T},\\ \tilde E_{0I}-\tilde E_{0R} &= \beta \tilde E_{0T} \end{aligned} \]

Add and subtract appropriately:

\[ \begin{aligned} 2\tilde E_{0I} &=(1+\beta)\tilde E_{0T} \\ \Rightarrow\quad \tilde E_{0T} &=\frac{2}{1+\beta}\tilde E_{0I}, \end{aligned} \]

and then

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde E_{0R} &=\tilde E_{0T}-\tilde E_{0I} \\ &=\left(\frac{2}{1+\beta}-1\right)\tilde E_{0I} \\ &=\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}\tilde E_{0I}. \end{aligned} \]

So

\[ \boxed{ \tilde E_{0R}=\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}\tilde E_{0I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0T}=\frac{2}{1+\beta}\tilde E_{0I}} \]

These are the normal-incidence amplitude relations.

9.3.2.4 Common approximation: \(\mu_1\approx \mu_2\)

For most optical media, \(\mu_1\approx\mu_2\approx \mu_0\), so

\[ \beta=\frac{v_1}{v_2}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \tilde E_{0R}=\frac{v_2-v_1}{v_2+v_1}\tilde E_{0I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0T}=\frac{2v_2}{v_2+v_1}\tilde E_{0I}} \]

or, using \(v=c/n\),

\[ \boxed{ E_{0R}=\left|\frac{n_1-n_2}{n_1+n_2}\right|E_{0I}, \qquad E_{0T}=\frac{2n_1}{n_1+n_2}E_{0I}} \]

with the sign of the reflected amplitude encoding phase reversal:

  • if \(v_2>v_1\) then reflected wave is in phase,
  • if \(v_2<v_1\) then reflected wave is \(180^\circ\) out of phase.

9.3.2.5 Reflection and transmission coefficients

Since

\[ I=\frac12 \epsilon v E_0^2, \]

the reflection coefficient is

\[ \boxed{ R\equiv \frac{I_R}{I_I} = \left(\frac{E_{0R}}{E_{0I}}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{n_1-n_2}{n_1+n_2}\right)^2} \]

and the transmission coefficient is

\[ \boxed{ T\equiv \frac{I_T}{I_I} = \frac{\epsilon_2 v_2}{\epsilon_1 v_1} \left(\frac{E_{0T}}{E_{0I}}\right)^2 = \frac{4n_1n_2}{(n_1+n_2)^2}} \]

when \(\mu_1=\mu_2=\mu_0\).

These satisfy

\[ \boxed{R+T=1}, \]

as they must.

9.3.2.6 Example / interpretation

For air to glass,

\[ n_1=1,\qquad n_2=1.5, \]

so

\[ R=0.04,\qquad T=0.96. \]

Only about \(4\%\) is reflected, and most is transmitted.

9.3.2.7 Solution technique to remember

For normal incidence:

  1. write incident, reflected, transmitted waves with correct propagation directions,
  2. be careful with the sign of \(\mathbf B_R\),
  3. apply tangential boundary conditions at the interface,
  4. solve the resulting two linear equations for \(\tilde E_{0R}\) and \(\tilde E_{0T}\),
  5. convert to intensities using \(I=\frac12 \epsilon v E_0^2\).

That is the standard template.

9.3.3 Reflection and Transmission at Oblique Incidence

9.3.3.1 General setup

Now let the incident wave strike the interface at angle \(\theta_I\).

Write

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\mathbf E}_I(\mathbf r,t)&=\tilde{\mathbf E}_{0I}e^{i(\mathbf k_I\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)},\\ \tilde{\mathbf B}_I(\mathbf r,t)&=\frac{1}{v_1}(\hat{\mathbf k}_I\times \tilde{\mathbf E}_I), \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\mathbf E}_R(\mathbf r,t)&=\tilde{\mathbf E}_{0R}e^{i(\mathbf k_R\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)},\\ \tilde{\mathbf B}_R(\mathbf r,t)&=\frac{1}{v_1}(\hat{\mathbf k}_R\times \tilde{\mathbf E}_R), \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\mathbf E}_T(\mathbf r,t)&=\tilde{\mathbf E}_{0T}e^{i(\mathbf k_T\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)},\\ \tilde{\mathbf B}_T(\mathbf r,t)&=\frac{1}{v_2}(\hat{\mathbf k}_T\times \tilde{\mathbf E}_T). \end{aligned} \]

All three waves have the same frequency \(\omega\), while the wave numbers satisfy

\[ \boxed{ k_I v_1=k_R v_1=k_T v_2=\omega} \]

so

\[ \boxed{ k_I=k_R=\frac{v_2}{v_1}k_T=\frac{n_1}{n_2}k_T}. \]

9.3.3.2 Matching the phase along the boundary

At \(z=0\), the boundary conditions have the generic form

\[ (\cdots)e^{i(\mathbf k_I\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)} + (\cdots)e^{i(\mathbf k_R\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)} = (\cdots)e^{i(\mathbf k_T\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)}. \]

Because this must hold for all points on the interface and for all time, the exponential factors must match when \(z=0\):

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf k_I\cdot\mathbf r = \mathbf k_R\cdot\mathbf r = \mathbf k_T\cdot\mathbf r \qquad (z=0)} \]

which implies equality of the components parallel to the interface.

9.3.3.3 The three laws of geometrical optics

Choose axes so that the incident wave lies in the \(xz\) plane. Then the reflected and transmitted wave vectors must also lie in that plane.

So:

9.3.3.3.1 First law

The incident, reflected, and transmitted wave vectors all lie in a single plane, the plane of incidence, which also contains the normal to the surface.

From matching tangential components,

\[ k_I\sin\theta_I=k_R\sin\theta_R=k_T\sin\theta_T. \]

Using \(k_I=k_R\), we get

9.3.3.3.2 Second law: reflection

\[ \boxed{\theta_I=\theta_R} \]

and using \(k_I/k_T=n_1/n_2\), we get

9.3.3.3.3 Third law: refraction (Snell’s law)

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\sin\theta_T}{\sin\theta_I}=\frac{n_1}{n_2}} \]

or equivalently

\[ \boxed{n_1\sin\theta_I=n_2\sin\theta_T}. \]

A key point is that these laws follow mostly from phase matching, before using the detailed electromagnetic boundary conditions.

9.3.3.4 Boundary conditions for amplitudes

After the exponentials cancel, the boundary conditions become relations among amplitudes:

\[ \begin{aligned} \epsilon_1(\tilde E_{0I}+\tilde E_{0R})_z &= \epsilon_2(\tilde E_{0T})_z,\\ (\tilde B_{0I}+\tilde B_{0R})_z &= (\tilde B_{0T})_z,\\ (\tilde E_{0I}+\tilde E_{0R})_{x,y} &= (\tilde E_{0T})_{x,y},\\ \frac{1}{\mu_1}(\tilde B_{0I}+\tilde B_{0R})_{x,y} &= \frac{1}{\mu_2}(\tilde B_{0T})_{x,y}. \end{aligned} \]

At this point polarization matters.

9.3.3.5 Case 1: polarization parallel to the plane of incidence

Take the incident electric field in the plane of incidence (\(xz\)-plane). Then the reflected and transmitted fields are also in that plane.

The boundary conditions reduce to

\[ \epsilon_1(-\tilde E_{0I}\sin\theta_I+\tilde E_{0R}\sin\theta_R) = \epsilon_2(-\tilde E_{0T}\sin\theta_T), \]

\[ \tilde E_{0I}\cos\theta_I+\tilde E_{0R}\cos\theta_R = \tilde E_{0T}\cos\theta_T, \]

\[ \frac{1}{\mu_1 v_1}(\tilde E_{0I}-\tilde E_{0R}) = \frac{1}{\mu_2 v_2}\tilde E_{0T}. \]

Using the laws of reflection and refraction, the first and third both reduce to

\[ \boxed{\tilde E_{0I}-\tilde E_{0R}=\beta \tilde E_{0T}} \]

with

\[ \boxed{\beta=\frac{\mu_1 v_1}{\mu_2 v_2} =\frac{\mu_1 n_2}{\mu_2 n_1}} \]

while the second becomes

\[ \boxed{\tilde E_{0I}+\tilde E_{0R}=\alpha \tilde E_{0T}} \]

where

\[ \boxed{\alpha\equiv \frac{\cos\theta_T}{\cos\theta_I}}. \]

Now solve exactly as in the normal-incidence case:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde E_{0I}-\tilde E_{0R}&=\beta \tilde E_{0T},\\ \tilde E_{0I}+\tilde E_{0R}&=\alpha \tilde E_{0T}. \end{aligned} \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{ \tilde E_{0R}=\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\tilde E_{0I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0T}=\frac{2}{\alpha+\beta}\tilde E_{0I}} \]

These are the Fresnel equations for polarization parallel to the plane of incidence.

9.3.3.6 Angular dependence and Brewster angle

Here

\[ \alpha=\frac{\cos\theta_T}{\cos\theta_I} = \frac{\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta_T}}{\cos\theta_I} = \frac{\sqrt{1-\left[(n_1/n_2)\sin\theta_I\right]^2}}{\cos\theta_I}. \]

So the reflected and transmitted amplitudes depend on the incident angle.

A remarkable feature is the existence of Brewster’s angle \(\theta_B\), where the reflected wave disappears:

\[ \tilde E_{0R}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha=\beta. \]

This yields

\[ \boxed{ \sin^2\theta_B=\frac{1-\beta^2}{(n_1/n_2)^2-\beta^2}} \]

and for the common case \(\mu_1\approx \mu_2\),

\[ \beta\approx \frac{n_2}{n_1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\tan\theta_B\approx \frac{n_2}{n_1}}. \]

At this angle, the reflected beam is extinguished for this polarization.

9.3.3.7 Intensities for the parallel-polarization case

The power per unit area of interface is the normal component of the Poynting vector, so

\[ \boxed{ I_I=\frac12 \epsilon_1 v_1 E_{0I}^2 \cos\theta_I} \]

\[ \boxed{ I_R=\frac12 \epsilon_1 v_1 E_{0R}^2 \cos\theta_R, \qquad I_T=\frac12 \epsilon_2 v_2 E_{0T}^2 \cos\theta_T} \]

The extra cosine factors appear because the wave fronts are tilted relative to the interface.

So the reflection and transmission coefficients are

\[ \boxed{ R=\frac{I_R}{I_I} = \left(\frac{E_{0R}}{E_{0I}}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\right)^2} \]

and

\[ \boxed{ T=\frac{I_T}{I_I} = \frac{\epsilon_2 v_2}{\epsilon_1 v_1} \left(\frac{E_{0T}}{E_{0I}}\right)^2 \frac{\cos\theta_T}{\cos\theta_I} = \alpha\beta\left(\frac{2}{\alpha+\beta}\right)^2} \]

with

\[ \boxed{R+T=1}. \]

At Brewster’s angle,

\[ R=0,\qquad T=1. \]

9.3.3.8 Case 2: polarization perpendicular to the plane of incidence

Griffiths leaves the full derivation to the problem set. The important conceptual takeaway is:

  • there is another pair of Fresnel equations for this polarization,
  • unlike the parallel-polarization case, there is no Brewster angle in the generic case,
  • the reflected component is not extinguished.

So polarization is not a minor detail; it changes the boundary-value problem.

9.3.3.9 Solution technique to remember for oblique incidence

The standard Griffiths strategy is:

  1. write incident, reflected, and transmitted plane waves in complex notation,
  2. impose equality of phase along the interface,
  3. deduce:
    • coplanarity of \(\mathbf k_I,\mathbf k_R,\mathbf k_T\),
    • \(\theta_I=\theta_R\),
    • Snell’s law,
  4. then impose amplitude boundary conditions,
  5. solve separately for each polarization.

This is the cleanest way to organize the algebra.

9.3.4 Summary sheet

9.3.4.1 Wave propagation in matter

\[ \boxed{ \nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}, \qquad \nabla^2\mathbf B=\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf B}{\partial t^2}} \]

\[ \boxed{ v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}}=\frac{c}{n}} \]

\[ \boxed{ n=\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\mu}{\epsilon_0\mu_0}} \approx \sqrt{\epsilon_r}} \]

9.3.4.2 Energy flow in matter

\[ \boxed{ u=\frac12\left(\epsilon E^2+\frac{1}{\mu}B^2\right)} \]

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf S=\frac{1}{\mu}(\mathbf E\times \mathbf B)} \]

\[ \boxed{ I=\frac12 \epsilon v E_0^2} \]

9.3.4.3 Boundary conditions at an interface

\[ \boxed{ \epsilon_1 E_1^\perp=\epsilon_2 E_2^\perp,\quad B_1^\perp=B_2^\perp,\quad E_1^\parallel=E_2^\parallel,\quad \frac{1}{\mu_1}B_1^\parallel=\frac{1}{\mu_2}B_2^\parallel} \]

9.3.4.4 Normal incidence

\[ \boxed{ \tilde E_{0R}=\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}\tilde E_{0I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0T}=\frac{2}{1+\beta}\tilde E_{0I}} \]

\[ \boxed{ \beta=\frac{\mu_1 v_1}{\mu_2 v_2}=\frac{\mu_1 n_2}{\mu_2 n_1}} \]

If \(\mu_1\approx\mu_2\),

\[ \boxed{ E_{0R}=\left|\frac{n_1-n_2}{n_1+n_2}\right|E_{0I}, \qquad E_{0T}=\frac{2n_1}{n_1+n_2}E_{0I}} \]

\[ \boxed{ R=\left(\frac{n_1-n_2}{n_1+n_2}\right)^2, \qquad T=\frac{4n_1n_2}{(n_1+n_2)^2}} \]

9.3.4.5 Oblique incidence

\[ \boxed{\theta_I=\theta_R} \]

\[ \boxed{n_1\sin\theta_I=n_2\sin\theta_T} \]

For polarization parallel to the plane of incidence,

\[ \boxed{ \tilde E_{0R}=\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\tilde E_{0I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0T}=\frac{2}{\alpha+\beta}\tilde E_{0I}} \]

with

\[ \boxed{ \alpha=\frac{\cos\theta_T}{\cos\theta_I}, \qquad \beta=\frac{\mu_1 v_1}{\mu_2 v_2}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{ R=\left(\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\right)^2, \qquad T=\alpha\beta\left(\frac{2}{\alpha+\beta}\right)^2} \]

9.3.4.6 Brewster angle

\[ \boxed{ \sin^2\theta_B=\frac{1-\beta^2}{(n_1/n_2)^2-\beta^2}} \]

For \(\mu_1\approx\mu_2\),

\[ \boxed{\tan\theta_B\approx \frac{n_2}{n_1}} \]
