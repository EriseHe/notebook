1.1 Problem 1.33

Test the divergence theorem for the function \(\mathbf{v}=(x y) \hat{\mathbf{x}}+(2 y z) \hat{\mathbf{y}}+(3 z x) \hat{\mathbf{z}}\). Take as your volume the cube shown in Fig. 1.30, with sides of length 2. ### (a) \[\begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot \vec{v} &= \partial_x(xy) + \partial_y(2yz) + \partial_z(3zx)\\ &= y + 2z + 3x \end{aligned}\]

\[\begin{aligned} \int_V (\nabla \cdot \vec{v}) \, d\tau &= \int_{x=0}^{2} \int_{y=0}^{4} \int_{z=0}^{2} (y + 2z + 3x) \, dx \, dy \, dz \\ &= 4 \int_0^{4} y \, dy + 8 \int_0^{2} z \, dz + 12 \int_0^{2} x \, dx \\ &= 8 + 16 + 24 \\ &= 48 \end{aligned}\]

1.1.1 (b) Use labels from Fig. 1.29:

\[d\vec{a} = \hat{x} \, dy \, dz \Big|_{x=2}\] \[\begin{aligned} \Rightarrow \int_{L_i} \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{a} &= \int_0^{2} xy \, dy \, dz \Big|_{x=2} \\ &= 8 \end{aligned}\]

  1. \[d\vec{a} = -\hat{x} \, dy \, dz \Big|_{x=0}\] \[\begin{aligned} \Rightarrow \int_{L_{ii}} \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{a} &= -\int_0^{2} xy \, dy \, dz \Big|_{x=0} \\ &= 0 \end{aligned}\]
  2. \[d\vec{a} = \hat{y} \, dx \, dz \Big|_{y=2}\] \[\begin{aligned} \Rightarrow \int_{L_{iii}} \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{a} &= \int_0^{2} 4y^2 \, dx \, dz \Big|_{y=2} \\ &= 16 \end{aligned}\]
  3. \[d\vec{a} = -\hat{y} \, dx \, dz \Big|_{y=0}\] \[\begin{aligned} \Rightarrow \int_{L_{iv}} \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{a} &= -\int_0^{2} 2y^2 \, dx \, dz \Big|_{y=0} \\ &= 0 \end{aligned}\]
  4. \[d\vec{a} = \hat{z} \, dy \, dx \Big|_{z=2}\] \[\begin{aligned} \Rightarrow \int_V \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{a} &= \int_0^{2} 3zx \, dx \, dy \Big|_{z=2} \\ &= 24 \end{aligned}\]

\[d\vec{a} = -\hat{z} \, dx \, dy \Big|_{z=0}\] \[\begin{aligned} \Rightarrow \int_{V_i} \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{a} &= -\int_0^{2} 3zx \, dx \, dy \Big|_{z=0} \\ &= 0 \end{aligned}\]

\[\begin{aligned} \Rightarrow \oint \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{a} &= \sum_{L=i}^{6} \int_L \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{a} \\ &= 48 \end{aligned}\]

    • (b): \[\int_V (\vec{\nabla} \cdot \vec{v}) \, d\tau = \oint_{\partial V} \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{a} = 48 \checkmark\]

1.3 Problem 1.62

P1.62 The integral \[ \mathbf{a} \equiv \int_{\mathcal{S}} d \mathbf{a} \] is sometimes called the vector area of the surface \(\mathcal{S}\). If \(\mathcal{S}\) happens to be flat, then \(|\mathbf{a}|\) is the ordinary (scalar) area, obviously.

1.3.1 Part A

Find the vector area of a hemispherical bowl of radius \(R\).

1.3.2 Part B

Show that \(\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}\) for any closed surface. [Hint: Use Prob. 1.61a.]

1.3.3 Part C

Show that a is the same for all surfaces sharing the same boundary.

1.3.4 Part D

Show that

\[ \mathbf{a}=\frac{1}{2} \oint \mathbf{r} \times d \mathbf{l}, \]

where the integral is around the boundary line. [Hint: One way to do it is to draw the cone subtended by the loop at the origin. Divide the conical surface up into infinitesimal triangular wedges, each with vertex at the origin and opposite side \(d l\), and exploit the geometrical interpretation of the cross product (Fig. 1.8).]

1.3.5 Part E

Show that

\[ \oint(\mathbf{c} \cdot \mathbf{r}) d \mathbf{l}=\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{c} \] for any constant vector c.[Hint: Let \(T=\mathrm{c} \cdot \mathrm{r}\) in Prob. 1.61e.]

1.4 Problem 1.64

P1.64 In case you’re not persuaded that \(\nabla^2(1 / r)=-4 \pi \delta^3(\mathbf{r})\) (Eq. 1.102, with \(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}\) for simplicity), try replacing \(r\) by \(\sqrt{r^2+\epsilon^2}\), and watching what happens as \(\epsilon \rightarrow 0\). Specifically, let \[ D(r, \epsilon) \equiv-\frac{1}{4 \pi} \nabla^2 \frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+\epsilon^2}} \]

To demonstrate that this goes to \(\delta^3(\mathbf{r})\) as \(\epsilon \rightarrow 0:\)

1.4.1 Part A

Show that \(D(r, \epsilon)=\left(3 \epsilon^2 / 4 \pi\right)\left(r^2+\epsilon^2\right)^{-5 / 2}\).

1.4.2 Part B

check that \(D(0, \epsilon) \rightarrow \infty\) as \(\epsilon \rightarrow 0\).

1.4.3 Part C

check that \(D(r, \epsilon) \rightarrow 0\), as \(\epsilon \rightarrow 0\), for all \(r \neq 0\).

1.6 Problem 2.35

Find the energy stored in a uniformly charged solid sphere of radius \(R\) and charge \(q\). Do it three different ways:

1.6.1 (a) Use Eq. 2.43. You found the potential in Prob. 2.22.

Solution. using \[\boxed{W = \frac{1}{2} \int \rho V d\tau}\] To get \(V\), use Gauss’ Law to get \(\vec{E}\) first:

outside: \[ \begin{aligned} E_{out} \cdot 4\pi r_{out}^2 &= \frac{Q_{en}}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0} \\ \Rightarrow \vec{E}_{out}(\vec{r}) &= \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2} \hat{r} \end{aligned} \]

inside: \[ \begin{aligned} E_{in} \cdot 4\pi r_{in}^2 &= \frac{Q_{en}}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0} \frac{\frac{4}{3}\pi r_{in}^3}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3} \\ \Rightarrow \vec{E}_{in} (\vec{r}) &= \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{\vec{r}}{R^3} \end{aligned} \]

Then, \(V(\vec{r})= -\int_{\infty}^{\vec{r}} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}\), only needed for \(r < R\): \[ \begin{aligned} \Rightarrow V(\vec{r}) &= - \int_{\infty}^{R} E_{out} \cdot dr - \int_{R}^{r} E_{in} \cdot dr \\ &= - \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \left\{ \int_{\infty}^{R} \frac{dr}{r^2} + \frac{1}{R^3} \int_{R}^{r} r dr \right\} \\ &= - \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \left\{ -\frac{1}{R} + \frac{1}{2R^3} (r^2 - R^2) \right\} \\ &= \frac{3}{8\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{R} \left(1 - \frac{r^2}{3R^2}\right) \end{aligned} \]

Finally, \[ \begin{aligned} W &= \frac{1}{2} \int_{sph} \rho V d\tau \\ &= \frac{q}{\frac{8}{3}\pi R^3} \cdot \frac{3}{8\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{R} 4\pi \int_0^R \left(1 - \frac{r^2}{3R^2}\right) r^2 dr \\ &\quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \underbrace{\quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad}_{\frac{1}{3}R^3 - \frac{1}{15}R^3 = \frac{4R^3}{15}} \\ \Rightarrow W &= \frac{3}{20\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q^2}{R} // \end{aligned} \] ### (b) Use Eq. 2.45. Don’t forget to integrate over all space. ### (c) Use Eq. 2.44. Take a spherical volume of radius \(a\). What happens as \(a \rightarrow \infty\) ?
