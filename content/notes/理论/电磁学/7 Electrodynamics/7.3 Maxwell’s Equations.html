7.3 Maxwell’s Equations

2026-04-09

Chapter 7.3 is the point where electrodynamics becomes a complete field theory. Before Maxwell, we had Gauss’s law, \(\nabla \cdot B = 0\), Faraday’s law, and the old Ampère law. The problem was that the old Ampère law fails for time-dependent situations, especially when charge accumulates, such as in a charging capacitor. Maxwell fixed this by adding the displacement current term. The result is the full set of Maxwell’s equations.

7.3.1 Electrodynamics before Maxwell

The pre-Maxwell equations were:

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot E &= \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0} &&\text{(Gauss's law)},\\ \nabla \cdot B &= 0,\\ \nabla \times E &= -\frac{\partial B}{\partial t} &&\text{(Faraday's law)},\\ \nabla \times B &= \mu_0 J &&\text{(old Ampère law)}. \end{aligned} \]

7.3.1.1 Why this is inconsistent

Take the divergence of the curl equations. For Faraday: \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot (\nabla \times E) &= \nabla \cdot \left(-\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}\right)\\ 0 &= -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla \cdot B), \end{aligned} \]

which is fine because \(\nabla \cdot B = 0\). But for old Ampère: \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot (\nabla \times B) &= \mu_0 \nabla \cdot J,\\ 0 &= \mu_0 \nabla \cdot J. \end{aligned} \] So this would force \[ \nabla \cdot J = 0, \] which is only true for steady currents. Hence the old Ampère law cannot be correct in general time-dependent electrodynamics.

7.3.1.2 Capacitor paradox

For a charging capacitor, if we apply Ampère’s law in integral form, \[ \oint B \cdot dl = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}, \] then the answer depends on which surface spans the same loop:

  • a flat surface cutting the wire gives \(I_{\text{enc}} = I\),
  • a bulging surface between capacitor plates gives \(I_{\text{enc}} = 0\).

So the old Ampère law makes \(I_{\text{enc}}\) surface-dependent, which is unacceptable.

7.3.2 How Maxwell fixed Ampère’s law

Use charge conservation: \[ \nabla \cdot J = -\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}. \] Then use Gauss’s law: \[ \rho = \epsilon_0 \nabla \cdot E. \] So \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot J &= -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\epsilon_0 \nabla \cdot E)\\ &= -\nabla \cdot \left(\epsilon_0 \frac{\partial E}{\partial t}\right). \end{aligned} \] This suggests correcting Ampère’s law by replacing \(J\) with \[ J + \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial E}{\partial t} \] Therefore the corrected equation is \[ \nabla \times B = \mu_0 J + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial E}{\partial t}. \] This extra term is called the displacement current density: \[ J_d \equiv \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial E}{\partial t}. \] #### Integral form

The corrected Ampère-Maxwell law is \[ \oint B \cdot dl = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \int \frac{\partial E}{\partial t} \cdot da. \] This resolves the charging capacitor paradox:

  • on the wire-cutting surface, the conduction current contributes;
  • on the balloon surface between the plates, the displacement current contributes.

Both surfaces give the same magnetic field.

7.3.2.1 Key idea

Faraday says: \[ \text{changing } B \Rightarrow \text{induces } E. \]

Maxwell’s correction says: \[ \text{changing } E \Rightarrow \text{induces } B. \]

This symmetry is one of the deep structural ideas of electrodynamics.

Remark

In some time-dependent situations, a nonzero conduction current can be exactly canceled by displacement current, so the magnetic field can still be zero by symmetry.

7.3.3 Maxwell’s equations

The finished Maxwell equations are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot E &= \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}, \\ \nabla \cdot B &= 0, \\ \nabla \times E &= -\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}, \\ \nabla \times B &= \mu_0 J + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial E}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \tag{Maxwell} \]

Together with the Lorentz force law, \[ F = q(E + v \times B), \] they summarize classical electrodynamics.

7.3.3.1 Continuity equation from Maxwell

Applying \(\nabla \cdot\) to Ampère-Maxwell gives \[ \begin{aligned} 0 &= \mu_0 \nabla \cdot J + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla \cdot E)\\ &= \mu_0 \nabla \cdot J + \mu_0 \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}, \end{aligned} \] hence \[ \nabla \cdot J = -\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}. \]

So charge conservation is built into Maxwell’s equations.

7.3.3.2 Alternative source-emphasizing form

It is often conceptually cleaner to write all field terms on the left and all source terms on the right:

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot E &= \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}, \\ \nabla \cdot B &= 0, \\ \nabla \times E + \frac{\partial B}{\partial t} &= 0, \\ \nabla \times B - \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial E}{\partial t} &= \mu_0 J. \end{aligned} \]

This emphasizes that the ultimate sources are \(\rho\) and \(J\).

7.3.4 Magnetic charge

In vacuum, where \(\rho = 0\) and \(J = 0\), Maxwell’s equations look very symmetric:

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot E &= 0, & \nabla \times E &= -\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}, \\ \nabla \cdot B &= 0, & \nabla \times B &= \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial E}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \]

This suggests asking: what if magnetic charge existed? Then one would write \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot E &= \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\rho_e, \\ \nabla \cdot B &= \mu_0 \rho_m, \\ \nabla \times E &= -\mu_0 J_m - \frac{\partial B}{\partial t}, \\ \nabla \times B &= \mu_0 J_e + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial E}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \] Then both electric and magnetic charges would satisfy continuity equations: \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot J_e &= -\frac{\partial \rho_e}{\partial t}, \\ \nabla \cdot J_m &= -\frac{\partial \rho_m}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \]

RemarkPhysical conclusion

This would be mathematically elegant, but experimentally no magnetic monopoles have been found. So, as far as classical electrodynamics goes, \[ \rho_m = 0, \qquad J_m = 0. \] That is why electric fields have monopole sources, but magnetic fields do not.

7.3.5 Maxwell’s equations in matter

Inside matter, it is useful to separate free charge/current from bound charge/current.

7.3.4.1.1 Bound sources

Electric polarization \(P\) produces bound charge density \[ \rho_b = -\nabla \cdot P. \]

Magnetization \(M\) produces bound current density \[ J_b = \nabla \times M. \]

If the polarization changes with time, there is also a polarization current \[ J_p = \frac{\partial P}{\partial t}. \]

This is important: \(J_p\) is not the same thing as \(J_b\).

  • \(J_b\) comes from magnetization,
  • \(J_p\) comes from changing electric polarization.

7.3.4.1.2 Total charge and current

Thus, \[ \rho = \rho_f + \rho_b = \rho_f - \nabla \cdot P, \] and \[ J = J_f + J_b + J_p = J_f + \nabla \times M + \frac{\partial P}{\partial t}. \]

7.3.4.1.3 Define \(D\) and \(H\)

Introduce the auxiliary fields \[ D \equiv \epsilon_0 E + P, \] and \[ H \equiv \frac{1}{\mu_0}B - M. \]

Then Maxwell’s equations become

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot D &= \rho_f, \\ \nabla \cdot B &= 0, \\ \nabla \times E &= -\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}, \\ \nabla \times H &= J_f + \frac{\partial D}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \]

These are not more fundamental than the vacuum-style equations; they are just more convenient in matter because they isolate the free sources.

7.3.4.1.4 Linear media

For linear materials, \[ P = \epsilon_0 \chi_e E, \qquad M = \chi_m H. \]

Therefore, \[ D = \epsilon E, \qquad H = \frac{1}{\mu} B, \] where \[ \epsilon = \epsilon_0(1+\chi_e), \qquad \mu = \mu_0(1+\chi_m). \]

In this context, one also writes the displacement current as \[ J_d \equiv \frac{\partial D}{\partial t}. \]

7.3.6 Boundary conditions

At an interface between two media, or across a surface carrying free surface charge density \(\sigma_f\) or free surface current density \(K_f\), the fields may jump.

Notation:

  • \(\perp\) means component normal to the boundary,
  • \(\parallel\) means component tangent to the boundary,
  • “above” and “below” refer to the two sides of the interface,
  • \(\hat n\) is the unit normal pointing from below to above.

7.3.6.1 General boundary conditions

From Maxwell’s equations in matter: $$ \[\begin{align} D_{\perp}^{\text{above}} - D_{\perp}^{\text{below}} & = \sigma_f \\ B_{\perp}^{\text{above}} - B_{\perp}^{\text{below}} & = 0\\ E_{\parallel}^{\text{above}} - E_{\parallel}^{\text{below}} & = 0 \\ H_{\parallel}^{\text{above}} - H_{\parallel}^{\text{below}} & = K_f \times \hat n. \end{align}\] $$ These are the basic jump conditions.

7.3.6.2 In linear media

Using \(D=\epsilon E\) and \(H=B/\mu\), these become

\[ \begin{align} \epsilon_{\text{above}} E_{\perp}^{\text{above}} - \epsilon_{\text{below}} E_{\perp}^{\text{below}} & = \sigma_f, \\ B_{\perp}^{\text{above}} - B_{\perp}^{\text{below}} & = 0, \\ E_{\parallel}^{\text{above}} - E_{\parallel}^{\text{below}} & = 0, \\ \frac{1}{\mu_{\text{above}}}B_{\parallel}^{\text{above}} - \frac{1}{\mu_{\text{below}}}B_{\parallel}^{\text{below}} & = K_f \times \hat n. \end{align} \]

7.3.6.2.1 If there is no free surface charge or current

If \(\sigma_f = 0\) and \(K_f = 0\), then

\[ \epsilon_{\text{above}} E_{\perp}^{\text{above}} = \epsilon_{\text{below}} E_{\perp}^{\text{below}}, \]

\[ B_{\perp}^{\text{above}} = B_{\perp}^{\text{below}}, \]

\[ E_{\parallel}^{\text{above}} = E_{\parallel}^{\text{below}}, \]

\[ \frac{1}{\mu_{\text{above}}}B_{\parallel}^{\text{above}} = \frac{1}{\mu_{\text{below}}}B_{\parallel}^{\text{below}}. \]

These are the boundary conditions used later for reflection and refraction.

7.3.7 What to remember most

1. The old Ampère law fails in time-dependent situations

The inconsistency is \[ \nabla \cdot (\nabla \times B) = 0 \quad \text{but} \quad \nabla \cdot J \neq 0 \] in general.

2. Maxwell’s correction is

\[ \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial E}{\partial t}. \]

3. The full Maxwell system is

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot E &= \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}, \\ \nabla \cdot B &= 0, \\ \nabla \times E &= -\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}, \\ \nabla \times B &= \mu_0 J + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial E}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \]

4. In matter, use

\[ D = \epsilon_0 E + P, \qquad H = \frac{1}{\mu_0}B - M. \]

5. In matter, Maxwell becomes

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot D &= \rho_f, \\ \nabla \cdot B &= 0, \\ \nabla \times E &= -\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}, \\ \nabla \times H &= J_f + \frac{\partial D}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \]

6. Boundary conditions are indispensable

They tell you exactly which components jump and by how much at interfaces.

7.3.8 One-line intuition

Electrostatics and magnetostatics are not separate worlds anymore. In full electrodynamics,

\[ \text{changing } B \leftrightarrow \text{induces } E, \qquad \text{changing } E \leftrightarrow \text{induces } B, \]

and Maxwell’s equations are the compact mathematical statement of that mutual coupling.
