Let $A\in\mathbb K^{m\times n}$ with $m\ge n$, where $\mathbb K=\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$. The symbol $*$ denotes conjugate transpose. Givens rotation formulas below are stated for real data, as in the source.

## 7.1 Review of Householder Triangularization

*Source: pp. 1-2; the algorithm on p. 2 repeats the last page of Lecture 6.*

The previous lecture contrasted two constructions:

- Gram-Schmidt multiplies on the right by upper triangular matrices to obtain orthonormal columns.
- Householder QR multiplies on the left by orthogonal transformations to obtain an upper triangular or upper trapezoidal matrix.

The stability motivation was the bound

$$
\operatorname{fl}(XA)=X(A+F),
\qquad
\frac{\|F\|_2}{\|A\|_2}\lesssim u\,\kappa_2(X),
$$

to first order in the unit roundoff $u$, with dimension-dependent constants suppressed. For orthogonal or unitary $X$, $\kappa_2(X)=1$.

At step $k$, a Householder reflector acts on the trailing rows:

$$
H_k=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_{k-1}&0\\
0&F_k
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
F_k=I-2\frac{v_kv_k^*}{v_k^*v_k}.
$$

It transforms the current column segment $x=A[k:m,k]$ to a multiple of its first coordinate vector. For real nonzero $x$, use

$$
v_k=x+\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\|x\|_2e_1,
\qquad \operatorname{sign}(0)=1,
$$

then optionally rescale $v_k$ so its first entry is 1. The sign avoids subtracting nearly equal quantities. A zero $x$ requires no transformation.

The update is

$$
A[k:m,k:n]\leftarrow
A[k:m,k:n]
-v_k\left(\frac{2}{v_k^*v_k}v_k^*A[k:m,k:n]\right).
$$

After all steps,

$$
H_n\cdots H_1A=R_{\mathrm{full}},
\qquad
A=Q_{\mathrm{full}}R_{\mathrm{full}},
\qquad
Q_{\mathrm{full}}=H_1\cdots H_n.
$$

The order matters even though each individual $H_k$ is its own inverse: two different reflectors generally do not commute.

> **Clarification.** The recap's absolute backward-error bound omits $\|A\|_2$; the relative form above restores the scaling. The repeated storage line $A[k+1:m,k]=v_k$ means the tail $v_k[2:\mathrm{end}]$, since the leading entry 1 is implicit.

## 7.2 Using Q without Forming It

*Source: p. 3.*

### 7.2.1 Implicit Storage

The algorithm need not form the $m\times m$ matrix $Q_{\mathrm{full}}$. Store only the vectors that define its reflectors. For one vector segment $z$,

$$
F_kz=z-v_k\left(\frac{2}{v_k^*v_k}v_k^*z\right).
$$

Thus applying a reflector requires an inner product and a scaled vector subtraction. Forming $v_kv_k^*$ as a dense matrix is unnecessary.

With $v_k[1]=1$, the remaining $m-k$ entries fit below the diagonal in column $k$ of the original matrix array. The diagonal and upper triangle store $R_1$. A scalar $\tau_k=2/(v_k^*v_k)$ can also be stored; setting $\tau_k=0$ represents an identity step.

### 7.2.2 Applying the Adjoint

Since each reflector is self-adjoint,

$$
Q_{\mathrm{full}}^*=H_n\cdots H_1.
$$

The rightmost factor acts first, so compute $Q_{\mathrm{full}}^*b$ by applying the reflectors in **forward construction order**:

```text
Input: b in K^m and the stored Householder vectors

for k = 1,...,n:
    v = reconstruct vector k with first entry 1
    b[k:m] = b[k:m] - v * (tau[k] * (v* b[k:m]))

Return b                       # now equal to Q_full* b
```

With no identity steps, $\tau_k=2/(v_k^*v_k)$ can be recomputed from $v_k$.

### 7.2.3 Applying Q

To compute

$$
Q_{\mathrm{full}}z=H_1\cdots H_nz,
$$

the rightmost $H_n$ acts first. Use **reverse construction order**:

```text
Input: z in K^m and the stored Householder vectors

for k = n,n-1,...,1:
    v = reconstruct vector k with first entry 1
    z[k:m] = z[k:m] - v * (tau[k] * (v* z[k:m]))

Return z                       # now equal to Q_full z
```

**Order check.** With just two reflectors,

$$
Q=H_1H_2,
\qquad Q^*=H_2H_1.
$$

To compute $Qz$, first apply $H_2$, then $H_1$. To compute $Q^*b$, first apply $H_1$, then $H_2$.

### 7.2.4 What Implicit Storage Gives Us

The lecture emphasizes three consequences:

1. **In-place storage:** the leading 1 of each reflector vector is implicit, and its tail occupies the space where subdiagonal entries of $R$ would be zero.
2. **A full QR factorization:** the product of reflectors acts on all of $\mathbb K^m$, even though only $n$ reflector vectors are stored.
3. **Orthogonality by construction:** the exact matrix defined by the stored reflector vectors is a product of unitary matrices.

> **Clarification.** “Orthogonal by definition” refers to the exact mathematical product associated with the stored vectors. Applying the reflectors or explicitly forming their product in floating-point arithmetic still introduces rounding error. An explicitly formed floating-point $\widehat Q$ is generally orthogonal to working precision, not entrywise exact in the identity $\widehat Q^*\widehat Q=I$.

## 7.3 Givens Rotations

*Source: pp. 4-5.*

Householder transformations clear an entire column tail in one step. If a matrix is already almost upper triangular, only a few individual entries may need to be removed. A transformation acting on two rows can then be more natural.

### 7.3.1 The Two-Dimensional Construction

Consider a real vector

$$
\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}>0.
$$

Define

$$
c=\frac ar,
\qquad
s=\frac br,
\qquad
G=
\begin{pmatrix}
c&s\\
-s&c
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Since $c^2+s^2=1$,

$$
G^TG=I,
\qquad\det G=1.
$$

Therefore $G$ is an orthogonal rotation. Its action is

$$
\begin{aligned}
G\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}
&=\begin{pmatrix}ca+sb\\-sa+cb\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}(a^2+b^2)/r\\(-ba+ab)/r\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}r\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For the leading $2\times2$ block shown in the lecture, $a=A_{11}$ and $b=A_{21}$. Applying $G$ to both columns rotates the first column onto the horizontal coordinate axis and applies the same rotation to the second column.

> **Clarification.** In this two-dimensional sketch, $\|a_1\|$ means the norm of the two entries being rotated. In a larger matrix, the denominator for a single Givens step is $\sqrt{A_{ik}^2+A_{jk}^2}$, not the norm of the entire column.

If $a=b=0$, choose $c=1$, $s=0$, so $G=I$. For floating-point implementation, compute $r$ with a scaled Euclidean-norm or `hypot` routine rather than naively squaring numbers that might overflow or underflow.

### 7.3.2 Embedding a Rotation in a Larger Matrix

To remove $A_{jk}$ using $A_{ik}$, where $i<j$, embed the $2\times2$ rotation in rows and columns $i,j$ of an $m\times m$ identity matrix. Its nontrivial entries are

$$
G_{ii}=G_{jj}=c,
\qquad
G_{ij}=s,
\qquad
G_{ji}=-s.
$$

Let

$$
a=A_{ik},
\qquad b=A_{jk},
\qquad r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}.
$$

Then left multiplication changes only two rows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{new row }i&=c\,\text{old row }i+s\,\text{old row }j,\\
\text{new row }j&=-s\,\text{old row }i+c\,\text{old row }j.
\end{aligned}
$$

At column $k$, the new entries are $r$ and 0. Both updates must use the old rows; overwriting one row and using it in the second update would implement a different transformation.

### 7.3.3 QR by Repeated Givens Rotations

Choose rotations that remove entries below the diagonal one at a time. A simple dense procedure is:

```text
for k = 1,...,n:
    for j = m,m-1,...,k+1:
        a = A[k,k]
        b = A[j,k]
        if b == 0:
            continue
        r = hypot(a, b)
        c = a/r
        s = b/r
        row_k = copy(A[k,k:n])
        row_j = copy(A[j,k:n])
        A[k,k:n] =  c*row_k + s*row_j
        A[j,k:n] = -s*row_k + c*row_j
        store the row pair (k,j) and the values c,s
```

This pseudocode expands the source's instruction to “repeat until upper triangular.” At column $k$, both rows already have zeros in columns $1:k-1$, so the rotation preserves those earlier zeros.

If the chronological sequence of rotations is $G_1,\ldots,G_p$, then

$$
G_p\cdots G_1A=R_{\mathrm{full}},
$$

and

$$
Q_{\mathrm{full}}
=(G_p\cdots G_1)^T
=G_1^T\cdots G_p^T.
$$

Unlike a Householder reflector, a general Givens rotation is not its own inverse. Its inverse is its transpose, which changes the sign of $s$. Storing the row indices and $c,s$ is enough to apply the transformation later.

### 7.3.4 Householder or Givens

| Transformation | Entries targeted by one step | Main use highlighted in the source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Householder reflector | A whole subdiagonal column segment | Efficient dense triangularization |
| Givens rotation | One selected subdiagonal entry | Matrices with useful sparsity or already near triangular form |

For dense matrices, eliminating one entry at a time is generally less efficient than using Householder column operations. For some sparse or structured matrices, rotations avoid unnecessary work on a long column segment.

> **Clarification.** Givens rotations are not automatically superior for every sparse matrix. Their advantage depends on the nonzero pattern and the fill created by mixing rows. The source makes the qualified statement that they are sometimes more efficient for sparse matrices.

## 7.4 Full and Reduced QR Factorizations

*Source: p. 6.*

### 7.4.1 Reduced QR

> [!definition] Reduced QR factorization
> For $A\in\mathbb K^{m\times n}$ with $m\ge n$, a reduced QR factorization is
> $$
> A=Q_1R_1,
> \qquad
> Q_1\in\mathbb K^{m\times n},\quad Q_1^*Q_1=I_n,
> \qquad
> R_1\in\mathbb K^{n\times n}\text{ upper triangular}.
> $$

Basic Gram-Schmidt directly constructs these $n$ orthonormal columns. If $A$ has full column rank, $R_1$ is nonsingular and

$$
\operatorname{range}(Q_1)=\operatorname{range}(A).
$$

### 7.4.2 Full QR

> [!definition] Full QR factorization
> A full QR factorization is
> $$
> A=Q_{\mathrm{full}}R_{\mathrm{full}},
> \qquad
> Q_{\mathrm{full}}\in\mathbb K^{m\times m},\quad Q_{\mathrm{full}}^*Q_{\mathrm{full}}=I_m,
> \qquad
> R_{\mathrm{full}}=
> \begin{pmatrix}R_1\\0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb K^{m\times n}.
> $$

Partition the full orthogonal factor as

$$
Q_{\mathrm{full}}=\begin{pmatrix}Q_1&Q_2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
Q_2\in\mathbb K^{m\times(m-n)}.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{pmatrix}Q_1&Q_2\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}R_1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=Q_1R_1=A.
$$

The reduced factorization discards the complementary columns $Q_2$ and the zero block of $R_{\mathrm{full}}$.

Householder and Givens algorithms naturally represent a full orthogonal transformation as a product, but they need not explicitly build all of its columns. Reduced QR can be extracted from either method. Conversely, a reduced $Q_1$ can be extended to a full orthonormal basis.

When $m=n$, full and reduced QR have the same dimensions.

### 7.4.3 Connection Back to Least Squares

**Expanded consequence of the full/reduced distinction.** For full column rank, write

$$
Q_{\mathrm{full}}^*b=
\begin{pmatrix}c\\d\end{pmatrix},
\qquad c\in\mathbb K^n,
\qquad d\in\mathbb K^{m-n}.
$$

Unitary invariance of the Euclidean norm gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|Ax-b\|_2^2
&=\|Q_{\mathrm{full}}^*(Ax-b)\|_2^2\\
&=\left\|
\begin{pmatrix}R_1x-c\\-d\end{pmatrix}
\right\|_2^2\\
&=\|R_1x-c\|_2^2+\|d\|_2^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the minimizer solves $R_1x=c$, and the minimum residual norm is $\|d\|_2$. One can compute both by applying the stored reflectors to $b$, without explicitly forming $Q_{\mathrm{full}}$.

For full column rank, $Q_2$ spans $\operatorname{range}(A)^\perp$, so $d=Q_2^*b$ contains the residual coordinates. This is the geometric least-squares interpretation from the earlier lectures, expressed in full orthonormal coordinates.

## 7.5 Existence of QR

*Source: p. 7.*

> [!theorem] Existence of full and reduced QR
> Every matrix $A\in\mathbb K^{m\times n}$ with $m\ge n$ has both a reduced QR factorization and a full QR factorization, including when its columns are linearly dependent.

### 7.5.1 Full Column Rank

Assume first that the columns $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ are linearly independent. At Gram-Schmidt step $k$, define the residual

$$
\widehat q_k
=a_k-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}q_j(q_j^*a_k).
$$

If $\widehat q_k=0$, then

$$
a_k\in\operatorname{span}(q_1,\ldots,q_{k-1}).
$$

For independent columns, Gram-Schmidt preserves the nested spans:

$$
\operatorname{span}(q_1,\ldots,q_{k-1})
=\operatorname{span}(a_1,\ldots,a_{k-1}).
$$

Thus $a_k$ would lie in the span of its predecessors, contradicting independence. Therefore

$$
r_{kk}=\|\widehat q_k\|_2>0,
\qquad
q_k=\widehat q_k/r_{kk}
$$

is always defined, and Gram-Schmidt gives a reduced QR factorization.

### 7.5.2 Rank-Deficient Matrices

If the columns are dependent, a residual may be zero. A zero residual means

$$
a_k=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}q_jr_{jk},
\qquad
r_{kk}=0.
$$

There is no residual direction to normalize. Instead, choose **any unit vector** $q_k$ orthogonal to $q_1,\ldots,q_{k-1}$, and continue.

Such a choice exists because $k\le n\le m$, so $k-1$ orthonormal vectors cannot fill the entire $m$-dimensional space. The equality representing column $a_k$ is unaffected, since its coefficient of $q_k$ is zero.

The completed construction still has

$$
a_k=\sum_{j=1}^{k}q_jr_{jk}
\quad\text{for every }k,
$$

and hence $A=Q_1R_1$ with $Q_1^*Q_1=I_n$ and $R_1$ upper triangular.

> **Clarification.** The arbitrary replacement vector must be normalized as well as orthogonal. Also, $r_{kk}=0$ does not imply the entire $k$th row of $R_1$ is zero: a later column may have a nonzero component along the chosen $q_k$. For rank-deficient $A$, the $n$ columns of $Q_1$ may include directions outside $\operatorname{range}(A)$.

### 7.5.3 Completing to a Full Factorization

Extend the $n$ orthonormal columns of $Q_1$ to an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb K^m$:

$$
Q_{\mathrm{full}}=\begin{pmatrix}Q_1&Q_2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then

$$
A=Q_{\mathrm{full}}
\begin{pmatrix}R_1\\0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

This proves existence of a full QR factorization. The additional columns do not affect $A$, because they multiply zero rows in the full $R$ factor.

## 7.6 Uniqueness and the Diagonal Convention

*Source: p. 8.*

The source asks whether a QR factorization is unique and distinguishes full, reduced, and rank-deficient cases. One additional hypothesis is essential: the signs or phases of the diagonal entries of $R$ must be fixed.

### 7.6.1 Sign and Phase Ambiguity

For any diagonal matrix

$$
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n),
\qquad |d_j|=1,
$$

we have

$$
Q_1R_1=(Q_1D)(D^*R_1).
$$

The columns of $Q_1D$ remain orthonormal, and $D^*R_1$ remains upper triangular. Over $\mathbb R$, each $d_j$ can be $+1$ or $-1$. Thus full column rank alone does not make reduced QR unique.

For example, the one-entry matrix $A=(1)$ has both factorizations

$$
(1)=(1)(1)=(-1)(-1).
$$

> **Clarification.** This one-dimensional example makes explicit the sign ambiguity missing from the handwritten theorem. Uniqueness holds after requiring every diagonal entry of $R_1$ to be positive real.

### 7.6.2 The Correct Uniqueness Theorem

> [!theorem] Uniqueness of normalized reduced QR
> If $A\in\mathbb K^{m\times n}$ has full column rank, then its reduced QR factorization
> $$
> A=Q_1R_1,
> \qquad Q_1^*Q_1=I_n,
> $$
> is unique when $R_1$ is upper triangular and all its diagonal entries are positive real.

**Geometric proof, expanding the board argument.** For each $k$, set

$$
V_k=\operatorname{span}(a_1,\ldots,a_k).
$$

Full column rank implies $\dim V_k=k$. Because $R_1$ is upper triangular with nonzero diagonal,

$$
V_k=\operatorname{span}(q_1,\ldots,q_k).
$$

Therefore $q_k$ lies in

$$
V_k\cap V_{k-1}^{\perp},
$$

which is one-dimensional. Any other valid $\widetilde q_k$ must lie along the same line:

$$
\widetilde q_k=\alpha_k q_k,
\qquad |\alpha_k|=1.
$$

This is the content of the source's decomposition

$$
\widetilde q_k=\alpha_k q_k+\sum_{j<k}\gamma_j a_j.
$$

The difference $\widetilde q_k-\alpha_kq_k$ belongs to both $V_{k-1}$ and $V_{k-1}^{\perp}$, so it is zero. Unit norm fixes $|\alpha_k|=1$, but does not yet fix its sign or phase.

The diagonal coefficient is

$$
r_{kk}=q_k^*a_k>0.
$$

For the alternative factorization,

$$
\widetilde r_{kk}
=\widetilde q_k^*a_k
=\overline{\alpha_k}\,r_{kk}.
$$

Both diagonal entries are positive real, so $\alpha_k=1$. Hence every $q_k$ is unique, and then $R_1=Q_1^*A$ is unique as well.

**Equivalent algebraic check.** If $A=QR=\widetilde Q\widetilde R$ are two normalized reduced factorizations, then

$$
S=Q^*\widetilde Q=R\widetilde R^{-1}
$$

is both unitary and upper triangular. An upper triangular unitary matrix must be diagonal with unit-modulus diagonal entries. Positivity of the diagonals of $R$ and $\widetilde R$ makes those entries 1. Thus $S=I$, $Q=\widetilde Q$, and $R=\widetilde R$.

### 7.6.3 Why Full QR Is Generally Not Unique

For $m>n$, even if normalized reduced QR is fixed, the complementary orthonormal basis $Q_2$ can vary. If $W\in\mathbb K^{(m-n)\times(m-n)}$ is unitary, then

$$
\widetilde Q_{\mathrm{full}}
=\begin{pmatrix}Q_1&Q_2W\end{pmatrix}
$$

is another full orthogonal factor, and

$$
\widetilde Q_{\mathrm{full}}
\begin{pmatrix}R_1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=Q_1R_1=A.
$$

The extra columns multiply a zero block, so their basis is not determined by $A$.

When $m=n$, there is no complementary block. Full QR is then the same as reduced QR, and the positive-diagonal uniqueness theorem applies for nonsingular $A$.

### 7.6.4 Why Rank Deficiency Removes Uniqueness

When a Gram-Schmidt residual vanishes, the replacement unit vector can be chosen freely in an orthogonal complement. Those choices generally lead to different factorizations. Furthermore, a rank-deficient square $R_1$ cannot have all diagonal entries strictly positive, because it is singular.

The statements to remember are:

- Every $A$ with $m\ge n$ has full and reduced QR factorizations.
- Full column rank gives a unique **reduced** QR after fixing positive real diagonal entries of $R_1$.
- Without a sign or phase convention, even full-rank reduced QR is not unique.
- When $m>n$, the extra columns in full QR are generally nonunique.
- For rank-deficient $A$, QR is generally nonunique even with a nonnegative-diagonal convention.
