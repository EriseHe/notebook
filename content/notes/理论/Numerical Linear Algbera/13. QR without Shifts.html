13. QR without Shifts

2026-10-21

Numerical Methods I — Florian Schaefer. Source: Lecture 13.pdf, pp. 1–7.

13.1 Recap

The preceding lecture related Schur factorization to diagonalization. If \(A=QTQ^*\) is normal, then

\[ A^*A=AA^* \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T^*T=TT^*. \]

Since \(T\) is upper triangular, comparison of the first diagonal entries gives

\[ |T_{11}|^2=\sum_{j=1}^m|T_{1j}|^2, \]

so the rest of the first row vanishes. Repeating this argument on the trailing block shows that \(T\) is diagonal.

For orthogonal iteration with two vectors, suppose the first vector has converged to a unit eigenvector \(x_1\). With \(P=I-x_1x_1^*\), the second vector is updated in the direction

\[ q_2^{(k)}\ \propto\ PAPq_2^{(k-1)}. \]

For an eigenpair \(Ax_i=\lambda_i x_i\), the source’s relation is

\[ PAPx_i=\lambda_i Px_i. \]

Because \(P^2=P\), this also gives \(PAP(Px_i)=\lambda_i Px_i\). Thus the second direction approaches \(Px_2\), subject to normalization and the power-iteration assumptions. In general \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) need not be orthogonal, so \(Px_2\) need not equal \(x_2\).

The general algorithm repeatedly multiplies all current vectors by \(A\) and orthonormalizes them:

for k in 1:iterations
    Q = A * Q
    Q, R = qr(Q)
end

Here and below qr denotes the mathematical QR factorization, with the required orthonormal columns of \(Q\) made explicit. We use \(\lambda_i\) for the eigenvalues, consistently with Lecture 10.

13.2 Schur and Eigenvalue Decompositions

Consider

\[ A=X\Lambda X^{-1}, \qquad \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m), \qquad |\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|>\cdots>|\lambda_m|. \]

The lecture calls this situation generic: over \(\mathbb C\), every matrix has a Schur factorization \(A=QTQ^*\), whose diagonal entries are its eigenvalues. An arbitrarily small diagonal perturbation \(D=\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_m)\) can make the diagonal entries of \(T+D\) have distinct magnitudes. Then

\[ \widetilde A=Q(T+D)Q^*=A+QDQ^* \]

has eigenvalues of distinct magnitudes and is diagonalizable.

Conversely, given an eigendecomposition, how do we obtain a Schur factorization? Factor the eigenvector matrix:

\[ \begin{aligned} X&=QR\\ A&=QR\Lambda R^{-1}Q^*\\ &=Q\underbrace{(R\Lambda R^{-1})}_{\text{upper triangular}}Q^*. \end{aligned} \]

Both \(R\) and \(R^{-1}\) are upper triangular, so \(T=R\Lambda R^{-1}\) is upper triangular. Thus orthonormalizing the ordered eigenvectors produces Schur vectors.

13.3 Convergence of Orthogonal Iteration

Consider a diagonalizable matrix \(A=X\Lambda X^{-1}\) and \(p\) simultaneous vectors, with a spectral gap

\[ |\lambda_p|>|\lambda_{p+1}|. \]

Write \(Q_k\in\mathbb K^{m\times p}\) for the orthonormal iterates. Since QR factorization does not change the column space of a full-rank matrix,

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{span}(Q_{k+1}) &=\operatorname{span}(AQ_k)\\ &=\operatorname{span}(A^{k+1}Q_0)\\ &=\operatorname{span}(X\Lambda^{k+1}X^{-1}Q_0). \end{aligned} \]

For an iteration index \(i\), scale the power by \(\lambda_p^i\):

\[ A^iQ_0 =\lambda_p^i X \begin{pmatrix} (\lambda_1/\lambda_p)^i&&0\\ &\ddots&\\ 0&&(\lambda_m/\lambda_p)^i \end{pmatrix} X^{-1}Q_0. \]

For \(j\le p\), \(|\lambda_j/\lambda_p|\ge1\); for \(j>p\), the ratio has magnitude less than one. Partition

\[ X=[\,X_p\ \ X_p'\,], \qquad X^{-1}Q_0=\begin{pmatrix}C_1\\C_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad C_1\in\mathbb K^{p\times p}, \]

and set

\[ \begin{pmatrix}V_i\\W_i\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} (\lambda_1/\lambda_p)^i&&0\\ &\ddots&\\ 0&&(\lambda_m/\lambda_p)^i \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}C_1\\C_2\end{pmatrix}. \]

The lower block satisfies \(W_i\to0\), while

\[ \operatorname{span}(A^iQ_0) =\operatorname{span}(X_pV_i+X_p'W_i). \]

If \(C_1\) is nonsingular, so is \(V_i\). Multiplying on the right by \(V_i^{-1}\) makes the limiting subspace explicit:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{span}(A^iQ_0) &=\operatorname{span}(X_p+X_p'W_iV_i^{-1})\\ W_iV_i^{-1} &=D_2^iC_2C_1^{-1}D_1^{-i}\longrightarrow0, \end{aligned} \]

where

\[ D_1=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1/\lambda_p,\ldots,\lambda_p/\lambda_p), \qquad D_2=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{p+1}/\lambda_p,\ldots,\lambda_m/\lambda_p). \]

Indeed,

\[ \|W_iV_i^{-1}\|_2 \le \left|\frac{\lambda_{p+1}}{\lambda_p}\right|^i \|C_2C_1^{-1}\|_2 \longrightarrow0. \]

Therefore

\[ \operatorname{span}(Q_i) \longrightarrow \operatorname{span}(X_p) =\operatorname{span}(x_1,\ldots,x_p). \]

It is the subspace that converges; individual columns need not converge to individual eigenvectors.

13.4 Convergence to Schur Form

Theorem. Consider full orthogonal iteration, with \(p=m\) and \(Q_0=I\). Suppose the eigenvalues of \(A=X\Lambda X^{-1}\) have distinct absolute values, ordered decreasingly, and the leading blocks

\[ (X^{-1})[1\!:\!j,1\!:\!j], \qquad j=1,\ldots,m-1, \]

are nonsingular. Assume the QR steps are well defined. Then the entries below the diagonal of

\[ A_i=Q_i^*AQ_i \]

tend to zero: the iteration approaches Schur form. With compatible limiting column phases, a limiting matrix is a Schur factor of \(A\).

Proof. For any \(1\le p<m\), partition the full orthogonal matrix into its first \(p\) columns and the remaining columns:

\[ Q_i=[\,\overline Q_i\ \ \widetilde Q_i\,], \qquad \overline Q_i\in\mathbb K^{m\times p}. \]

Then

\[ A_i= \begin{pmatrix} \overline Q_i^*A\overline Q_i&\overline Q_i^*A\widetilde Q_i\\ \widetilde Q_i^*A\overline Q_i&\widetilde Q_i^*A\widetilde Q_i \end{pmatrix}. \]

The first \(p\) columns follow the same subspace iteration as in the preceding section. Its initial overlap matrix is

\[ (X^{-1}Q_0[:,1\!:\!p])[1\!:\!p,:] =(X^{-1})[1\!:\!p,1\!:\!p], \]

which is nonsingular by hypothesis. Consequently,

\[ \operatorname{span}(\overline Q_i) \longrightarrow\operatorname{span}(x_1,\ldots,x_p). \]

The limiting subspace is invariant under \(A\):

\[ A[\,x_1\ \cdots\ x_p\,] =[\,x_1\ \cdots\ x_p\,]\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_p). \]

Thus \(A\overline Q_i\) approaches the subspace spanned by \(\overline Q_i\). Since \(\widetilde Q_i\) is orthogonal to that subspace,

\[ \widetilde Q_i^*A\overline Q_i\longrightarrow0. \]

This holds for every splitting point \(p\), so all entries below the diagonal of \(Q_i^*AQ_i\) tend to zero. Each \(A_i\) is unitarily similar to \(A\), hence any upper-triangular limit is a Schur form of \(A\).

13.5 Deriving Basic QR Iteration

Orthogonal iteration gives

\[ T_k=Q_k^*AQ_k. \]

Computing the next iterate this way requires \(Q_k\) and multiplication by \(A\). Can we obtain \(T_{k+1}\) directly from \(T_k\)?

The orthogonal-iteration step is

\[ AQ_k=Q_{k+1}R_{k+1}. \]

Since the \(Q_k\) are square and unitary,

\[ \begin{aligned} R_{k+1}Q_k^*&=Q_{k+1}^*A\\ T_k&=Q_k^*(Q_{k+1}R_{k+1})\\ T_{k+1}&=(R_{k+1}Q_k^*)Q_{k+1}. \end{aligned} \]

Define

\[ U_{k+1}=Q_k^*Q_{k+1}. \]

This matrix is unitary, and the preceding relations become

\[ \boxed{ T_k=U_{k+1}R_{k+1}, \qquad T_{k+1}=R_{k+1}U_{k+1}. } \]

The first equation is a QR factorization of \(T_k\); the second reverses the order of its factors. Also,

\[ T_{k+1}=U_{k+1}^*T_kU_{k+1}, \]

so each step preserves the eigenvalues.

13.6 Algorithm: Basic QR without Shifts

Simply compute QR and swap the order of the factors:

T = A

for k in 1:max_iter
    U, R = qr(T)
    T = R * U
end

return diag(T)

The diagonal approximates the eigenvalues when \(T\) has become approximately upper triangular under the preceding convergence assumptions.

How do we recover the Schur vectors \(Q\)? The basic algorithm implicitly starts with \(Q_0=I\). Since

\[ U_{k+1}=Q_k^*Q_{k+1}, \]

the accumulated product telescopes:

\[ \begin{aligned} U_1U_2\cdots U_{k+1} &=(Q_0^*Q_1)(Q_1^*Q_2)\cdots(Q_k^*Q_{k+1})\\ &=Q_0^*Q_{k+1}\\ &=Q_{k+1}. \end{aligned} \]

Thus the \(U_i\) give successive orthogonal or unitary corrections taking \(I\) to the current Schur-vector approximation. One can accumulate them by updating \(Q\leftarrow QU\) at every step. Near a suitably phase-aligned limit, these are small corrections.

13.7 Corrections and Clarifications to the Source

  • Notation: Used \(\lambda_i\) for the source’s \(\delta_i\), following Lecture 10, and \(Q^*\) for the adjoint. For real matrices this is the source’s \(Q^T\). The prime in \(X_p'\) labels the remaining eigenvector columns; it is not a transpose.
  • Page 1: The projected power update is a direction followed by normalization. The displayed relation \(PAPx_i=\lambda_i Px_i\) also makes \(Px_i\) an eigenvector of \(PAP\) because \(P^2=P\).
  • Page 2: A diagonal perturbation in Schur coordinates changes \(A\) by \(QDQ^*\); it is not generally a diagonal perturbation in the original coordinates. The genericity argument is over \(\mathbb C\).
  • Page 3: Restored the power \(\Lambda^i\) in the convergence calculation, used one iteration index consistently, and made the nonsingularity of \(C_1\) explicit. Multiplication by \(V_i^{-1}\) completes the source’s subspace-limit argument.
  • Pages 4–5: The required leading-block condition is on \(X^{-1}Q_0\) (on \(X^{-1}\) when \(Q_0=I\)), rather than the leading blocks of \(X\) written in the theorem. The proof shows that entries below the diagonal tend to zero. Signs or complex phases can prevent literal convergence of all entries without a phase convention.
  • Page 7: The recovered columns are Schur vectors; they need not be eigenvectors for a nonnormal matrix. The description of each update as a small rotation presumes compatible column signs or phases near convergence.
