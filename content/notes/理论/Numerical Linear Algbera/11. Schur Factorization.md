## 11.1 Recap: Eigenvalue Computations

<!-- Source: Lecture 11.pdf, p. 1. -->

An eigenvalue can be a root of a polynomial. For the monic polynomial

$$
p(z)=z^m+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}a_i z^i,
$$

the companion matrix

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&0&1\\
-a_0&-a_1&\cdots&-a_{m-2}&-a_{m-1}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

satisfies $Ax=zx$ for $x=(1,z,\ldots,z^{m-1})^T$ precisely when $p(z)=0$.

If $A=X\Lambda X^{-1}$ and $z=X\varphi$, power iteration gives

$$
A^kz=X\Lambda^k\varphi,
\qquad
\Lambda^k=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k,\ldots,\lambda_m^k).
$$

The powers favor the eigendirection of largest magnitude: if $|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_j|$ for $j>1$, then

$$
\lambda_1^{-k}\Lambda^k
=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(1,(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^k,\ldots,(\lambda_m/\lambda_1)^k\right)
\longrightarrow\operatorname{diag}(1,0,\ldots,0).
$$

The companion-matrix construction is the preceding lecture's negative result: an exact general eigenvalue formula would give an exact general polynomial-root formula. The lecture also recalls matrix functions, inverse iteration, and Rayleigh-quotient iteration. The spectrum sketch illustrates inversion moving an eigenvalue close to zero far away from the other eigenvalues.

## 11.2 Deflation

<!-- Source: Lecture 11.pdf, p. 2. -->

Suppose we have computed a largest eigenpair $(x,\lambda_1)$. How can we avoid finding the same eigenpair again? The lecture proposes deflation by forming

$$
A-\lambda_1\frac{xx^*}{x^*x}.
$$

The computed eigenvalue becomes zero for the direction $x$. Deflation raises the question of how to obtain the remaining eigenpairs without recomputing the same one.

## 11.3 Subspace Iteration

<!-- Source: Lecture 11.pdf, p. 3. -->

Individual eigenvalues can in principle be computed one by one, and individual eigenpairs can in principle be computed one by one. Doing so needs at least $m$ matrix-vector products for $m$ eigenpairs. Because these products happen serially, the matrix must be loaded from RAM $m$ times.

The alternative is to apply power iteration simultaneously to multiple vectors. Matrix-vector products become matrix-matrix products: **subspace iteration**.

## 11.4 Eigenvalue Computation as Matrix Factorization

<!-- Source: Lecture 11.pdf, p. 4. -->

If $A\in\mathbb K^{m\times m}$ has $m$ distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m$ and corresponding eigenvectors $x_1,\ldots,x_m$, collect the eigenvectors into $X=(x_1,\ldots,x_m)$. Then

$$
A=X\Lambda X^{-1},
\qquad \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m).
$$

For a self-adjoint matrix, $X$ can be chosen unitary, so $A=X\Lambda X^*$.

## 11.5 Defective Matrices and Invariant Subspaces

<!-- Source: Lecture 11.pdf, p. 5. -->

Are all matrices diagonalizable? Consider

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its eigenpairs have eigenvalue $\lambda$ and eigenvectors proportional to $e_1$. The matrix has only one linearly independent eigenvector. Such a matrix is called **defective**. For $\lambda=0$, the source's sketch is the action

$$
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}e_2=e_1,
\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}e_1=0.
$$

The lecture describes the common exponential scale as "$\lambda^n$ growth," motivating an invariant subspace in place of an eigenspace. This is an intuition about the eigenvalue scale, not an exact formula for all iterates of a Jordan block.

This motivates replacing the search for a $\lambda$-eigenspace with the search for an **invariant subspace**. A subspace spanned by the columns of $X\in\mathbb K^{m\times n}$ is $A$-invariant when

$$
AX=X\beta
\quad\text{for some }\beta\in\mathbb K^{n\times n}.
$$

<!-- Source: Lecture 11.pdf, p. 6. -->

The Jordan normal form states that for every $A\in\mathbb K^{m\times m}$ there is a nonsingular $S\in\mathbb C^{m\times m}$ such that

$$
A=SJS^{-1},
\qquad
J=\operatorname{diag}(J_1,\ldots,J_r),
\qquad
J_\ell=\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda_\ell&1&&0\\
&\lambda_\ell&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&1\\
0&&&\lambda_\ell
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Each Jordan block has only one linearly independent eigenvector, its first coordinate vector. Jordan form decomposes the space into invariant subspaces, but it is **not numerically stable**: a small perturbation can change the block structure, for example

$$
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}
\longrightarrow
\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&1\\0&2\epsilon\end{pmatrix}.
$$

## 11.6 Schur Factorization

<!-- Source: Lecture 11.pdf, p. 7. -->

**Theorem (complex Schur form).** For any $A\in\mathbb C^{m\times m}$, there exist a unitary $Q$ and an upper-triangular $T$ such that

$$
A=QTQ^*.
$$

The eigenvalues of $A$ appear on the diagonal of $T$.

**Proof.** Every complex square matrix has at least one eigenvalue $\lambda$ with an eigenvector $u$. Normalize $u$ and extend it to a unitary matrix $U=(u,\widetilde U)$. Then

$$
U^*AU=
\begin{pmatrix}
u^*Au&u^*A\widetilde U\\
\widetilde U^*Au&\widetilde U^*A\widetilde U
\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda&u^*A\widetilde U\\
0&\widetilde U^*A\widetilde U
\end{pmatrix},
$$

because $Au=\lambda u$ and $\widetilde U^*u=0$. Apply the same construction recursively to the lower-right block. $\square$

<!-- Source: Lecture 11.pdf, p. 8. -->

For real matrices, it is preferable to stay in real arithmetic. **Real Schur form** gives an orthogonal $Q$ and a block-upper-triangular $T$ with diagonal blocks of size $1\times1$ or $2\times2$ such that $A=QTQ^*$. The $2\times2$ blocks represent complex-conjugate eigenvalue pairs.

## 11.7 Computing Eigenvectors from Schur Form

<!-- Source: Lecture 11.pdf, p. 9. -->

Schur factorization yields eigenvalues directly. To obtain an eigenvector, solve $Tx=\lambda x$ and then use $Qx$, since

$$
A(Qx)=QTQ^*(Qx)=QTx=\lambda Qx.
$$

Partition the upper-triangular matrix around a chosen diagonal entry $\lambda$. Here $T_{11}$ and $T_{33}$ denote the leading and trailing triangular blocks, while the middle diagonal block is the scalar $\lambda$:

$$
(T-\lambda I)x=
\begin{pmatrix}
T_{11}-\lambda I&T_{12}&T_{13}\\
0&0&T_{23}\\
0&0&T_{33}-\lambda I
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$

The block equations are

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
(T_{11}-\lambda I)x_1+T_{12}x_2+T_{13}x_3\\
T_{23}x_3\\
(T_{33}-\lambda I)x_3
\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$

Take $x_2=1$ and $x_3=0$. If $\lambda I-T_{11}$ is nonsingular, solve the first block row by triangular substitution:

$$
x=\begin{pmatrix}
(\lambda I-T_{11})^{-1}T_{12}\\
1\\
0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

## Corrections and Clarifications to the Source

- **Notation:** The handwritten lecture uses $\delta$ for eigenvalues. These notes use $\lambda$, consistent with Lecture 10. Complex adjoints are denoted by $^*$; for real vectors the deflation formula is exactly the source's formula with $x^T$.
- **Page 1:** The companion matrix and its monic polynomial relation are written out where the source uses an abbreviated matrix picture.
- **Page 2:** The stated deflation formula removes the computed eigenvalue along $x$. For a general nonnormal matrix, computing other eigenpairs by deflation needs more care with left and right eigenvectors.
- **Pages 6–7:** Jordan representation uses a complex similarity matrix even when $A$ is real. The Schur proof also uses a complex eigenvector; real Schur form is stated separately on page 8.
- **Page 5:** The source's "$\lambda^n$ growth" is an exponential-scale intuition; Jordan powers can also have polynomial factors. The zero-eigenvalue example is nilpotent.
- **Page 9:** Made the block partition and the nonsingularity needed for $(\lambda I-T_{11})^{-1}$ explicit. The formula denotes a triangular solve, not a recommendation to form an inverse.
