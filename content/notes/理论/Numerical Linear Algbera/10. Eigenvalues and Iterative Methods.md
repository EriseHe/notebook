## 10.1 Recap

<!-- Source: Lecture 10.pdf, p. 1. -->

The preceding lectures developed LU, QR, and Cholesky factorizations through elimination and orthogonal projection. For a Hermitian positive definite matrix, symmetry-preserving elimination gives

$$
A=LL^*,
\qquad
A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\A_{21}&A_{22}\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
L=\begin{pmatrix}L_{11}&0\\L_{21}&L_{22}\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The remaining block is described by the Schur complement:

$$
\boxed{A_{22}-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}=L_{22}L_{22}^*.}
$$

Positive definiteness keeps the elimination pivots positive. It also controls factor growth through

$$
\begin{aligned}
(|L||L|^T)_{ij}
&=\sum_k |L_{ik}|\,|L_{jk}|\\
&\le\sqrt{\sum_k|L_{ik}|^2}\sqrt{\sum_k|L_{jk}|^2}
=\sqrt{A_{ii}A_{jj}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

We now turn to factorizations organized around eigenvectors. The lecture names eigenvalue, polar, Schur, and singular value decompositions, then begins with the problem of computing a single eigenpair.

## 10.2 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

<!-- Source: Lecture 10.pdf, p. 2. -->

Write $\mathbb{K}$ for $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and let $A\in\mathbb{K}^{m\times m}$. The star denotes conjugate transpose.

> [!definition] Eigenpair
> A scalar $\lambda\in\mathbb{K}$ and a nonzero vector $x\in\mathbb{K}^m$ form an eigenpair of $A$ when
> $$
> Ax=\lambda x.
> $$

Multiplication by $A$ preserves the direction of an eigenvector and scales it by its eigenvalue. We use $\lambda_i$ for the eigenvalues denoted by $\delta_i$ on the sheet.[^eigenpair-notation]

### 10.2.1 Hermitian Matrices

Suppose $A=A^*$.

> [!theorem] Reality and orthogonality
> Every eigenvalue of a Hermitian matrix is real. Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

If $Ax=\lambda x$, then

$$
\lambda=\frac{x^*Ax}{x^*x}.
$$

The denominator is positive, and the numerator is real because

$$
\overline{x^*Ax}=x^*A^*x=x^*Ax.
$$

Thus $\lambda=\overline\lambda$.[^rayleigh-denominator]

For $Ax_i=\lambda_i x_i$ and $Ax_j=\lambda_j x_j$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_i x_i^*x_j
&=(Ax_i)^*x_j\\
&=x_i^*Ax_j
=\lambda_j x_i^*x_j.
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently,

$$
(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)x_i^*x_j=0,
\qquad
\lambda_i\ne\lambda_j\Longrightarrow x_i^*x_j=0.
$$

The spectral theorem supplies an orthonormal eigenbasis, including within repeated eigenspaces. With

$$
X=\begin{pmatrix}x_1&\cdots&x_m\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m),
$$

we can therefore write

$$
\boxed{A=X\Lambda X^*,\qquad X^*X=I.}
$$

The subsequent convergence arguments use this Hermitian setting. A general matrix need not have an orthonormal eigenbasis, or even be diagonalizable.

## 10.3 Why Eigenvalue Computation Is Iterative

<!-- Source: Lecture 10.pdf, p. 3. -->

LU, Cholesky, and QR can be computed by a finite sequence of elimination or orthogonalization steps, using $O(m^3)$ arithmetic work for a dense square matrix. Ignoring roundoff, these procedures produce exact factorizations.

> Can we compute eigenvalues by a similar finite formula?

The lecture invokes the Abel–Ruffini obstruction: there is no general formula in radicals for the roots of a polynomial of degree five or higher.[^abel-ruffini] Eigenvalue computation contains polynomial root finding as a special case.

To see this, consider a monic polynomial

$$
p(z)=z^m+a_{m-1}z^{m-1}+\cdots+a_1z+a_0
$$

and its companion matrix

$$
C=\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&0&1\\
-a_0&-a_1&-a_2&\cdots&-a_{m-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

If $p(z)=0$, define

$$
v(z)=\begin{pmatrix}1&z&\cdots&z^{m-1}\end{pmatrix}^T.
$$

Then

$$
Cv(z)
=\begin{pmatrix}
z\\z^2\\\vdots\\z^{m-1}\\-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}a_i z^i
\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}z\\z^2\\\vdots\\z^{m-1}\\z^m\end{pmatrix}
=zv(z).
$$

Thus every root of $p$ is an eigenvalue of $C$. A general finite radical formula for eigenvalues would give one for polynomial roots as well.

This companion matrix is not generally Hermitian; the example explains the obstruction for the general eigenvalue problem. For numerical computation, we instead seek a sequence of increasingly accurate approximations.

## 10.4 Power Iteration

<!-- Source: Lecture 10.pdf, pp. 4–5. -->

For $A=X\Lambda X^*$, write a starting vector in eigenvector coordinates:

$$
z=Xy=\sum_{i=1}^m y_i x_i.
$$

Repeated multiplication gives

$$
Az=X\Lambda y,
\qquad
A^2z=X\Lambda^2y,
\qquad
A^kz=X\Lambda^k y.
$$

Assume the eigenvalues are ordered by magnitude and that there is a unique dominant one:

$$
|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|\ge\cdots\ge|\lambda_m|.
$$

If $y_1\ne0$, then

$$
A^kz
=\lambda_1^k y_1
\left(
x_1+\sum_{i=2}^m\frac{y_i}{y_1}
\left(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\right)^k x_i
\right).
$$

Every ratio in the sum has magnitude less than one. The component along $x_1$ therefore dominates as $k$ grows.

### 10.4.1 Normalizing at Every Step

Computing $A^kz$ directly can overflow or underflow. We only need its direction, so normalize after each multiplication:

$$
\boxed{
z^{(k)}=Aq^{(k)},
\qquad
q^{(k+1)}=\frac{z^{(k)}}{\|z^{(k)}\|_2}.
}
$$

In exact arithmetic this produces the direction of $A^{k+1}q^{(0)}$ without explicitly forming a matrix power. A Gaussian starting vector has a nonzero component in the dominant eigendirection with probability one under the continuous model.

For a nonzero vector $q$, define its **Rayleigh quotient** by

$$
r(q)=\frac{q^*Aq}{q^*q}.
$$

For a unit vector, this reduces to $r(q)=q^*Aq$. If $q=x_i$, the quotient is exactly $\lambda_i$, so it provides an eigenvalue estimate along with the eigenvector estimate.

### 10.4.2 Algorithm

The following Julia-style transcription assumes a real symmetric floating-point matrix and a prescribed number of iterations. `norm` and `dot` are from Julia's `LinearAlgebra` standard library.

```julia
q = randn(size(A, 1))
q /= norm(q)

for k in 1:maxiter
    z = A * q
    rho = norm(z)
    if iszero(rho)
        error("Power iteration reached a zero vector.")
    end
    q = z / rho
end

lambda = dot(q, A * q)
```

The final quotient is evaluated at the returned vector. The sheet evaluates it before the normalization within the loop, which estimates the eigenvalue using the preceding iterate.[^iteration-code]

## 10.5 Convergence of Power Iteration

<!-- Source: Lecture 10.pdf, pp. 6–7. -->

Let $q^{(0)}$ be a unit vector with expansion

$$
q^{(0)}=\sum_{i=1}^m\alpha_i x_i,
\qquad \alpha_1\ne0,
\qquad \sum_i|\alpha_i|^2=1.
$$

Set

$$
\rho=\left|\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right|<1.
$$

The relevant error is the angle $\theta_k$ between $q^{(k)}$ and the line spanned by $x_1$. Using the orthonormal eigenbasis,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tan\theta_k
&=\frac{\left\|\sum_{i=2}^m\alpha_i\lambda_i^k x_i\right\|_2}
{|\alpha_1\lambda_1^k|}\\
&=\left(
\sum_{i=2}^m\left|\frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_1}\right|^2
\left|\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\right|^{2k}
\right)^{1/2}\\
&\le\frac{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_1|^2}}{|\alpha_1|}\rho^k.
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!theorem] Power iteration convergence
> If $A$ is Hermitian, $|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|$, and $\alpha_1\ne0$, then the normalized power iterates converge to the dominant eigendirection with
> $$
> \sin\theta_k=O(\rho^k),
> \qquad \rho=|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|.
> $$

For a negative dominant eigenvalue, the vector may alternate signs at every iteration. Over $\mathbb C$, an eigenvector is similarly defined only up to a unit-modulus scalar. Convergence of the eigendirection allows these sign or phase changes.[^power-direction]

### 10.5.1 Accuracy of the Eigenvalue Estimate

For a unit vector $q=\sum_i\beta_i x_i$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
r(q)-\lambda_1
&=\sum_i\lambda_i|\beta_i|^2-\lambda_1\sum_i|\beta_i|^2\\
&=\sum_{i=2}^m(\lambda_i-\lambda_1)|\beta_i|^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\sum_{i=2}^m|\beta_i|^2=\sin^2\theta$,

$$
|r(q)-\lambda_1|
\le\max_{i\ge2}|\lambda_i-\lambda_1|\,\sin^2\theta.
$$

Therefore the power iteration estimates satisfy

$$
\boxed{|r(q^{(k)})-\lambda_1|=O(\rho^{2k}).}
$$

The eigenvalue error is second order in the error of the eigenvector direction.

### 10.5.2 When Convergence Is Slow

The ratio $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|$ determines how quickly competing components disappear. If it is close to one, convergence is slow. A small initial coefficient $|\alpha_1|$ can also delay the point at which the dominant component becomes visible.

The sketches on the sheet distinguish nearby eigenvalues with the same sign from eigenvalues with nearly equal magnitude and opposite signs. Both give slow separation in magnitude. Opposite signs additionally cause relative sign changes between the corresponding components.

If $|\lambda_1|=|\lambda_2|$, the strict dominance assumption fails. Power iteration need not select one eigenvector; for example, eigenvalues $\lambda$ and $-\lambda$ can produce an alternating direction.

## 10.6 Matrix Functions and Polynomial Filtering

<!-- Source: Lecture 10.pdf, p. 8. -->

> How can we change the eigenvalues while preserving the eigenvectors?

If $Ax=\lambda x$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^kx&=\lambda^k x,\\
(\alpha A)x&=(\alpha\lambda)x,\\
(A+\beta I)x&=(\lambda+\beta)x,\\
p(A)x&=p(\lambda)x.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last identity follows by expanding a polynomial $p(t)=\sum_{j=0}^d c_jt^j$:

$$
p(A)x
=\sum_{j=0}^d c_j A^j x
=\left(\sum_{j=0}^d c_j\lambda^j\right)x
=p(\lambda)x.
$$

For a diagonalizable matrix,

$$
p(A)=Xp(\Lambda)X^{-1}.
$$

A polynomial can amplify some eigendirections relative to others by making the corresponding values $|p(\lambda_i)|$ larger. This is the lecture's idea of **polynomial filtering**.

The number-line sketch illustrates a shift by $I$: every eigenvalue moves from $\lambda_i$ to $\lambda_i+1$. A suitable shift can change which transformed eigenvalue has largest magnitude. It does not change the eigenvectors, although different eigenvalues can map to the same transformed value.

## 10.7 Inverse Iteration

<!-- Source: Lecture 10.pdf, p. 9. -->

Power iteration targets the eigenvalue of largest magnitude. Suppose instead that we want an eigenvalue near a real number $\mu$.

If $\mu$ is not an eigenvalue of $A$, then

$$
(A-\mu I)x_i=(\lambda_i-\mu)x_i
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
(A-\mu I)^{-1}x_i=\frac{1}{\lambda_i-\mu}x_i.
$$

The eigenvalue closest to $\mu$ produces the transformed eigenvalue of largest magnitude. Apply power iteration to this inverse:

$$
\boxed{
(A-\mu I)z^{(k)}=q^{(k)},
\qquad
q^{(k+1)}=\frac{z^{(k)}}{\|z^{(k)}\|_2}.
}
$$

The inverse is a mathematical description. Numerically, each step solves a linear system.

### 10.7.1 Algorithm with a Fixed Shift

For a fixed $\mu$, the matrix $A-\mu I$ does not change. Its LU factorization can be computed once and reused:

```julia
q = randn(size(A, 1))
q /= norm(q)
F = lu(A - mu * I)

for k in 1:maxiter
    z = F \ q
    q = z / norm(z)
end

lambda = dot(q, A * q)
```

Here `lu` and `I` are also from `LinearAlgebra`, and $A-\mu I$ is assumed nonsingular. Reusing the factorization implements the fixed-shift solves on the sheet without repeating the factorization at every iteration.

### 10.7.2 Convergence Factor

Suppose $\lambda_j$ is uniquely closest to $\mu$ and the starting vector has a nonzero component along $x_j$. The power iteration argument gives the convergence factor

$$
\rho_\mu
=\max_{i\ne j}\left|\frac{\lambda_j-\mu}{\lambda_i-\mu}\right|
=\frac{|\lambda_j-\mu|}{\min_{i\ne j}|\lambda_i-\mu|}
<1.
$$

A shift close to $\lambda_j$, compared with its distance from the other eigenvalues, gives fast convergence. The Rayleigh quotient is still evaluated using the original $A$, so it estimates $\lambda_j$ itself.

## 10.8 Rayleigh Quotient Iteration

<!-- Source: Lecture 10.pdf, p. 10. -->

> What if a good shift is not known in advance?

Use the current eigenvector estimate to choose it:

$$
\mu_k=r(q^{(k)}).
$$

One inverse iteration step then gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
(A-\mu_k I)z^{(k)}&=q^{(k)},\\
q^{(k+1)}&=\frac{z^{(k)}}{\|z^{(k)}\|_2},\\
\mu_{k+1}&=r(q^{(k+1)}).
\end{aligned}
$$

A better vector gives a better shift, and a better shift strengthens the next inverse iteration step.

The lecture compares this feedback with Newton's method. The rate still needs its own analysis; for a symmetric matrix near a simple eigenpair, the combination will give cubic convergence.

### 10.8.1 Algorithm

For a real symmetric matrix, the Julia-style iteration is

```julia
q = randn(size(A, 1))
q /= norm(q)
mu = dot(q, A * q)

for k in 1:maxiter
    if norm(A * q - mu * q) <= tol
        break
    end
    z = (A - mu * I) \ q
    q = z / norm(z)
    mu = dot(q, A * q)
end
```

Here `tol` is a chosen absolute residual tolerance. The residual check is an explicit stopping condition added to the lecture loop.[^iteration-code] An exact eigenpair has zero residual and should be accepted before attempting a singular shifted solve.

Unlike fixed-shift inverse iteration, the coefficient matrix changes with $\mu_k$, so its numerical factorization generally changes at each step. The convergence argument below assumes the shifted systems encountered before termination are nonsingular. A zero residual is sufficient for termination, but a nonzero residual alone does not guarantee that a Rayleigh shift avoids the spectrum.

## 10.9 Why the Rayleigh Quotient Is Accurate

<!-- Source: Lecture 10.pdf, pp. 11–12. -->

For the derivative calculation, take $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ symmetric, as on the sheet. For $x\ne0$,

$$
r(x)=\frac{x^TAx}{x^Tx}.
$$

The quotient is unchanged by a nonzero scalar rescaling of $x$. Differentiating the numerator and denominator gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla r(x)
&=\frac{2Ax\,(x^Tx)-2x\,(x^TAx)}{(x^Tx)^2}\\
&=\frac{2}{x^Tx}\bigl(Ax-r(x)x\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$

At an eigenvector $x_j$,

$$
Ax_j=\lambda_jx_j,
\qquad r(x_j)=\lambda_j,
\qquad \boxed{\nabla r(x_j)=0.}
$$

Thus eigenvectors are stationary points of the Rayleigh quotient. For a small perturbation $h$, Taylor expansion yields[^taylor]

$$
\begin{aligned}
r(x_j+h)
&=r(x_j)+\underbrace{\nabla r(x_j)^Th}_{0}
+\frac12h^T\nabla^2r(x_j)h+O(\|h\|_2^3)\\
&=\lambda_j+O(\|h\|_2^2).
\end{aligned}
$$

There is no first-order change. This explains the locally flat graph of $r$ in the lecture and the second-order accuracy of the eigenvalue estimate.

### 10.9.1 An Exact Error Identity

Normalize $x_j$ and $q$, and write

$$
q=\alpha x_j+e,
\qquad e\perp x_j,
\qquad \alpha^2+\|e\|_2^2=1.
$$

The cross terms vanish because $Ax_j=\lambda_jx_j$. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
r(q)-\lambda_j
&=\alpha^2\lambda_j+e^TAe-\lambda_j\\
&=e^T(A-\lambda_jI)e.
\end{aligned}
$$

In particular,

$$
\boxed{|r(q)-\lambda_j|\le\|A-\lambda_jI\|_2\,\|e\|_2^2.}
$$

This gives the same second-order estimate directly in terms of the error perpendicular to the target eigendirection. It will also supply the shift estimate in the convergence proof.

## 10.10 Local Cubic Convergence

<!-- Source: Lecture 10.pdf, p. 13. -->

The final page combines the improved Rayleigh shift with inverse iteration. We make the separation and nonsingularity assumptions explicit.[^cubic-proof]

> [!theorem] Local convergence of Rayleigh quotient iteration
> Let $A$ be real symmetric and let $\lambda_j$ be a simple eigenvalue with unit eigenvector $x_j$. For normalized iterates sufficiently close to its eigendirection, each well-defined Rayleigh quotient iteration step satisfies
> $$
> \sin\theta_{k+1}=O(\sin^3\theta_k),
> $$
> where $\theta_k$ is the angle to the line spanned by $x_j$. This is local cubic convergence in exact arithmetic.

### 10.10.1 Proof

Choose the sign of the current unit vector $q$ so that

$$
q=\alpha x_j+e,
\qquad e\perp x_j,
\qquad \alpha>0,
\qquad \epsilon=\|e\|_2=\sin\theta.
$$

Near the target eigendirection, $\alpha=\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}$ is bounded away from zero. Define the spectral gap

$$
g=\min_{i\ne j}|\lambda_i-\lambda_j|>0.
$$

For $\mu=r(q)$, the preceding identity gives

$$
|\mu-\lambda_j|=O(\epsilon^2).
$$

Once $q$ is sufficiently close, $|\mu-\lambda_j|<g/2$, and hence

$$
|\lambda_i-\mu|\ge g/2,
\qquad i\ne j.
$$

For a nonsingular shifted system, decompose its solution as

$$
\begin{aligned}
z=(A-\mu I)^{-1}q
&=\underbrace{\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_j-\mu}x_j}_{\text{target component}}
+\underbrace{(A-\mu I)^{-1}e}_{w\perp x_j}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The inverse is bounded on the orthogonal complement of $x_j$:

$$
\|w\|_2\le\frac{2}{g}\|e\|_2=\frac{2\epsilon}{g}.
$$

Normalization does not change the ratio between the perpendicular and target components. If $q_+=z/\|z\|_2$ has angle $\theta_+$ to $x_j$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tan\theta_+
&=\frac{\|w\|_2}{|\alpha/(\lambda_j-\mu)|}\\
&\le\frac{2\epsilon\,|\lambda_j-\mu|}{g\alpha}\\
&=O(\epsilon^3).
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\sin\theta_+\le\tan\theta_+$,

$$
\boxed{\sin\theta_+=O(\epsilon^3)=O(\sin^3\theta).}
$$

The mechanism is now explicit: an eigenvector error of size $\epsilon$ produces a shift error of size $O(\epsilon^2)$, and the inverse step reduces the relative unwanted component by this additional factor. The result is an error of size $O(\epsilon^3)$.

The theorem is local: a random starting vector is not asserted to enter this neighborhood in every case. It also concerns exact arithmetic; rounding and the accuracy of the shifted solves limit the improvement observed in a numerical implementation.
