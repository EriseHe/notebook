## 3.1 Recap

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 1. -->

The preceding lecture discussed array storage, the distinction between arithmetic work and memory-access cost, cache, loop ordering, and tiling. It then introduced triangular substitution and Gaussian elimination for linear systems.

The question now is how to organize elimination into a factorization that can be stored and used efficiently.

## 3.2 Elementary Gaussian Elimination

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 2. -->

At step $k$, let $A$ denote the current matrix after the first $k-1$ elimination steps. Its first $k-1$ columns are already zero below their diagonals. Assume $A_{kk}\ne0$.

The elementary lower-triangular matrix $L_k$ has ones on the diagonal and

$$
(L_k)_{ik}=-\frac{A_{ik}}{A_{kk}},\qquad i=k+1,\ldots,m,
$$

with all other off-diagonal entries zero. Multiplication by $L_k$ performs

$$
\begin{aligned}
(L_kA)_{ik}
&=A_{ik}-\frac{A_{ik}}{A_{kk}}A_{kk}=0,
&&i>k,\\
(L_kA)_{ij}
&=A_{ij}-\frac{A_{ik}}{A_{kk}}A_{kj},
&&i>k,\ j>k.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is Theorem 1 from the preceding lecture: each step creates zeros below one more diagonal entry and updates the trailing block.

## 3.3 LU Factorization

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 3. -->

If all the required elimination steps are defined, then

$$
U=L_m\cdots L_1A
$$

is upper triangular. As in the source, the product includes a final, trivial factor $L_m=I$. The corresponding transformed system is[^transformed-system]

$$
\underbrace{L_m\cdots L_1A}_{U}x=L_m\cdots L_1b.
$$

Define

$$
L=(L_m\cdots L_1)^{-1}
=L_1^{-1}\cdots L_m^{-1}.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{A=LU.}
$$

To make this useful, we need to show that $L$ is lower triangular and understand its entries.

## 3.4 Algebra of Lower-Triangular Matrices

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 4. -->

A matrix $L\in\mathbb{K}^{m\times m}$ is lower triangular when $L_{ij}=0$ for $i<j$.

> [!theorem] Theorem 2 — Closure properties
> Lower-triangular matrices are closed under addition, scalar multiplication, and matrix multiplication. The inverse of a **nonsingular** lower-triangular matrix is also lower triangular.

Addition and scalar multiplication preserve the zeros above the diagonal immediately.

For multiplication, let $L$ and $\widetilde{L}$ be lower triangular. Then

$$
(L\widetilde{L})_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^m L_{ik}\widetilde{L}_{kj}.
$$

A nonzero summand requires both

$$
L_{ik}\ne0\Longrightarrow i\ge k,
\qquad
\widetilde{L}_{kj}\ne0\Longrightarrow k\ge j.
$$

Consequently,

$$
L_{ik}\widetilde{L}_{kj}\ne0\Longrightarrow i\ge k\ge j.
$$

If $i<j$, no such $k$ exists, so $(L\widetilde{L})_{ij}=0$. Hence the product is lower triangular.

The inversion property is completed after analyzing elementary lower-triangular matrices.

## 3.5 Elementary Lower-Triangular Matrices

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, pp. 4–5. -->

Write the elimination multipliers as $\ell_{ik}$ and define

$$
\ell_k=
\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
\ell_{k+1,k}\\
\vdots\\
\ell_{mk}
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\underbrace{(\ell_k)_1=\cdots=(\ell_k)_k}_{\text{first }k\text{ entries}}=0.
$$

Let $e_k$ be the $k$-th coordinate vector. The star denotes conjugate transpose, so $e_k^*\ell_k$ selects the $k$-th entry of $\ell_k$. Then

$$
L_k=I-\ell_ke_k^*,
\qquad e_k^*\ell_k=0.
$$

> [!theorem] Theorem 3 — Inverses and products of elementary factors
> For $L_k=I-\ell_ke_k^*$ with $(\ell_k)_i=0$ for $i\le k$,
> $$
> L_k^{-1}=I+\ell_ke_k^*.
> $$
> Moreover, in the indicated order,
> $$
> L_1^{-1}\cdots L_m^{-1}
> =I+\sum_{k=1}^m\ell_ke_k^*
> =\begin{pmatrix}
> 1&0&\cdots&0\\
> \ell_{21}&1&\ddots&\vdots\\
> \vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
> \ell_{m1}&\cdots&\ell_{m,m-1}&1
> \end{pmatrix}.
> $$

### 3.5.1 Proof of the Inverse Formula

Expand the product:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(I+\ell_ke_k^*)(I-\ell_ke_k^*)
&=I+\cancel{\ell_ke_k^*}-\cancel{\ell_ke_k^*}
  -\ell_k\underbrace{(e_k^*\ell_k)}_{0}e_k^*\\
&=I.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus changing the signs of the multipliers gives the inverse of an elementary elimination matrix.

### 3.5.2 Proof of the Product Formula

The lecture proves the result entrywise. Consider

$$
\begin{aligned}
(L_1^{-1}\cdots L_m^{-1})_{ij}
&=e_i^*(I+\ell_1e_1^*)\cdots(I+\ell_me_m^*)e_j\\
&=e_i^*(I+\ell_1e_1^*)\cdots
  (I+\ell_{j-1}e_{j-1}^*)(e_j+\ell_j)\\
&=e_i^*(I+\ell_1e_1^*)\cdots
  (I+\ell_{j-1}e_{j-1}^*)\ell_j+e_i^*e_j\\
&=e_i^*\ell_j+e_i^*e_j.
\end{aligned}
$$

The factors with index greater than $j$ leave $e_j$ unchanged. After the $j$-th factor produces $e_j+\ell_j$, the factors with index less than $j$ leave both vectors unchanged, because their relevant coordinates are zero.

The resulting matrix has ones on the diagonal and the multipliers $\ell_{ij}$ below it. Therefore the lower-triangular factor in $A=LU$ is obtained simply by storing these multipliers.

### 3.5.3 Completing the Inversion Property

For a product of elementary factors, Theorem 3 and closure under multiplication show that its inverse is lower triangular:

$$
(L_m\cdots L_1)^{-1}=L_1^{-1}\cdots L_m^{-1}.
$$

For the general nonsingular lower-triangular matrix, the missing step can be supplied directly by forward substitution.[^inverse-completion] Solve $Lx=e_j$. For $i<j$, the right-hand side is zero, and forward substitution gives

$$
x_1=\cdots=x_{j-1}=0.
$$

Since this solution is the $j$-th column of $L^{-1}$, every entry of $L^{-1}$ above its diagonal is zero. This completes Theorem 2.

## 3.6 Computing LU in Place

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 6. -->

Gaussian elimination transforms $A$ into $U$. The multipliers needed for $L$ can be stored in the positions below the diagonal that elimination would otherwise set to zero.

At step $k$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{ik}&\leftarrow\frac{A_{ik}}{A_{kk}},
&&i=k+1,\ldots,m,\\
A_{ij}&\leftarrow A_{ij}-A_{ik}A_{kj},
&&i,j=k+1,\ldots,m.
\end{aligned}
$$

In the second line, $A_{ik}$ is already the stored multiplier. The result overwrites $A$ with both factors:

$$
A_{ij}^{\text{stored}}
=\begin{cases}
L_{ij},&i>j,\\
U_{ij},&i\le j.
\end{cases}
\qquad L_{ii}=1.
$$

The diagonal of $L$ does not need to be stored.

### 3.6.1 Algorithm: LU Factorization without Pivoting

The following is a Julia-style transcription of the algorithm on the sheet. The input array is assumed to be square and to have a floating-point element type. A zero-pivot check makes the algorithm's required assumption explicit.[^lu-code]

```julia
m = size(A, 1)

for k in 1:m
    if iszero(A[k, k])
        error("Zero pivot in LU without pivoting.")
    end

    for i in (k + 1):m
        A[i, k] /= A[k, k]
    end

    for j in (k + 1):m
        for i in (k + 1):m
            A[i, j] -= A[i, k] * A[k, j]
        end
    end
end
```

The trailing update uses $i$ as the inner-loop index, consistent with the column-oriented memory traversal discussed in Lecture 02.

## 3.7 Zero Pivots

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 7. -->

Does every invertible matrix admit the preceding LU factorization without pivoting? No. Consider

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The matrix is invertible, but the first pivot is $A_{11}=0$, so the algorithm immediately attempts division by zero.

> [!theorem] Nonzero pivots and leading submatrices
> Suppose elimination without pivoting has completed its first $k-1$ steps with nonzero pivots. If the $k$-th pivot is zero, then the leading principal submatrix $A[1\!:\!k,1\!:\!k]$ is singular.
>
> Consequently, elimination has nonzero pivots throughout if and only if every leading principal submatrix is nonsingular.

### 3.7.1 Proof

The triangular structure gives the leading-block identity

$$
\begin{aligned}
A[1\!:\!k,1\!:\!k]
&=L[1\!:\!k,:]\,U[:,1\!:\!k]\\
&=\underbrace{L[1\!:\!k,1\!:\!k]}_{\text{unit lower triangular}}
  \underbrace{U[1\!:\!k,1\!:\!k]}_{\text{upper triangular}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The leading block of $L$ is nonsingular because its diagonal entries are one. The leading block of $U$ is nonsingular exactly when its first $k$ diagonal entries—the pivots—are nonzero.

The same leading-block relation is available at step $k$ before dividing by the $k$-th pivot. Given nonzero earlier pivots, a zero $k$-th pivot makes that leading block singular. Conversely, nonsingularity of each successive leading block prevents a zero pivot and allows elimination to continue.

## 3.8 Small Pivots Can Also Be Dangerous

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 7. -->

Avoiding an exactly zero pivot is not enough to ensure an accurate computation. The lecture perturbs the preceding example to

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad 0<\epsilon\ll1.
$$

In this example, $\epsilon$ is a small matrix entry. In the next section, the lecture reuses $\epsilon$ for machine precision.

The exact factors are

$$
L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\\epsilon^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
U=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&1\\0&1-\epsilon^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Their exact product is[^small-pivot-sign]

$$
\begin{aligned}
LU
&=\begin{pmatrix}
\epsilon&1\\
1&\underbrace{\epsilon^{-1}+(1-\epsilon^{-1})}_{1}
\end{pmatrix}\\
&=A.
\end{aligned}
$$

However, if $\epsilon^{-1}$ is so large that the $1$ is lost when evaluating $1-\epsilon^{-1}$, while the intermediate numbers remain representable, then

$$
\operatorname{fl}(1-\epsilon^{-1})\approx-\epsilon^{-1}.
$$

The cancellation in the reconstructed product can then give

$$
LU\ \text{computed in floating point}
\ \approx\
\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
$$

rather than $\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. The arrow toward $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ in the sheet is a schematic illustration of this floating-point failure for small $\epsilon$, not the exact-arithmetic limit of $LU$.

The small pivot creates large multipliers and large intermediate entries whose cancellation can lose the desired result.

## 3.9 Error Analysis: The Main Estimate

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 8. -->

The lecture ends by stating an error estimate for the computed LU factors. Here $\epsilon$ denotes machine precision, and absolute values of matrices are taken entrywise.

> [!theorem] Componentwise LU error estimate — first-order form
> Suppose LU factorization terminates without a zero pivot and the floating-point operations remain in the relative-error regime. For the computed factors $L$ and $U$, write
> $$
> A=LU+E.
> $$
> To first order in machine precision, the lecture's estimate is
> $$
> \boxed{|E|\lesssim m\epsilon\,|L|\,|U|.}
> $$

The product $|L|\,|U|$ is an ordinary matrix product of the entrywise absolute values, not the entrywise absolute value of $LU$.

The handwritten sheet writes $|E|\le m\epsilon|L||U|$. It suppresses higher-order rounding factors; the first-order interpretation is made explicit here rather than treating the stated coefficient as an exact finite-precision bound.[^roundoff-form]

Lecture 04 develops this estimate and uses it to motivate pivoting.
