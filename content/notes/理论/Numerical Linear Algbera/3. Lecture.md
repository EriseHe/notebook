## 3.2 Gaussian Elimination

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 2. -->

**Theorem 1.** Let $A\in\mathbb K^{m\times m}$ be the current matrix after the first $k-1$ elimination steps, with $A_{kk}\ne0$. Its form is

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
A_{11}&\cdots&A_{1,k-1}&A_{1k}&A_{1,k+1}&\cdots&A_{1m}\\
0&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&A_{k-1,k-1}&A_{k-1,k}&A_{k-1,k+1}&\cdots&A_{k-1,m}\\
0&\cdots&0&A_{kk}&A_{k,k+1}&\cdots&A_{km}\\
0&\cdots&0&A_{k+1,k}&A_{k+1,k+1}&\cdots&A_{k+1,m}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&A_{mk}&A_{m,k+1}&\cdots&A_{mm}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Define the elementary lower-triangular matrix

$$
L_k=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&&&&&&0\\
&\ddots&&&&&\\
&&1&&&&\\
&&&1&&&\\
&&&-\dfrac{A_{k+1,k}}{A_{kk}}&1&&\\
&&&\vdots&&\ddots&\\
0&&&-\dfrac{A_{mk}}{A_{kk}}&&&1
\end{pmatrix}
$$

The displayed column containing the negative multipliers is column $k$; all unspecified off-diagonal entries are zero. Then

$$
L_kA=
\begin{pmatrix}
A_{11}&\cdots&A_{1,k-1}&A_{1k}&A_{1,k+1}&\cdots&A_{1m}\\
0&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&A_{k-1,k-1}&A_{k-1,k}&A_{k-1,k+1}&\cdots&A_{k-1,m}\\
0&\cdots&0&A_{kk}&A_{k,k+1}&\cdots&A_{km}\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\widetilde A_{k+1,k+1}&\cdots&\widetilde A_{k+1,m}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\widetilde A_{m,k+1}&\cdots&\widetilde A_{mm}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Thus the first $k$ columns are zero below their diagonals. The row operation behind the matrix picture is

$$
\text{row}_i\leftarrow\text{row}_i-\frac{A_{ik}}{A_{kk}}\text{row}_k,
\qquad i>k
$$

In particular,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(L_kA)_{ik}&=A_{ik}-\frac{A_{ik}}{A_{kk}}A_{kk}=0\\
\widetilde A_{ij}&=A_{ij}-\frac{A_{ik}}{A_{kk}}A_{kj},
\qquad i,j>k
\end{aligned}
$$

## 3.3 LU Factorization

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 3. -->

Successive elimination gives an upper-triangular matrix

$$
U=L_m\cdots L_1A
$$

Here the last factor is trivial: $L_m=I$. Applying the same transformations to $Ax=b$ gives

$$
\underbrace{L_m\cdots L_1A}_{U}x=L_m\cdots L_1b
$$

Set

$$
L=(L_m\cdots L_1)^{-1}=L_1^{-1}\cdots L_m^{-1}
$$

Then

$$
A=LU
$$

Is $L$ lower triangular, and can we compute it efficiently?

## 3.4 Algebra of Lower-Triangular Matrices

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 4. -->

**Theorem 2.** Lower-triangular matrices in $\mathbb K^{m\times m}$ are closed under addition, scalar multiplication, and matrix multiplication. The inverse of a nonsingular lower-triangular matrix is lower triangular.

Addition and scalar multiplication preserve the zeros above the diagonal. For multiplication,

$$
(L\widetilde L)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^m L_{ik}\widetilde L_{kj}
$$

A nonzero term requires

$$
L_{ik}\widetilde L_{kj}\ne0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
i\ge k\ge j
$$

Therefore $(L\widetilde L)_{ij}=0$ when $i<j$. The inversion claim is addressed after the elementary factors.

## 3.5 Elementary Lower-Triangular Matrices

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, pp. 4–5. -->

Write $\ell_{ik}=A_{ik}/A_{kk}$ for the multipliers at step $k$.

**Theorem 3.** For an elementary lower-triangular matrix,

$$
L_k=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&&&&&&0\\
&\ddots&&&&&\\
&&1&&&&\\
&&&1&&&\\
&&&-\ell_{k+1,k}&1&&\\
&&&\vdots&&\ddots&\\
0&&&-\ell_{mk}&&&1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
L_k^{-1}=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&&&&&&0\\
&\ddots&&&&&\\
&&1&&&&\\
&&&1&&&\\
&&&\ell_{k+1,k}&1&&\\
&&&\vdots&&\ddots&\\
0&&&\ell_{mk}&&&1
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Moreover, in this order,

$$
L_1^{-1}\cdots L_m^{-1}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&\cdots&0\\
\ell_{21}&1&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\ell_{m1}&\cdots&\ell_{m,m-1}&1
\end{pmatrix}
$$

### 3.5.1 Proof of the Inverse Formula

Define

$$
\ell_k=
\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
\ell_{k+1,k}\\
\vdots\\
\ell_{mk}
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
L_k=I-\ell_ke_k^*,
\qquad e_k^*\ell_k=0
$$

The first $k$ entries of $\ell_k$ are zero, $e_k$ is the $k$-th coordinate vector, and $*$ denotes conjugate transpose. Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
(I+\ell_ke_k^*)(I-\ell_ke_k^*)
&=I+\ell_ke_k^*-\ell_ke_k^*
  -\ell_k(e_k^*\ell_k)e_k^*\\
&=I
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence $L_k^{-1}=I+\ell_ke_k^*$.

### 3.5.2 Proof of the Product Formula

The lecture computes the $(i,j)$ entry:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(L_1^{-1}\cdots L_m^{-1})_{ij}
&=e_i^*(I+\ell_1e_1^*)\cdots(I+\ell_me_m^*)e_j\\
&=e_i^*(I+\ell_1e_1^*)\cdots
  (I+\ell_{j-1}e_{j-1}^*)(e_j+\ell_j)\\
&=e_i^*(I+\ell_1e_1^*)\cdots
  (I+\ell_{j-1}e_{j-1}^*)\ell_j+e_i^*e_j\\
&=e_i^*\ell_j+e_i^*e_j
\end{aligned}
$$

Factors with index greater than $j$ leave $e_j$ unchanged. Factors with index less than $j$ leave both $e_j$ and $\ell_j$ unchanged. The product therefore has ones on the diagonal and the multipliers below it.

### 3.5.3 Inversion of a Lower-Triangular Matrix

For the elimination product, Theorem 3 gives

$$
(L_m\cdots L_1)^{-1}=L_1^{-1}\cdots L_m^{-1}
$$

Each factor is lower triangular, so their product is lower triangular by Theorem 2.

For a general nonsingular lower-triangular matrix $L$, solve $Lx=e_j$ by forward substitution. Its first $j-1$ equations give

$$
x_1=\cdots=x_{j-1}=0
$$

Since $x$ is column $j$ of $L^{-1}$, the inverse has zeros above its diagonal.

## 3.6 Computing LU in Place

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 6. -->

Transform $A$ into $U$ while storing the multipliers in the entries that elimination would otherwise set to zero. The final array has the form

$$
A_{\mathrm{stored}}=
\begin{pmatrix}
U_{11}&U_{12}&\cdots&U_{1m}\\
\ell_{21}&U_{22}&\cdots&U_{2m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\ell_{m1}&\cdots&\ell_{m,m-1}&U_{mm}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

The diagonal of $L$ consists of ones and does not need to be stored.

### 3.6.1 Algorithm: LU Factorization without Pivoting

The lecture's algorithm, in Julia notation, assumes the required pivots are nonzero:

```julia
m = size(A, 1)

for k in 1:m
    A[(k + 1):m, k] /= A[k, k]

    for j in (k + 1):m
        for i in (k + 1):m
            A[i, j] -= A[i, k] * A[k, j]
        end
    end
end
```

The outer-product update uses the multipliers already stored in column $k$. The inner loop runs down a column, as in the preceding lecture's memory discussion.

## 3.7 Zero Pivots

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 7. -->

Does every invertible $A$ have an LU factorization without pivoting? No:

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}
$$

This matrix is invertible, but its first pivot is zero.

**Theorem.** If the first $k-1$ pivots are nonzero and the $k$-th pivot is zero, then $A[1\!:\!k,1\!:\!k]$ is singular. Elimination has nonzero pivots throughout if and only if all leading principal submatrices are nonsingular.

*Proof.* The triangular structure gives the leading-block relation

$$
\begin{aligned}
A[1\!:\!k,1\!:\!k]
&=L[1\!:\!k,:]\,U[:,1\!:\!k]\\
&=\underbrace{L[1\!:\!k,1\!:\!k]}_{\text{unit lower triangular}}
  \underbrace{U[1\!:\!k,1\!:\!k]}_{\text{upper triangular}}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first factor is nonsingular. The second is nonsingular precisely when its first $k$ pivots are nonzero. At step $k$, this relation already holds for the leading blocks before division by the current pivot, so a zero pivot makes the leading block singular. Conversely, nonsingularity of each leading block allows the next elimination step.

## 3.8 Small Pivots

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 7. -->

Avoiding zero pivots is not sufficient. Consider

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad 0<\epsilon\ll1
$$

Its exact factors are

$$
L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\\epsilon^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
U=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&1\\0&1-\epsilon^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
$$

In exact arithmetic,

$$
LU=
\begin{pmatrix}
\epsilon&1\\
1&\epsilon^{-1}+(1-\epsilon^{-1})
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}
$$

If floating-point arithmetic loses the $1$ in $1-\epsilon^{-1}$, the computed factors instead have the product

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\\epsilon^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&1\\0&-\epsilon^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
\approx
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

The small pivot produces large entries whose cancellation loses the desired result.

## 3.9 Error Analysis for LU Factorization

<!-- Source: Lecture 03.pdf, p. 8. -->

**Theorem — first-order form.** Let $\epsilon$ now denote machine precision. Suppose LU factorization terminates without a zero pivot and the operations satisfy the relative rounding-error model. For the computed factors,

$$
A=LU+E,
\qquad
|E|\lesssim m\epsilon\,|L|\,|U|
$$

Absolute values and the inequality are entrywise; $|L|\,|U|$ is an ordinary matrix product. Lecture 4 develops the proof.

## Corrections and Clarifications to the Source

- **Page 3:** Restored the missing $x$ in the transformed equation for $Ax=b$.
- **Page 5:** The handwritten inversion argument establishes the claim for products of elementary factors. The short forward-substitution argument completes the stated claim for any nonsingular lower-triangular matrix.
- **Page 7:** Corrected the lower-right product entry to $\epsilon^{-1}+(1-\epsilon^{-1})$. The source's arrow to $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ illustrates loss of accuracy in floating-point arithmetic; it is not the exact-arithmetic limit of $LU$.
- **Page 8:** The source writes $\le m\epsilon|L||U|$ while suppressing higher-order rounding factors. Here $\lesssim$ marks that first-order convention; overflow and underflow outside the relative-error model are not included.
