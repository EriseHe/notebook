Schäfer, Fall 2026

Deadline: Oct. 05 Monday, 4:55 pm

1 One-upping [20 pts]

Let \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\) have full rank. Assume that we have already computed the QR decomposition of \(\boldsymbol{A}\). For \(\boldsymbol{u}, \boldsymbol{v} \in \mathbb{R}^m\), we call the matrix \(\boldsymbol{B} = \boldsymbol{A} + \boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\) a rank-1 update of \(\boldsymbol{A}\).

1.1.1 (a) [5 pts]

Prove that if \(\boldsymbol{v}^{T} \boldsymbol{A}^{-1} \boldsymbol{u} \neq - 1\), then \(\boldsymbol{B}\) is invertible.

Proof. Set \(\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}\) and \(\alpha=\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{z}\ne-1\). If \(\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}\), then \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{x})&=\boldsymbol{0}\\ \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}&=-\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{x})\\ \boldsymbol{x}&=-\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{x}) =-\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{x})\\ \boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{x}&=-(\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{z})(\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{x}) =-\alpha(\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{x})\\ (1+\alpha)\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{x}&=0 \end{aligned} \] Thus \(\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{x}=0\), hence \(\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}\). Therefore the square matrix \(\boldsymbol{B}\) is invertible. This uses the nullspace characterization in Lecture 2, §2.5.

\(\square\)

1.1.2 (b) [7.5 pts]

Design an algorithm that provably solves the system of equations \(\boldsymbol{B} \boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{b}\) in \(O(m^2)\) operations.

Solution. Under the condition in (a), use the given factorization \(\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}\). The QR solve is Lecture 5, §5.6.2, with back substitution from Lecture 2, §2.6.2. Apply \(\boldsymbol{Q}^T\) and use backward substitution to compute \(\boldsymbol{y}\) and \(\boldsymbol{z}\), then set \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{y}&=\boldsymbol{Q}^T\boldsymbol{b}, &\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{z}&=\boldsymbol{Q}^T\boldsymbol{u}\\ c=\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{x} &=\boldsymbol{v}^T(\boldsymbol{y}-c\boldsymbol{z}) =\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{y}-c\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{z}\\ c(1+\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{z})&=\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{y}\\ c&=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{y}}{1+\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{z}}, &\boldsymbol{x}&=\boldsymbol{y}-c\boldsymbol{z} \end{aligned} \] Here we seek \(\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{y}-c\boldsymbol{z}\) and choose \(c=\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{x}\); part (a) makes its denominator nonzero. Since \(\boldsymbol{Q}\) is square orthogonal, \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{y} &=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{y} =\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}^T\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{b},\\ \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z} &=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{z} =\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}^T\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}. \end{aligned} \] Since \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{b}\) and \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{u}\), \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x} &=(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}^T)(\boldsymbol{y}-c\boldsymbol{z})\\ &=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{y}-c\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z} +\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{y} -c\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{z}\\ &=\boldsymbol{b}-c\boldsymbol{u} +\boldsymbol{u}\bigl(\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{y}-c\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{z}\bigr)\\ &=\boldsymbol{b}+\boldsymbol{u}\underbrace{ \bigl(\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{y}-c(1+\boldsymbol{v}^T\boldsymbol{z})\bigr)}_{0} =\boldsymbol{b} \end{aligned} \] Since each application of \(\boldsymbol{Q}^T\) and each triangular solve costs \(O(m^2)\), and the dot products and final update cost \(O(m)\), the total is \(O(m^2)\). Explicitly, a dense matrix-vector product costs \(m(2m-1)\) operations, and one triangular solve costs \[ \sum_{i=1}^m[2(m-i)+1]=2\frac{m(m-1)}2+m=m^2, \] under the separate multiply/add convention of Lecture 2, §2.3.

\(\square\)

1.1.3 (c) r-upping [7.5 pts]

Extend the algorithm from the previous exercise to the case of \(\boldsymbol{B} = \boldsymbol{A} + \boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\), for \(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}\) and \(r \ll m\). Calculate the asymptotic complexity of the resulting algorithm.

Solution. Compute \(\boldsymbol{y}\) and the columns of \(\boldsymbol{Z}\) using the existing QR factors (Lecture 5, §5.6.2): \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{y}&=\boldsymbol{Q}^T\boldsymbol{b}, &\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{Z}&=\boldsymbol{Q}^T\boldsymbol{U}\\ \boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{x} &=\boldsymbol{V}^T(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{t}) =\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{t}\\ \boldsymbol{S}&=\mathbf{I}_r+\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{Z}, &\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{t}&=\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{y}\\ \boldsymbol{x}&=\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{t} \end{aligned} \] Assume \(\boldsymbol{S}\) is nonsingular, the rank-\(r\) analogue of the condition in (a). It is necessary when \(\boldsymbol{B}\) is invertible: if \(\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{t}=0\), then \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{t} &=\boldsymbol{U}(\mathbf I_r+\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{Z})\boldsymbol{t} =\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{t}=0,\\ \boldsymbol{B}\text{ invertible} &\Longrightarrow\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{t}=0\\ &\Longrightarrow\boldsymbol{t} =\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{t}=0. \end{aligned} \] Thus \(\boldsymbol{S}\) has zero nullspace and is invertible by Lecture 2, §2.5. Since \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{b}\) and \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{U}\), \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x} &=(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{V}^T)(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{t})\\ &=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{t} +\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{y} -\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{t}\\ &=\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{t} +\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{V}^T(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{t})\\ &=\boldsymbol{b}+\boldsymbol{U}\underbrace{ \bigl(\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{t}\bigr)}_{\boldsymbol{0}} =\boldsymbol{b} \end{aligned} \] The costs are \[ \begin{aligned} &\underbrace{O(m^2(r+1))}_{\text{$r+1$ QR solves}} +\underbrace{O(mr^2)}_{\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{Z}} +\underbrace{O(r^3)}_{\text{solve in }\boldsymbol{S}} +\underbrace{O(mr)}_{\text{remaining products}}\\ &\hspace{2em}=O(m^2r+mr^2+r^3)=O(m^2r) \end{aligned} \] for \(1\le r\ll m\). Here \(mr^2\le m^2r\) and \(r^3\le m^2r\) because \(r\le m\). The counts follow Lecture 2, §2.3: \(r+1\) QR solves, \(r^2\) length-\(m\) dot products, one \(r\times r\) solve, and vector products.

\(\square\)

2 You Factor [30 pts]

For \(\varepsilon\) the unit roundoff error and \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}, \tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\) the LU factors computed in finite precision, and \(|\cdot|\) the element-wise absolute value, we have derived in class that, for all \(1 \leq i,j \leq m\), \[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{L}} \tilde{\boldsymbol{U}} = \boldsymbol{A} + \boldsymbol{E}, \quad \text{with} \quad \left|\boldsymbol{E}\right|_{ij} \leq m \varepsilon \left(\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}} \right| \left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\right|\right)_{ij} \tag{1} \] But this result is only true up to terms quadratic in \(m \varepsilon\). In this problem, we remove this caveat and draw its conclusions for row-pivoted LU factorization.

1.2.1 (a) [7.5 pts]

Assuming \(m \varepsilon < 1\) and defining the quantity \(\gamma_m = \frac{m \varepsilon}{1 - m \varepsilon}\), prove that for \(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m\), we have \[ \mathrm{float.}\left( \sum \limits_{i = 1}^{m} x_i y_i \right) = \sum \limits_{i = 1}^{m} x_i y_i (1 + \delta_i), \quad \text{with} \quad |\delta_i| \leq \gamma_m \tag{2} \] if the operations on the left side are performed in floating point arithmetic with unit roundoff \(\varepsilon\) and no overflow or underflow occurs.

Proof. Writing \(\operatorname{fl}\) for \(\mathrm{float.}\), we use \(\operatorname{fl}(a\mathbin{\circ}b)=(a\mathbin{\circ}b)(1+\eta)\), \(|\eta|\le\varepsilon\), for the operations in question (Lecture 4, §4.2). Here \(\varepsilon\) is unit roundoff; the spacing parameter in Lecture 1, §1.4.1 is twice this value for binary round-to-nearest. For \(p\varepsilon<1\), \(|\eta_t|\le\varepsilon\), and \(q_t\in\{-1,1\}\), each factor \((1+\eta_t)^{q_t}\) lies between \(1-\varepsilon\) and \((1-\varepsilon)^{-1}\). Hence Bernoulli’s inequality gives \[ \begin{aligned} (1-\varepsilon)^0&=1,\\ (1-\varepsilon)^q\ge1-q\varepsilon &\Longrightarrow(1-\varepsilon)^{q+1} \ge(1-q\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon)\\ &=1-(q+1)\varepsilon+q\varepsilon^2 \ge1-(q+1)\varepsilon,\\ 1-p\varepsilon &\le(1-\varepsilon)^p \le\prod_{t=1}^{p}(1+\eta_t)^{q_t} \le(1-\varepsilon)^{-p} \le\frac{1}{1-p\varepsilon}\\ \left|\prod_{t=1}^{p}(1+\eta_t)^{q_t}-1\right| &\le\frac{p\varepsilon}{1-p\varepsilon}=\gamma_p \end{aligned} \] For ordinary sequential accumulation, let \[ \begin{aligned} p_i&=\operatorname{fl}(x_i y_i)=x_i y_i(1+\alpha_i), &&|\alpha_i|\le\varepsilon\\ s_1&=p_1\\ s_k&=\operatorname{fl}(s_{k-1}+p_k) =(s_{k-1}+p_k)(1+\beta_k), &&|\beta_k|\le\varepsilon\quad(k\ge2) \end{aligned} \] Expanding the recurrence, \[ \begin{aligned} s_2&=(p_1+p_2)(1+\beta_2),\\ s_3&=p_1(1+\beta_2)(1+\beta_3) +p_2(1+\beta_2)(1+\beta_3)+p_3(1+\beta_3),\\ s_m &=x_1y_1(1+\alpha_1)\prod_{k=2}^{m}(1+\beta_k) +\sum_{i=2}^{m}x_i y_i(1+\alpha_i)\prod_{k=i}^{m}(1+\beta_k)\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_i y_i(1+\delta_i) \end{aligned} \] The coefficients are \[ \begin{aligned} 1+\delta_1&=(1+\alpha_1)\prod_{k=2}^m(1+\beta_k),\\ 1+\delta_i&=(1+\alpha_i)\prod_{k=i}^m(1+\beta_k),\qquad i\ge2. \end{aligned} \] Their factor counts are \(1+(m-1)=m\) and \(1+(m-i+1)\le m\). For \(0\le p\le m\), \[ \gamma_m-\gamma_p =\frac{(m-p)\varepsilon}{(1-m\varepsilon)(1-p\varepsilon)}\ge0. \] Each coefficient contains at most \(m\) rounding factors, so the product bound gives \(|\delta_i|\le\gamma_m\). Empty products equal \(1\), covering \(m=1\). Lecture 4, §4.2.2 gives the first-order estimate; this product calculation proves the exact bound.

\(\square\)

1.2.2 (b) [7.5 pts]

Assume that the entries of the computed factors are obtained by first evaluating the sum, then subtracting it from the corresponding entry of \(\boldsymbol{A}\), and finally dividing by the pivot, i.e. \[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{jk} = \mathrm{float.}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{jk} - \mathrm{float.}\left(\sum \limits_{i = 1}^{j - 1} \tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{ji} \tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{ik}\right)\right) \quad \text{for } j \leq k \tag{3} \]

\[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{jk} = \mathrm{float.}\left(\mathrm{float.}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{jk} - \mathrm{float.}\left(\sum \limits_{i = 1}^{k - 1} \tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{ji} \tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{ik}\right)\right) \Big/ \tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{kk}\right) \quad \text{for } j > k \tag{4} \] Use the previous result to show that \[ \left|\boldsymbol{E}\right|_{ij} \leq \gamma_m \left(\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}} \right| \left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\right|\right)_{ij} \tag{5} \]

Proof. Set \(\gamma_0=0\) and \(\boldsymbol{E}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}-\boldsymbol{A}\). Use the entry relations in Lecture 4, §4.2.1 and rounding rearrangements in §4.2.3. Assume \(m\varepsilon<1\), nonzero pivots, no overflow or underflow, and the stated order of operations. Fix \(j,k\) and abbreviate \(t_i=\tilde{L}_{ji}\tilde{U}_{ik}\). For \(j\le k\), part (a) and the subtraction give \[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{U}_{jk} &=\left(A_{jk}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}t_i(1+\delta_i)\right)(1+\alpha)\\ \frac{t_j}{1+\alpha} &=A_{jk}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}t_i(1+\delta_i)\\ A_{jk} &=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}t_i(1+\delta_i)+t_j(1+\theta)\\ E_{jk}&=\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_i-A_{jk}\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_i -\left[\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}t_i+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}t_i\delta_i+t_j+t_j\theta\right]\\ &=-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}t_i\delta_i-t_j\theta \end{aligned} \] Here \(|\delta_i|\le\gamma_{j-1}\), \(|\alpha|\le\varepsilon\), and \(t_j=\tilde{U}_{jk}\) because \(\tilde{L}_{jj}=1\). We have \(1+\theta=(1+\alpha)^{-1}\), so \(|\theta|\le\gamma_1\) by (a). For \(j>k\), include the rounding of the final division: \[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{L}_{jk} &=\frac{A_{jk}-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}t_i(1+\delta_i)} {\tilde{U}_{kk}}(1+\alpha)(1+\beta)\\ \frac{t_k}{(1+\alpha)(1+\beta)} &=A_{jk}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}t_i(1+\delta_i)\\ A_{jk} &=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}t_i(1+\delta_i)+t_k(1+\theta)\\ E_{jk}&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_i-A_{jk}\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_i -\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}t_i+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}t_i\delta_i+t_k+t_k\theta\right]\\ &=-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}t_i\delta_i-t_k\theta \end{aligned} \] where \[ |\delta_i|\le\gamma_{k-1},\qquad |\alpha|,|\beta|\le\varepsilon,\qquad 1+\theta=\frac{1}{(1+\alpha)(1+\beta)} \] The reciprocal-product bound from (a) gives \(|\theta|\le\gamma_2\le\gamma_m\); this case has \(m\ge2\). In both cases, triangularity and monotonicity of \(\gamma_p\) give the exact bound \[ \begin{aligned} |E_{jk}| &\le\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}|t_i||\delta_i|+|t_h||\theta|, \qquad h=\min(j,k)\\ &\le\gamma_m\left(\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}|t_i|+|t_h|\right)\\ &\le\gamma_m\sum_{i=1}^{\min(j,k)} |\tilde{L}_{ji}|\,|\tilde{U}_{ik}|\\ &=\gamma_m\bigl(|\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}|\,|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}|\bigr)_{jk} \end{aligned} \] Terms beyond \(h\) vanish by triangularity. Empty sums are zero; \(m=1\) uses only the \(j\le k\) case. The lecture uses the opposite error sign, with the same absolute bound. The division case is Lecture 4, §4.2.4.

\(\square\)

1.2.3 (c) [5 pts]

Deduce that under row-pivoted \(LU\) factorization and taking \(\| \cdot \|_{\infty}\) to signify the vector-infinity norm, we have \[ \left\|\boldsymbol{E}\right\|_{\infty} \leq m \gamma_{m} \left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\right\|_{\infty} \tag{6} \] This prompts us to investigate the growth factor \(\rho \coloneqq \frac{\left\| \boldsymbol{U} \right\|_{\infty}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\right\|_{\infty}}\) of row-pivoted LU factorization. After justification, you may use that row-pivoted LU factorization reduces to unpivoted LU factorization on a permuted matrix \(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{A}\), where \(\boldsymbol{P}\) is a permutation matrix.

Proof. Use the entrywise norm \(\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_\infty=\max_{i,j}|M_{ij}|\). This treats the entries as a vector, not the induced maximum-row-sum norm. Row pivoting and commuting permutations are Lecture 4, §4.4 and §4.5.

A later row swap \(\boldsymbol{T}_\ell\) can be moved past an earlier elementary elimination matrix \(\boldsymbol{F}_k\) by \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{T}_\ell\boldsymbol{F}_k &=\boldsymbol{T}_\ell\boldsymbol{F}_k (\boldsymbol{T}_\ell\boldsymbol{T}_\ell)\\ &=(\boldsymbol{T}_\ell\boldsymbol{F}_k\boldsymbol{T}_\ell)\boldsymbol{T}_\ell, \qquad\boldsymbol{T}_\ell^2=\mathbf I,\quad\ell>k. \end{aligned} \] Writing \(\boldsymbol{F}_k=\mathbf I-\boldsymbol{\ell}_k\boldsymbol{e}_k^T\), the later swap fixes \(\boldsymbol{e}_k\), so \[ \boldsymbol{T}_\ell\boldsymbol{F}_k\boldsymbol{T}_\ell =\mathbf I-(\boldsymbol{T}_\ell\boldsymbol{\ell}_k)\boldsymbol{e}_k^T. \] The conjugation only reorders the multipliers below the \(k\)th diagonal entry. Repeating this operation gives an unpivoted factorization of \(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{A}\), where \(\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{T}_m\cdots\boldsymbol{T}_1\). Thus (b) applies with \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}=\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}\).

Row pivoting selects a largest-magnitude entry in the active column, so every multiplier has magnitude at most \(1\), including after rounding since \(\pm1\) are representable. This rounded claim assumes correctly rounded arithmetic; the relative-error inequality alone only gives \(1+\varepsilon\). Thus \(|\tilde{L}_{ik}|\le1\), and (b) gives \[ \begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{E}\|_\infty &\le\gamma_m\max_{i,j}\sum_{k=1}^{m} |\tilde{L}_{ik}|\,|\tilde{U}_{kj}|\\ &\le\gamma_m\max_{i,j}\sum_{k=1}^m 1\cdot\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\|_\infty\\ &\le m\gamma_m\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\|_\infty \end{aligned} \]

\(\square\)

1.2.4 (d) [5 pts]

Verify that the rows \(\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{T}, \boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{T}\) of \(\boldsymbol{U}, \boldsymbol{A}\) satisfy \[ \boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{T} = \boldsymbol{a}_i^T - \sum \limits_{j = 1}^{i - 1} \boldsymbol{L}_{ij} \boldsymbol{u}_{j}^T \tag{7} \]

Proof. For \(\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{U}\), \(L_{ii}=1\) and \(L_{ij}=0\) for \(j>i\) give (Lecture 4, §4.2.1) \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{a}_i^T &=\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{ij}\boldsymbol{u}_j^T\\ &=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}L_{ij}\boldsymbol{u}_j^T +L_{ii}\boldsymbol{u}_i^T +\underbrace{\sum_{j=i+1}^{m}L_{ij}\boldsymbol{u}_j^T}_{0}\\ &=\boldsymbol{u}_i^T+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}L_{ij}\boldsymbol{u}_j^T\\ \boldsymbol{u}_i^T &=\boldsymbol{a}_i^T-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}L_{ij}\boldsymbol{u}_j^T \end{aligned} \] Under row pivoting, replace \(\boldsymbol{a}_i^T\) by \(\boldsymbol{a}_{\pi(i)}^T\), the \(i\)th row of \(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{A}\) (Lecture 4, §4.5). This identity is for exact factors; the computed version also includes the row of \(\boldsymbol{E}\) because \(\tilde{\boldsymbol L}\tilde{\boldsymbol U}=\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}\).

\(\square\)

1.2.5 (e) [5 pts]

Use part (d) to show that under row pivoting, \(\left\|\boldsymbol{U}\right\|_{\infty} \leq 2^{m - 1} \left\| \boldsymbol{A}\right\|_{\infty}\). Give an example of a family of matrices \(\boldsymbol{A}^{(m)} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\) that saturates this bound (up to constant factors).

Proof. Set \(a=\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_\infty\). By (d), row pivoting, and induction (Lecture 4, §4.4), \[ \begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{u}_1\|_\infty&\le a\\ \|\boldsymbol{u}_i\|_\infty &\le\|\boldsymbol{a}_{\pi(i)}\|_\infty +\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}|L_{ij}|\,\|\boldsymbol{u}_j\|_\infty\\ &\le a+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}2^{j-1}a\\ &=a+a\frac{2^{i-1}-1}{2-1} =2^{i-1}a\\ \|\boldsymbol{U}\|_\infty &=\max_i\|\boldsymbol{u}_i\|_\infty \le2^{m-1}\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_\infty \end{aligned} \] For a family attaining the bound, take the example from Lecture 5, §5.1.1: \[ A^{(m)}_{ij}= \begin{cases} 1,&i=j\text{ or }j=m\\ -1,&i>j,\ j<m\\ 0,&\text{otherwise} \end{cases} \] Retain the current pivot on ties. The first \(m-1\) columns remain unchanged during earlier eliminations because each pivot row is zero in later columns before the last; their active pivot candidates remain \(1\) and \(-1\). No swaps occur; \(L_{ij}=-1\) for \(i>j\), and the first \(m-1\) columns of \(\boldsymbol{U}\) equal those of \(\mathbf{I}\). The last column satisfies \[ \begin{aligned} U_{1m}&=1\\ U_{im}&=A_{im}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}L_{ij}U_{jm} =1+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}U_{jm}\\ &=1+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}2^{j-1} =1+\frac{2^{i-1}-1}{2-1}=2^{i-1}\\ \|\boldsymbol{A}^{(m)}\|_\infty&=1, \qquad\|\boldsymbol{U}\|_\infty=2^{m-1}, \qquad\rho=2^{m-1} \end{aligned} \] Thus the bound is attained exactly. This is exact-arithmetic growth, not a claim that the example must incur large rounding error; its powers of two are representable until overflow, as noted in the cited lecture.

\(\square\)

3 Diagonal Dominance [25 pts]

Suppose that \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{K}^{m \times m}\) is strictly column diagonally dominant, meaning that for all \(1 \leq k \leq m\), \[ \left|\boldsymbol{A}_{kk}\right| > \sum \limits_{j \neq k} \left|\boldsymbol{A}_{jk}\right| \tag{8} \] Show that if LU factorization with row pivoting is applied to \(\boldsymbol{A}\), no row interchange takes place.

Proof. Let \(\boldsymbol{C}\) be a strictly column diagonally dominant active matrix. The elimination update is Lecture 2, §2.7.2, and the pivot rule is Lecture 4, §4.4. In its first column, \[ |C_{11}|>\sum_{i>1}|C_{i1}|\ge|C_{j1}|\qquad(j>1) \] Hence the first pivot is nonzero and uniquely largest in magnitude. The remaining matrix is the Schur complement \[ S_{ij}=C_{ij}-\frac{C_{i1}C_{1j}}{C_{11}},\qquad i,j\ge2 \] For each remaining column \(j\), the triangle inequalities give \[ \begin{aligned} |S_{jj}| &\ge |C_{jj}|-\frac{|C_{j1}||C_{1j}|}{|C_{11}|},\\ \sum_{\substack{i\ge2\\i\ne j}}|S_{ij}| &\le\sum_{\substack{i\ge2\\i\ne j}}|C_{ij}| +\frac{|C_{1j}|}{|C_{11}|}\sum_{\substack{i\ge2\\i\ne j}}|C_{i1}|,\\ |S_{jj}|-\sum_{\substack{i\ge2\\i\ne j}}|S_{ij}| &\ge|C_{jj}|-\sum_{\substack{i\ge2\\i\ne j}}|C_{ij}| -\frac{|C_{1j}|}{|C_{11}|}\sum_{i\ge2}|C_{i1}|\\ &=|C_{jj}|-\left(\sum_{i\ne j}|C_{ij}|-|C_{1j}|\right) -\frac{|C_{1j}|}{|C_{11}|}\sum_{i\ge2}|C_{i1}|\\ &=\underbrace{|C_{jj}|-\sum_{i\ne j}|C_{ij}|}_{>0} +|C_{1j}|\underbrace{ \left(1-\frac{\sum_{i\ge2}|C_{i1}|}{|C_{11}|}\right)}_{>0} >0 \end{aligned} \] Thus the Schur complement is again strictly column diagonally dominant. Starting with \(\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{A}\) and repeating proves that every diagonal pivot is chosen without a row interchange. The first term is strictly positive and the second is nonnegative, including when \(C_{1j}=0\). This is an exact-arithmetic statement; rounding can erase a sufficiently small dominance margin.

\(\square\)
