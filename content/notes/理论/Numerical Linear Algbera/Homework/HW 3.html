1 Schur Complements I [25 pts]

Consider the matrix

\[ \mathbf{M} \coloneqq \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B}\\ \mathbf{C} & \mathbf{D} \end{pmatrix}, \tag{1} \]

where \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{K}^{m \times m}\) and \(\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}\) are square matrices and \(\mathbf{A}\) is invertible. The matrix \(\mathbf{M}/\mathbf{A} \coloneqq \mathbf{D} - \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B}\) is called the Schur complement of \(\mathbf{A}\) in \(\mathbf{M}\).

Relate the Schur complement \(\mathbf{M} / \mathbf{A}\) to the block LU factorization of \(\mathbf{M}\).

1.1.2 (b) [5 pts]

Show that the determinant of \(\mathbf{M}\) is given by \(\det\left(\mathbf{A}\right) \det\left(\mathbf{M} / \mathbf{A}\right)\).

1.1.2.1 Solution to 1(b)

1.1.3 (c) [7.5 pts]

Let \(\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{U}\) be the LU factorization of \(\mathbf{M}\). Split its factors into block matrices

\[ \mathbf{L} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{L}_{1, 1} & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{L}_{2, 1} & \mathbf{L}_{2, 2} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{U} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{U}_{1, 1} & \mathbf{U}_{1, 2} \\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{U}_{2, 2} \end{pmatrix} \tag{2} \]

according to the same blocking as \(\mathbf{M}\). Show that \(\mathbf{M} / \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L}_{2, 2} \mathbf{U}_{2, 2}\).

1.1.3.1 Solution to 1(c)

Multiplying the blocks of \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{U}\) and comparing with \(\mathbf{M}\) gives \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}&=\mathbf{L}_{1,1}\mathbf{U}_{1,1}\\ \mathbf{B}&=\mathbf{L}_{1,1}\mathbf{U}_{1,2}\\ \mathbf{C}&=\mathbf{L}_{2,1}\mathbf{U}_{1,1}\\ \mathbf{D}&=\mathbf{L}_{2,1}\mathbf{U}_{1,2} +\mathbf{L}_{2,2}\mathbf{U}_{2,2} \end{aligned} \]

Since we know that \(\mathbf{A}\) is invertible, its square factors \(\mathbf{L}_{1,1}\) and \(\mathbf{U}_{1,1}\) are also invertible. Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B} &=(\mathbf{L}_{2,1}\mathbf{U}_{1,1}) (\mathbf{U}_{1,1}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{1,1}^{-1}) (\mathbf{L}_{1,1}\mathbf{U}_{1,2})\\ &=\mathbf{L}_{2,1}\mathbf{U}_{1,2} \end{aligned} \]

Subtracting this from \(\mathbf{D}\) give us that \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{M}/\mathbf{A} &=\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\\ &=\mathbf{L}_{2,1}\mathbf{U}_{1,2} +\mathbf{L}_{2,2}\mathbf{U}_{2,2} -\mathbf{L}_{2,1}\mathbf{U}_{1,2}\\ &=\boxed{\mathbf{L}_{2,2}\mathbf{U}_{2,2}} \end{aligned} \]

1.1.4 (d) [7.5 pts]

Now consider the \(3 \times 3\) block matrix

\[ \mathbf{N} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{N}_{1, 1} & \mathbf{N}_{1, 2} & \mathbf{N}_{1, 3} \\ \mathbf{N}_{2, 1} & \mathbf{N}_{2, 2} & \mathbf{N}_{2, 3} \\ \mathbf{N}_{3, 1} & \mathbf{N}_{3, 2} & \mathbf{N}_{3, 3} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{3} \]

Prove the Quotient Property of Schur complements

\[ \left(\mathbf{N} / \mathbf{N}_{1, 1}\right) / \left(\mathbf{N} / \mathbf{N}_{1, 1}\right)_{1, 1} = \mathbf{N} \Big / \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{N}_{1, 1} & \mathbf{N}_{1, 2} \\ \mathbf{N}_{2, 1} & \mathbf{N}_{2, 2} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{4} \]

Here, \(\left(\mathbf{N} / \mathbf{N}_{1, 1}\right)_{1, 1}\) is the top left block of \(\mathbf{N} / \mathbf{N}_{1, 1}\), and the blocks \(\mathbf{N}_{1,1}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{N}_{1, 1} & \mathbf{N}_{1, 2} \\ \mathbf{N}_{2, 1} & \mathbf{N}_{2, 2} \end{pmatrix}\) are assumed to be invertible.

1.1.4.1 Solution to 1(d)

Write

\[ \mathbf{S}\coloneqq\mathbf{N}/\mathbf{N}_{1,1} =\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{S}_{1,1}&\mathbf{S}_{1,2}\\ \mathbf{S}_{2,1}&\mathbf{S}_{2,2} \end{pmatrix} \]

where the four blocks are

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{S}_{1,1} &=\mathbf{N}_{2,2}-\mathbf{N}_{2,1}\mathbf{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\mathbf{N}_{1,2}\\ \mathbf{S}_{1,2} &=\mathbf{N}_{2,3}-\mathbf{N}_{2,1}\mathbf{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\mathbf{N}_{1,3}\\ \mathbf{S}_{2,1} &=\mathbf{N}_{3,2}-\mathbf{N}_{3,1}\mathbf{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\mathbf{N}_{1,2}\\ \mathbf{S}_{2,2} &=\mathbf{N}_{3,3}-\mathbf{N}_{3,1}\mathbf{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\mathbf{N}_{1,3} \end{aligned} \]

Let \(\mathbf{K}\) denote the leading two-by-two block matrix on the right-hand side of (4). By part (b),

\[ \det(\mathbf{K}) =\det(\mathbf{N}_{1,1})\det(\mathbf{S}_{1,1}) \]

The two assumed inverses therefore imply that \(\mathbf{S}_{1,1}\) is invertible.

To evaluate the Schur complement with respect to \(\mathbf{K}\), solve

\[ \mathbf{K} \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}\\\mathbf{Y}\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{N}_{1,3}\\\mathbf{N}_{2,3}\end{pmatrix} \]

Elimination in this block system gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{X} &=\mathbf{N}_{1,1}^{-1} (\mathbf{N}_{1,3}-\mathbf{N}_{1,2}\mathbf{Y})\\ \mathbf{S}_{1,1}\mathbf{Y}&=\mathbf{S}_{1,2}\\ \mathbf{Y}&=\mathbf{S}_{1,1}^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{1,2} \end{aligned} \]

Substitute these expressions into \(\mathbf{N}/\mathbf{K}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{N}/\mathbf{K} &=\mathbf{N}_{3,3} -\mathbf{N}_{3,1}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{N}_{3,2}\mathbf{Y}\\ &=\mathbf{N}_{3,3} -\mathbf{N}_{3,1}\mathbf{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\mathbf{N}_{1,3}\\ &\quad-\bigl(\mathbf{N}_{3,2} -\mathbf{N}_{3,1}\mathbf{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\mathbf{N}_{1,2}\bigr) \mathbf{S}_{1,1}^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{1,2}\\ &=\mathbf{S}_{2,2} -\mathbf{S}_{2,1}\mathbf{S}_{1,1}^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{1,2}\\ &=\mathbf{S}/\mathbf{S}_{1,1} \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{N}/\mathbf{N}_{1,1}\), this is precisely the quotient property (4): successive elimination and elimination of the combined block give the same Schur complement.

2 Schur Complements II [25 pts + 10 bonus]

Let \(\mathbf{M}\) be as in the previous problem.

1.2.1 (a) [5 pts]

Divide the inverse of \(\mathbf{M}\) into blocks (of the same size as those of \(\mathbf{M}\)) as

\[ \mathbf{M}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\alpha} & \boldsymbol{\beta} \\ \boldsymbol{\gamma} & \boldsymbol{\delta} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{5} \]

Show that the Schur complement \(\mathbf{M} / \mathbf{A}\) is the inverse of \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\).

1.2.1.1 Solution to 2(a)

Write \(\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{M}/\mathbf{A}\). Since \(\mathbf{M}\) and \(\mathbf{A}\) are invertible, Question 1(b) implies that \(\mathbf{S}\) is invertible. Invert the block LU factors from Question 1(a) in reverse order:

\[ \mathbf{M}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{A}^{-1}&-\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}^{-1}\\ \mathbf{0}&\mathbf{S}^{-1} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{I}_m&\mathbf{0}\\ -\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}&\mathbf{I}_n \end{pmatrix} \]

The lower-right block of this product is \(\mathbf{S}^{-1}\). Comparing with (5),

\[ \boldsymbol{\delta}=\mathbf{S}^{-1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\mathbf{M}/\mathbf{A}=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{-1}} \]

1.2.2 (b) [5 pts]

For \(\mathbf{M}\) symmetric positive definite (symmetric meaning Hermitian in the complex case), show that if \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(\mathbf{M} / \mathbf{A}\), then \(\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathbf{M} \right) \leq \lambda \leq \lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbf{M}\right)\).

1.2.2.1 Solution to 2(b)

Because \(\mathbf{M}\) is Hermitian positive definite, so is \(\mathbf{M}^{-1}\). Its lower-right principal block \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) is positive definite, since for \(\boldsymbol{y}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\),

\[ \boldsymbol{y}^*\boldsymbol{\delta}\boldsymbol{y} = \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}^* \mathbf{M}^{-1} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}>0 \]

Thus \(\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{M}/\mathbf{A}=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{-1}\) is also Hermitian positive definite.

The eigendecomposition used in Lecture 8 gives, for every vector \(\boldsymbol{z}\),

\[ \frac{\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_2^2}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{M})} \le \boldsymbol{z}^*\mathbf{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{z} \le \frac{\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_2^2}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M})} \]

Indeed, in an orthonormal eigenbasis, the quadratic form is a sum of squared coordinate magnitudes weighted by the eigenvalues \(1/\lambda_j(\mathbf{M})\).

Now take an eigenvector \(\boldsymbol{y}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\) with \(\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{y}=\lambda\boldsymbol{y}\). Then \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\boldsymbol{y}=\lambda^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\). Applying the preceding bound to \(\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y})^T\) yields

\[ \frac{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_2^2}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{M})} \le \frac{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_2^2}{\lambda} \le \frac{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_2^2}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M})} \]

Divide by \(\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_2^2\) and take reciprocals, reversing the inequalities:

\[ \boxed{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M}) \le\lambda\le\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{M})} \]

1.2.3 (c) [7.5 pts]

Show that the size of the pivots occurring in the Cholesky or LU factorization of a s.p.d. matrix \(\mathbf{M}\) is lower bounded by \(\left\|\mathbf{M}^{-1}\right\|^{-1}\), where \(\|\cdot\|\) denotes the spectral norm.

1.2.3.1 Solution to 2(c)

As in Lecture 9, a pivot is the diagonal entry of the active matrix before taking a square root. After eliminating the first \(k-1\) variables, the active matrix is

\[ \mathbf{S}^{(k-1)} =\mathbf{M}/\mathbf{M}_{1:k-1,\,1:k-1} \qquad (k\ge2) \]

The next pivot is \(p_k=(\mathbf{S}^{(k-1)})_{1,1}\). The leading principal block is positive definite, so this Schur complement exists. By part (b) and the quadratic-form bound,

\[ \begin{aligned} p_k &=\boldsymbol{e}_1^*\mathbf{S}^{(k-1)}\boldsymbol{e}_1\\ &\ge\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{S}^{(k-1)})\\ &\ge\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M}) \end{aligned} \]

For the first pivot, the same bound follows directly from \(p_1=\boldsymbol{e}_1^*\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{e}_1\). Since \(\mathbf{M}\) is positive definite,

\[ \|\mathbf{M}^{-1}\|_2 =\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M})} \]

Therefore every elimination pivot satisfies

\[ \boxed{p_k\ge\|\mathbf{M}^{-1}\|_2^{-1}} \]

In unit-lower-triangular LU, \(p_k=U_{k,k}\). In Cholesky, \(p_k=L_{k,k}^2\), so the factor’s positive diagonal entries satisfy \(L_{k,k}\ge\|\mathbf{M}^{-1}\|_2^{-1/2}\).

1.2.4 (d) [7.5 pts]

For \(\mathbf{M}\) s.p.d, show that

\[ \boldsymbol{y}^* \left(\mathbf{M} / \mathbf{A}\right) \boldsymbol{y} = \min \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{K}^{m}} \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{x}\\ \boldsymbol{y} \end{pmatrix}^* \mathbf{M} \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{x}\\ \boldsymbol{y} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{6} \]

1.2.4.1 Solution to 2(d)

Hermitian symmetry gives \(\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{B}^*\) and \((\mathbf{A}^{-1})^*=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\). Expand and complete the square:

\[ \begin{aligned} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}^* \mathbf{M} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix} &=\boldsymbol{x}^*\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x} +\boldsymbol{x}^*\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{y} +\boldsymbol{y}^*\mathbf{B}^*\boldsymbol{x} +\boldsymbol{y}^*\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{y}\\ &=(\boldsymbol{x}+\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{y})^* \mathbf{A} (\boldsymbol{x}+\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{y})\\ &\quad+\boldsymbol{y}^* (\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{B}^*\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}) \boldsymbol{y} \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\mathbf{A}\) is positive definite, the first term is nonnegative and vanishes exactly at

\[ \boldsymbol{x}=-\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{y} \]

The second term is \(\boldsymbol{y}^*(\mathbf{M}/\mathbf{A})\boldsymbol{y}\). Consequently,

\[ \boxed{ \min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{K}^m} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}^* \mathbf{M} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix} =\boldsymbol{y}^*(\mathbf{M}/\mathbf{A})\boldsymbol{y} } \]

1.2.5 (e) [10 bonus pts]

By replacing the \(\min\) with a \(\min \max\) over suitable variables, derive a version of the results of (d) that is valid for general \(\mathbf{M}\).

1.2.5.1 Solution to 2(e)

Introduce an independent vector \(\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{K}^m\) in the left argument and define

\[ \begin{aligned} F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}) &=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{z}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}^* \mathbf{M} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}\\ &=\boldsymbol{z}^*(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{y}) +\boldsymbol{y}^*(\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{x}+\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{y}) \end{aligned} \]

First let \(\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}\). For fixed \(\boldsymbol{x}\), write \(\boldsymbol{r}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{y}\). If \(\boldsymbol{r}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\), choosing \(\boldsymbol{z}=t\boldsymbol{r}\) and letting \(t\to+\infty\) makes \(F\) tend to \(+\infty\). If \(\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{0}\), the expression is independent of \(\boldsymbol{z}\). Thus

\[ \sup_{\boldsymbol{z}}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}) =\begin{cases} \boldsymbol{y}^*(\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{x}+\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{y}) &\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}\\ +\infty&\text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Since \(\mathbf{A}\) is invertible, the only finite case is \(\boldsymbol{x}=-\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{y}\). Substitution gives the general real-matrix version:

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{y}^*(\mathbf{M}/\mathbf{A})\boldsymbol{y} =\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^m} \sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^m} F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}) } \]

The supremum allows the value \(+\infty\) away from the constraint; at the minimizing \(\boldsymbol{x}\), every \(\boldsymbol{z}\) attains the inner maximum. Neither symmetry nor positive definiteness is required.

For a general complex matrix, the scalar on the left may be complex, so apply minimization and maximization to its real and imaginary parts:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Re}\!\left(\boldsymbol{y}^*(\mathbf{M}/\mathbf{A})\boldsymbol{y}\right) &=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^m} \sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^m} \operatorname{Re}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\\ \operatorname{Im}\!\left(\boldsymbol{y}^*(\mathbf{M}/\mathbf{A})\boldsymbol{y}\right) &=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^m} \sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^m} \operatorname{Im}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}) \end{aligned} \]

The same proof uses \(\boldsymbol{z}=t\boldsymbol{r}\) for the real part and \(\boldsymbol{z}=-it\boldsymbol{r}\) for the imaginary part. For Hermitian \(\mathbf{M}\), the Schur-complement quadratic form is real, so the first identity alone gives its value.

3 Cholesky and QR [25 pts]

1.3.1 (a) [5 pts]

Assume that the invertible matrix \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{K}^{m \times m}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{U}\). Derive a way to write \(\mathbf{A}\) as the sum of rank-one matrices in outer product form.

1.3.1.1 Solution to 3(a)

Insert \(\mathbf{I}=\sum_{k=1}^m\boldsymbol{e}_k\boldsymbol{e}_k^*\) between the factors:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} &=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{U}\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^m (\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{e}_k)(\boldsymbol{e}_k^*\mathbf{U})\\ &=\boxed{\sum_{k=1}^m\mathbf{L}_{:,k}\mathbf{U}_{k,:}} \end{aligned} \]

Each summand is the outer product of column \(k\) of \(\mathbf{L}\) and row \(k\) of \(\mathbf{U}\). Equivalently, set \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_k=\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{e}_k\) and \(\boldsymbol{u}_k=\mathbf{U}^*\boldsymbol{e}_k\) to obtain \(\mathbf{A}=\sum_k\boldsymbol{\ell}_k\boldsymbol{u}_k^*\). Both factors are invertible, so these vectors are nonzero and every summand has rank one.

1.3.2 (b) [5 pts]

Show that the LU factorization of an invertible matrix \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{K}^{m \times m}\), with \(\mathbf{L}\) unit lower triangular, is unique (when it exists).

1.3.2.1 Solution to 3(b)

Suppose there are two such factorizations:

\[ \mathbf{A}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{U} =\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}} \]

All factors are invertible because \(\mathbf{A}\) is invertible. Rearranging gives

\[ \mathbf{L}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}} =\mathbf{U}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{-1} \]

The left-hand side is unit lower triangular; the right-hand side is upper triangular. Their common value is therefore diagonal, and its diagonal entries are all \(1\). Hence

\[ \mathbf{L}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{I} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{L} \qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}=\mathbf{U}} \]

1.3.3 (c) [5 pts]

Assume that we factor \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{K}^{m \times m}\) as \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{L}^{*}\) for lower triangular \(\mathbf{L}\). Up to which choices is this factorization unique? Prove your results (as always) and make sure to treat the case \(\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}\).

1.3.3.1 Solution to 3(c)

Nonsingular case. If \(\mathbf{A}\) is invertible, then every \(L_{k,k}\ne0\). Define

\[ \mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(L_{1,1},\ldots,L_{m,m}) \]

Normalize the lower factor to obtain a unit-lower-triangular LU factorization:

\[ \mathbf{A} =\underbrace{(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{D}^{-1})}_{\text{unit lower triangular}} \underbrace{(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{L}^*)}_{\text{upper triangular}} \]

By part (b), these two normalized factors are unique. Moreover,

\[ (\mathbf{D}\mathbf{L}^*)_{k,k}=|L_{k,k}|^2 \]

so the magnitudes of the diagonal entries of \(\mathbf{L}\) are fixed.

For another factor \(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}\), let \(\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}\) contain its diagonal entries. Uniqueness of the normalized lower factor gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{L}\mathbf{D}^{-1} &=\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1}\\ \widetilde{\mathbf{L}} &=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}\\ &=\mathbf{L}\operatorname{diag}(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_m) \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\omega_k=\widetilde L_{k,k}/L_{k,k}\) and \(|\omega_k|=1\). Conversely, multiplying columns by such scalars preserves the factorization, since

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{W}&=\operatorname{diag}(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_m)\\ (\mathbf{L}\mathbf{W})(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{W})^* &=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^*\mathbf{L}^* =\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^* \end{aligned} \]

Thus the factor is unique up to independent column signs over \(\mathbb{R}\) and independent column phases \(\omega_k=e^{i\theta_k}\) over \(\mathbb{C}\). Requiring every \(L_{k,k}\) to be positive real removes this freedom and gives the unique Cholesky factor.

If \(\mathbf{A}\) is singular, uniqueness up to column phases need not hold. For example,

\[ \mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{L}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \qquad \widetilde{\mathbf{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix} \]

Both lower-triangular factors satisfy \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^*=\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}^*=\mathbf{A}\), but column phases cannot move a nonzero entry from one column to another. The sign/phase uniqueness conclusion therefore requires nonsingularity.

1.3.4 (d) [5 pts]

Using these results, prove and explain the relationship between the Cholesky factor of \(\mathbf{B}^* \mathbf{B}\) and the QR factorization of \(\mathbf{B}\), for \(\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{K}^{m \times n}\) with \(m \geq n\) and full column rank.

1.3.4.1 Solution to 3(d)

Since \(\mathbf{B}\) has full column rank, \(\mathbf{B}^*\mathbf{B}\) is positive definite:

\[ \boldsymbol{x}^*\mathbf{B}^*\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{x} =\|\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2>0 \qquad(\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}) \]

Take the reduced QR factorization \(\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}\) with \(\mathbf{Q}^*\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}_n\) and positive real diagonal entries in \(\mathbf{R}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{B}^*\mathbf{B} &=(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R})^*(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R})\\ &=\mathbf{R}^*\mathbf{Q}^*\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}\\ &=\mathbf{R}^*\mathbf{R} \end{aligned} \]

The matrix \(\mathbf{R}^*\) is lower triangular with positive real diagonal. By part (c), it is the unique lower Cholesky factor:

\[ \boxed{\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{R}^*} \]

Conversely, given \(\mathbf{B}^*\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^*\), set \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{L}^*\) and \(\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\). Then \(\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}\) and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{Q}^*\mathbf{Q} &=(\mathbf{R}^{-1})^*\mathbf{B}^*\mathbf{B}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\\ &=(\mathbf{R}^*)^{-1}\mathbf{R}^*\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\\ &=\mathbf{I}_n \end{aligned} \]

Thus the upper Cholesky factor of the Gram matrix is exactly the triangular factor in reduced QR, with the same positive-diagonal convention.

1.3.5 (e) [5 pts]

For \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{B}^* \mathbf{B}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) as in (d), write

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{A}_{1,1}& \mathbf{A}_{1,2} \\ \mathbf{A}_{2,1}& \mathbf{A}_{2,2} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{B}_{1} & \mathbf{B}_{2} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{7} \]

Relate the Schur complement \(\mathbf{A}_{2,2} - \mathbf{A}_{2,1} \left(\mathbf{A}_{1, 1}\right)^{-1} \mathbf{A}_{1,2}\) to the Gram-matrix of the columns of \(\mathbf{B}_2\), projected onto the orthogonal complement of the column space of \(\mathbf{B}_1\). Use this result to refine your comment in (d) on the relationship of Cholesky and QR factorization.

1.3.5.1 Solution to 3(e)

The blocks of the Gram matrix are

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}_{1,1}&=\mathbf{B}_1^*\mathbf{B}_1 &\mathbf{A}_{1,2}&=\mathbf{B}_1^*\mathbf{B}_2\\ \mathbf{A}_{2,1}&=\mathbf{B}_2^*\mathbf{B}_1 &\mathbf{A}_{2,2}&=\mathbf{B}_2^*\mathbf{B}_2 \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\mathbf{B}_1\) has full column rank, the orthogonal projector onto its column space is

\[ \mathbf{P}_1 =\mathbf{B}_1(\mathbf{B}_1^*\mathbf{B}_1)^{-1}\mathbf{B}_1^* \qquad \mathbf{P}_1^*=\mathbf{P}_1 \qquad \mathbf{P}_1^2=\mathbf{P}_1 \]

Let \(\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_2=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_1)\mathbf{B}_2\) be the projected columns. Since \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_1\) is also Hermitian and idempotent,

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\mathbf{B}}_2^*\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_2 &=\mathbf{B}_2^*(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_1)^*(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_1)\mathbf{B}_2\\ &=\mathbf{B}_2^*(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_1)\mathbf{B}_2\\ &=\mathbf{B}_2^*\mathbf{B}_2 -\mathbf{B}_2^*\mathbf{B}_1 (\mathbf{B}_1^*\mathbf{B}_1)^{-1}\mathbf{B}_1^*\mathbf{B}_2\\ &=\boxed{\mathbf{A}_{2,2} -\mathbf{A}_{2,1}\mathbf{A}_{1,1}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{1,2}} \end{aligned} \]

Thus the Schur complement is the Gram matrix of the columns of \(\mathbf{B}_2\) after removing their projections onto \(\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{B}_1)\).

To connect this with part (d), partition the reduced QR factorization in the same way:

\[ \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}_1&\mathbf{B}_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_1&\mathbf{Q}_2\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{R}_{1,1}&\mathbf{R}_{1,2}\\ \mathbf{0}&\mathbf{R}_{2,2} \end{pmatrix} \]

The block equations give

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{B}_1&=\mathbf{Q}_1\mathbf{R}_{1,1}\\ \mathbf{B}_2&=\mathbf{Q}_1\mathbf{R}_{1,2} +\mathbf{Q}_2\mathbf{R}_{2,2} \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\mathbf{P}_1=\mathbf{Q}_1\mathbf{Q}_1^*\) and \(\mathbf{Q}_1^*\mathbf{Q}_2=\mathbf{0}\). Hence

\[ \widehat{\mathbf{B}}_2 =(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}_1\mathbf{Q}_1^*)\mathbf{B}_2 =\mathbf{Q}_2\mathbf{R}_{2,2} \]

Taking its Gram matrix yields

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{A}/\mathbf{A}_{1,1} =\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_2^*\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_2 =\mathbf{R}_{2,2}^*\mathbf{R}_{2,2} } \]

Therefore \(\mathbf{L}_{2,2}=\mathbf{R}_{2,2}^*\) under the positive-diagonal convention. Cholesky’s trailing Schur-complement update corresponds exactly to Gram–Schmidt’s projection step: the updated matrix stores the inner products of the remaining orthogonalized columns.
