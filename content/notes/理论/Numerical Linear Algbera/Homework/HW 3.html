1 Schur Complements I [25 pts]

Consider the matrix \[ \boldsymbol{M} \coloneqq \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{A} & \boldsymbol{B}\\ \boldsymbol{C} & \boldsymbol{D} \end{pmatrix}, \tag{1} \] where \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{K}^{m \times m}\) and \(\boldsymbol{D} \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}\) are square matrices and \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is invertible. The matrix \(\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A} \coloneqq \boldsymbol{D} - \boldsymbol{C} \boldsymbol{A}^{-1} \boldsymbol{B}\) is called the Schur complement of \(\boldsymbol{A}\) in \(\boldsymbol{M}\).

1.1.1 (a) [5 pts]

Relate the Schur complement \(\boldsymbol{M} / \boldsymbol{A}\) to the block LU factorization of \(\boldsymbol{M}\).

1.1.1.1 Solution to 1(a)

We eliminate the lower-left block using the block row operation: \[ \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{I}_m&\boldsymbol{0}\\ -\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}&\boldsymbol{I}_n \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{A}&\boldsymbol{B}\\ \boldsymbol{C}&\boldsymbol{D} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{A}&\boldsymbol{B}\\ \boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B} \end{pmatrix} \] then, invert the elimination matrix gives the block LU factorization \[ \boldsymbol{M}= \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{I}_m&\boldsymbol{0}\\ \boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}&\boldsymbol{I}_n \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{A}&\boldsymbol{B}\\ \boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A} \end{pmatrix} \]

thus, the \(\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A}\) is the trailing block of the block upper-triangular factor after eliminating \(\boldsymbol{C}\).

Derivation details — added study explanation.

The block-LU construction is in Lecture 9 §9.9, following block elimination in §9.8. Set \(S=D-CA^{-1}B\). The four blocks of the product are

\[ \begin{aligned} (EM)_{11}&=I_mA+0C=A,\\ (EM)_{12}&=I_mB+0D=B,\\ (EM)_{21}&=(-CA^{-1})A+I_nC=-C+C=0,\\ (EM)_{22}&=(-CA^{-1})B+I_nD=S. \end{aligned} \]

To invert \(E\), check both products:

\[ \begin{aligned} E&=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\-CA^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}, &F&=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\CA^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix},\\ EF&=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\-CA^{-1}+CA^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}=I, &FE&=I,\\ M&=E^{-1}(EM) =\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\CA^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}A&B\\0&S\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

Only \(A\) must be invertible; \(M\) and \(S\) may be singular here.

1.1.2 (b) [5 pts]

Show that the determinant of \(\boldsymbol{M}\) is given by \(\det\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) \det\left(\boldsymbol{M} / \boldsymbol{A}\right)\).

1.1.2.1 Solution to 1(b)

We take determinants in the factorization from part (a), and since we know that the unit block lower-triangular factor has determinant \(1\), and the determinant of a block triangular matrix is the product of the determinants of its diagonal blocks, then

\[ \begin{aligned} \det(\boldsymbol{M}) &=\det(\boldsymbol{I}_m)\det(\boldsymbol{I}_n) \det(\boldsymbol{A})\det(\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A})\\ &=\det(\boldsymbol{A})\det(\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A}) \end{aligned} \]

Derivation details — added study explanation.

The determinant calculation for triangular LU factors appears in Lecture 3 §3.7; the block factors are from Lecture 9 §9.9. Write the factors in 1(a) as \(\mathcal L,\mathcal U\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \det M&=\det(\mathcal L\mathcal U) =\det\mathcal L\,\det\mathcal U,\\ \det\mathcal L&=\prod_{i=1}^{m+n}\mathcal L_{ii}=1,\\ \det\mathcal U&=\det\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\0&S\end{pmatrix} =\det A\,\det S,\\ \det M&=1\cdot\det A\,\det S. \end{aligned} \]

For the block determinant step, a nonzero term in the determinant’s permutation expansion must assign the last \(n\) rows to the last \(n\) columns: entries in their first \(m\) columns are zero. The first \(m\) rows then use the first \(m\) columns. The two permutation sums are exactly \(\det A\) and \(\det S\); the block \(B\) contributes no term. This also covers singular \(S\).

1.1.3 (c) [7.5 pts]

Let \(\boldsymbol{M} = \boldsymbol{L} \boldsymbol{U}\) be the LU factorization of \(\boldsymbol{M}\). Split its factors into block matrices \[ \boldsymbol{L} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{L}_{1, 1} & \boldsymbol{0} \\ \boldsymbol{L}_{2, 1} & \boldsymbol{L}_{2, 2} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{U} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{U}_{1, 1} & \boldsymbol{U}_{1, 2} \\ \boldsymbol{0} & \boldsymbol{U}_{2, 2} \end{pmatrix} \tag{2} \] according to the same blocking as \(\boldsymbol{M}\). Show that \(\boldsymbol{M} / \boldsymbol{A} = \boldsymbol{L}_{2, 2} \boldsymbol{U}_{2, 2}\).

1.1.3.1 Solution to 1(c)

Multiplying the blocks of \(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{U}\) and compare with \(\boldsymbol{M}\) provides \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{A}&=\boldsymbol{L}_{1,1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1,1}\\ \boldsymbol{B}&=\boldsymbol{L}_{1,1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1,2}\\ \boldsymbol{C}&=\boldsymbol{L}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1,1}\\ \boldsymbol{D}&=\boldsymbol{L}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1,2} +\boldsymbol{L}_{2,2}\boldsymbol{U}_{2,2} \end{aligned} \]

Since we know that \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is invertible, its square factors \(\boldsymbol{L}_{1,1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{U}_{1,1}\) are also invertible. Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B} &=(\boldsymbol{L}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1,1}) (\boldsymbol{U}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}_{1,1}^{-1}) (\boldsymbol{L}_{1,1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1,2})\\ &=\boldsymbol{L}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1,2} \end{aligned} \]

finally, substracting this from \(\boldsymbol{D}\) give us that \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A} &=\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}\\ &=\boldsymbol{L}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1,2} +\boldsymbol{L}_{2,2}\boldsymbol{U}_{2,2} -\boldsymbol{L}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1,2}\\ &=\boldsymbol{L}_{2,2}\boldsymbol{U}_{2,2} \end{aligned} \]

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Use Lecture 9 §9.8’s block multiplication with general LU factors. From \(A=L_{11}U_{11}\) and \(\det A\ne0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} 0\ne\det A&=\det L_{11}\det U_{11},\\ A^{-1}&=U_{11}^{-1}L_{11}^{-1},\\ A(U_{11}^{-1}L_{11}^{-1}) &=L_{11}(U_{11}U_{11}^{-1})L_{11}^{-1}=I. \end{aligned} \]

Substitute the four block equations, retaining the order of multiplication:

\[ \begin{aligned} CA^{-1}B &=L_{21}U_{11}U_{11}^{-1}L_{11}^{-1}L_{11}U_{12}\\ &=L_{21}(U_{11}U_{11}^{-1})(L_{11}^{-1}L_{11})U_{12}\\ &=L_{21}U_{12},\\ M/A&=D-CA^{-1}B\\ &=(L_{21}U_{12}+L_{22}U_{22})-L_{21}U_{12}\\ &=L_{22}U_{22}. \end{aligned} \]

No inverse of \(L_{22}\) or \(U_{22}\) is used.

1.1.4 (d) [7.5 pts]

Now consider the \(3 \times 3\) block matrix \[ \boldsymbol{N} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{N}_{1, 1} & \boldsymbol{N}_{1, 2} & \boldsymbol{N}_{1, 3} \\ \boldsymbol{N}_{2, 1} & \boldsymbol{N}_{2, 2} & \boldsymbol{N}_{2, 3} \\ \boldsymbol{N}_{3, 1} & \boldsymbol{N}_{3, 2} & \boldsymbol{N}_{3, 3} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{3} \] Prove the Quotient Property of Schur complements \[ \left(\boldsymbol{N} / \boldsymbol{N}_{1, 1}\right) / \left(\boldsymbol{N} / \boldsymbol{N}_{1, 1}\right)_{1, 1} = \boldsymbol{N} \Big / \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{N}_{1, 1} & \boldsymbol{N}_{1, 2} \\ \boldsymbol{N}_{2, 1} & \boldsymbol{N}_{2, 2} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{4} \] Here, \(\left(\boldsymbol{N} / \boldsymbol{N}_{1, 1}\right)_{1, 1}\) is the top left block of \(\boldsymbol{N} / \boldsymbol{N}_{1, 1}\), and the blocks \(\boldsymbol{N}_{1,1}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{N}_{1, 1} & \boldsymbol{N}_{1, 2} \\ \boldsymbol{N}_{2, 1} & \boldsymbol{N}_{2, 2} \end{pmatrix}\) are assumed to be invertible.

1.1.4.1 Solution to 1(d)

We write

\[ \boldsymbol{S}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{N}/\boldsymbol{N}_{1,1} =\begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{S}_{1,1}&\boldsymbol{S}_{1,2}\\ \boldsymbol{S}_{2,1}&\boldsymbol{S}_{2,2} \end{pmatrix} \]

where the four blocks are

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{S}_{1,1} &=\boldsymbol{N}_{2,2}-\boldsymbol{N}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,2}\\ \boldsymbol{S}_{1,2} &=\boldsymbol{N}_{2,3}-\boldsymbol{N}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,3}\\ \boldsymbol{S}_{2,1} &=\boldsymbol{N}_{3,2}-\boldsymbol{N}_{3,1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,2}\\ \boldsymbol{S}_{2,2} &=\boldsymbol{N}_{3,3}-\boldsymbol{N}_{3,1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,3} \end{aligned} \]

Let \(\boldsymbol{K}\) denote the leading 2 by 2 block matrix on the righthand side of (4). By part (b), we have

\[ \det(\boldsymbol{K}) =\det(\boldsymbol{N}_{1,1})\det(\boldsymbol{S}_{1,1}) \]

and we know thus \(\boldsymbol{S}_{1,1}\) is also invertible. To evaluate the Schur complement, solve

\[ \boldsymbol{K} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{X}\\\boldsymbol{Y}\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,3}\\\boldsymbol{N}_{2,3}\end{pmatrix} \]

Elimination in this block system gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{X} &=\boldsymbol{N}_{1,1}^{-1} (\boldsymbol{N}_{1,3}-\boldsymbol{N}_{1,2}\boldsymbol{Y})\\ \boldsymbol{S}_{1,1}\boldsymbol{Y}&=\boldsymbol{S}_{1,2}\\ \boldsymbol{Y}&=\boldsymbol{S}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S}_{1,2} \end{aligned} \]

Substitute these expressions into \(\boldsymbol{N}/\boldsymbol{K}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{N}/\boldsymbol{K} &=\boldsymbol{N}_{3,3} -\boldsymbol{N}_{3,1}\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{N}_{3,2}\boldsymbol{Y}\\ &=\boldsymbol{N}_{3,3} -\boldsymbol{N}_{3,1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,3}\\ &\quad-\bigl(\boldsymbol{N}_{3,2} -\boldsymbol{N}_{3,1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{N}_{1,2}\bigr) \boldsymbol{S}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S}_{1,2}\\ &=\boldsymbol{S}_{2,2} -\boldsymbol{S}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{S}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S}_{1,2}\\ &=\boldsymbol{S}/\boldsymbol{S}_{1,1} \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{N}/\boldsymbol{N}_{1,1}\), this is then precisely the quotient property (4).

Derivation details — added study explanation.

This quotient identity is a homework derivation from the Schur-complement definition in Lecture 9 §9.8, not an additional cited lecture theorem. Use \(S_{ij}\) and \(K\) defined above. Part 1(b) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \det K&=\det N_{11}\det S_{11},\\ \det K\ne0,\quad\det N_{11}\ne0 &\Longrightarrow\det S_{11}\ne0. \end{aligned} \]

Expand the block system for \(K^{-1}(N_{13},N_{23})^T\):

\[ \begin{aligned} N_{11}X+N_{12}Y&=N_{13},\\ N_{21}X+N_{22}Y&=N_{23},\\ X&=N_{11}^{-1}N_{13}-N_{11}^{-1}N_{12}Y,\\ N_{21}N_{11}^{-1}N_{13} -N_{21}N_{11}^{-1}N_{12}Y+N_{22}Y&=N_{23},\\ (N_{22}-N_{21}N_{11}^{-1}N_{12})Y &=N_{23}-N_{21}N_{11}^{-1}N_{13},\\ S_{11}Y&=S_{12},\qquad Y=S_{11}^{-1}S_{12}. \end{aligned} \]

Now eliminate \(X\) before grouping the remaining terms:

\[ \begin{aligned} N/K&=N_{33}-(N_{31},N_{32})K^{-1} \begin{pmatrix}N_{13}\\N_{23}\end{pmatrix}\\ &=N_{33}-N_{31}X-N_{32}Y\\ &=N_{33}-N_{31}N_{11}^{-1}N_{13} +N_{31}N_{11}^{-1}N_{12}Y-N_{32}Y\\ &=(N_{33}-N_{31}N_{11}^{-1}N_{13}) -(N_{32}-N_{31}N_{11}^{-1}N_{12})Y\\ &=S_{22}-S_{21}S_{11}^{-1}S_{12} =(N/N_{11})/S_{11}. \end{aligned} \]

2 Schur Complements II [25 pts + 10 bonus]

Let \(\boldsymbol{M}\) be as in the previous problem.

1.2.1 (a) [5 pts]

Divide the inverse of \(\boldsymbol{M}\) into blocks (of the same size as those of \(\boldsymbol{M}\)) as \[ \boldsymbol{M}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\alpha} & \boldsymbol{\beta} \\ \boldsymbol{\gamma} & \boldsymbol{\delta} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{5} \] Show that the Schur complement \(\boldsymbol{M} / \boldsymbol{A}\) is the inverse of \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\).

1.2.1.1 Solution to 2(a)

We write \(\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A}\). Since \(\boldsymbol{M}\) and \(\boldsymbol{A}\) are invertible, question 1(b) implies that \(\boldsymbol{S}\) is alsi invertible. Then, we invert the block LU factors from question 1(a) in reverse order to have

\[ \boldsymbol{M}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{A}^{-1}&-\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{S}^{-1}\\ \boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{S}^{-1} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{I}_m&\boldsymbol{0}\\ -\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}&\boldsymbol{I}_n \end{pmatrix} \]

here, the lower-right block of this product is \(\boldsymbol{S}^{-1}\). Comparing with (5), we then know

\[ \boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{S}^{-1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{-1} \]

Derivation details — added study explanation.

This is the block-inverse lemma, Lecture 9 §9.9. Here both \(M\) and \(A\) must be invertible. By 1(b), \(\det S=\det M/\det A\ne0\). For \(\mathcal U=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\0&S\end{pmatrix}\), solve \(\mathcal U\mathcal U^{-1}=I\) block by block:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal U^{-1}&=\begin{pmatrix}X&Y\\Z&W\end{pmatrix},\\ SZ=0&\Longrightarrow Z=0,\\ SW=I&\Longrightarrow W=S^{-1},\\ AX+BZ=I&\Longrightarrow X=A^{-1},\\ AY+BW=0&\Longrightarrow Y=-A^{-1}BS^{-1}. \end{aligned} \]

Multiplication by \(\mathcal L^{-1}\) now gives the whole inverse:

\[ \begin{aligned} M^{-1} &=\begin{pmatrix}A^{-1}&-A^{-1}BS^{-1}\\0&S^{-1}\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}I&0\\-CA^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}\\ &=\begin{pmatrix} A^{-1}+A^{-1}BS^{-1}CA^{-1}&-A^{-1}BS^{-1}\\ -S^{-1}CA^{-1}&S^{-1} \end{pmatrix},\\ \delta&=S^{-1}\Longrightarrow\delta^{-1}=S=M/A. \end{aligned} \]

1.2.2 (b) [5 pts]

For \(\boldsymbol{M}\) symmetric positive definite (symmetric meaning Hermitian in the complex case), show that if \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(\boldsymbol{M} / \boldsymbol{A}\), then \(\lambda_{\min}\left(\boldsymbol{M} \right) \leq \lambda \leq \lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}\right)\).

1.2.2.1 Solution to 2(b)

Because \(\boldsymbol{M}\) is Hermitian positive definite, so is \(\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\), and its \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) is positive definite, since for \(\boldsymbol{y}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\),

\[ \boldsymbol{y}^*\boldsymbol{\delta}\boldsymbol{y} = \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}^* \boldsymbol{M}^{-1} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}>0 \]

thus \(\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{-1}\) is also Hermitian p.d.. We use the eigendecomposition in Lecture 8 to have, for every vector \(\boldsymbol{z}\), Since \(Sy=\lambda y\) with \(y\neq 0\), we have \(\delta y=\lambda^{-1}y\). Taking \(z=(0,y)^T\), we obtain \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\|z\|_2^2}{\lambda_{\max}(M)} &\le z^*M^{-1}z \le \frac{\|z\|_2^2}{\lambda_{\min}(M)}\\ \frac{\|y\|_2^2}{\lambda_{\max}(M)} &\le \begin{pmatrix}0\\y\end{pmatrix}^* M^{-1} \begin{pmatrix}0\\y\end{pmatrix} = y^*\delta y = \lambda^{-1} y^* y = \frac{\|y\|_2^2}{\lambda} \le \frac{\|y\|_2^2}{\lambda_{\min}(M)}\\ \frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}(M)} &\le \frac{1}{\lambda} \le \frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(M)}\\ \lambda_{\min}(M) &\le \lambda \le \lambda_{\max}(M) \end{aligned} \]

Derivation details — added study explanation.

The Hermitian eigendecomposition is used in Lecture 8 §8.7.2; the orthonormal-eigenbasis statement is explicit in Lecture 10 §10.2.1. Write \(M=V\operatorname{diag}(\mu_i)V^*\), with \(V^*V=I\) and \(0<\mu_{\min}\le\mu_i\le\mu_{\max}\). For \(c=V^*z\),

\[ \begin{aligned} M^{-1}&=V\operatorname{diag}(\mu_i^{-1})V^*,\\ z^*M^{-1}z&=\sum_i\frac{|c_i|^2}{\mu_i}, &\|c\|_2^2&=\|z\|_2^2,\\ \frac{\|z\|_2^2}{\mu_{\max}} &\le\sum_i\frac{|c_i|^2}{\mu_i} \le\frac{\|z\|_2^2}{\mu_{\min}}. \end{aligned} \]

This also proves \(M^{-1}\) positive definite. Setting \(z=(0,y)^T\) proves \(y^*\delta y>0\) for \(y\ne0\). Apply the same eigendecomposition argument to \(\delta=W\operatorname{diag}(d_i)W^*\), where \(d_i>0\). By the block-inverse identity,

\[ \begin{aligned} S&=\delta^{-1}=W\operatorname{diag}(d_i^{-1})W^*=S^*,\\ y^*Sy&=\sum_i d_i^{-1}|w_i^*y|^2>0\quad(y\ne0). \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(S\) is Hermitian positive definite. If \(Sy=\lambda y\), then

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda&=\frac{y^*Sy}{\|y\|_2^2}>0,\\ \delta Sy&=y\Longrightarrow\lambda\delta y=y \Longrightarrow\delta y=\lambda^{-1}y,\\ \frac{\|y\|_2^2}{\mu_{\max}} &\le y^*\delta y=\lambda^{-1}\|y\|_2^2 \le\frac{\|y\|_2^2}{\mu_{\min}},\\ \frac1{\mu_{\max}}&\le\frac1\lambda\le\frac1{\mu_{\min}},\\ \mu_{\min}&\le\lambda\le\mu_{\max}. \end{aligned} \]

Division uses \(\|y\|_2^2>0\); taking reciprocals reverses each inequality because all three scalars are positive.

1.2.3 (c) [7.5 pts]

Show that the size of the pivots occurring in the Cholesky or LU factorization of a s.p.d. matrix \(\boldsymbol{M}\) is lower bounded by \(\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\right\|^{-1}\), where \(\|\cdot\|\) denotes the spectral norm.

1.2.3.1 Solution to 2(c)

After eliminating the first \(k-1\) variables, the active matrix is

\[ \boldsymbol{S}^{(k-1)} =\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{M}_{1:k-1,\,1:k-1} \qquad (k\ge2) \]

and the next pivot is \(p_k=(\boldsymbol{S}^{(k-1)})_{1,1}\). The leading principal block is p.d., so this Schur complement exists. By part (b),

\[ \begin{aligned} p_k &=\boldsymbol{e}_1^*\boldsymbol{S}^{(k-1)}\boldsymbol{e}_1\\ &\ge\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{S}^{(k-1)})\\ &\ge\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{M}) \end{aligned} \]

then for the first pivot, the same bound follows directly from \(p_1=\boldsymbol{e}_1^*\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{e}_1\). Since \(\boldsymbol{M}\) is p.d.,

\[ \|\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\|_2 =\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{M})} \]

thus every elimination pivot satisfies

\[ p_k\ge\|\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\|_2^{-1} \]

In unit lower-triangular LU, \(p_k=U_{k,k}\). In Cholesky, \(p_k=L_{k,k}^2\), so the factor’s positive diagonal entries satisfy \(L_{k,k}\ge\|\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\|_2^{-1/2}\).

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Lecture 9 §9.5 proves positive pivots by congruence; §9.5.1 distinguishes the elimination pivot from its square root. Every leading principal block \(H\) is positive definite, since

\[ \begin{aligned} x^*Hx&=\begin{pmatrix}x\\0\end{pmatrix}^* M\begin{pmatrix}x\\0\end{pmatrix}>0\quad(x\ne0). \end{aligned} \]

Thus its inverse exists. The quotient property in 1(d) identifies successive trailing matrices with \(S^{(k-1)}=M/M_{1:k-1,1:k-1}\). Apply 2(b), then expand the Rayleigh bound using the eigenbasis cited there:

\[ \begin{aligned} p_k&=e_1^*S^{(k-1)}e_1 =\sum_j\lambda_j(S^{(k-1)})|v_j^*e_1|^2\\ &\ge\lambda_{\min}(S^{(k-1)})\sum_j|v_j^*e_1|^2\\ &=\lambda_{\min}(S^{(k-1)})\|e_1\|_2^2 \ge\lambda_{\min}(M). \end{aligned} \]

The same calculation uses \(S^{(0)}=M\) for \(k=1\). For \(\|z\|_2=1\) and \(M=V\operatorname{diag}(\mu_i)V^*\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \|M^{-1}z\|_2^2 &=\sum_i\mu_i^{-2}|v_i^*z|^2 \le\mu_{\min}^{-2}\sum_i|v_i^*z|^2 =\mu_{\min}^{-2},\\ \|M^{-1}v_{\min}\|_2&=\mu_{\min}^{-1},\\ \|M^{-1}\|_2&=\mu_{\min}^{-1},\\ p_k&\ge\|M^{-1}\|_2^{-1},\qquad L_{kk}=\sqrt{p_k}\ge\|M^{-1}\|_2^{-1/2}. \end{aligned} \]

The bound \(\|M^{-1}\|_2^{-1}\) applies to the pivot before the Cholesky square root. It is not generally a bound for \(L_{kk}\) itself: \(M=4I\) gives \(p_k=4\) but \(L_{kk}=2\).

1.2.4 (d) [7.5 pts]

For \(\boldsymbol{M}\) s.p.d, show that \[ \boldsymbol{y}^* \left(\boldsymbol{M} / \boldsymbol{A}\right) \boldsymbol{y} = \min \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{K}^{m}} \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{x}\\ \boldsymbol{y} \end{pmatrix}^* \boldsymbol{M} \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{x}\\ \boldsymbol{y} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{6} \]

1.2.4.1 Solution to 2(d)

Hermitian symmetry gives \(\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{B}^*\) and \((\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})^*=\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\). Expand and complete the square:

\[ \begin{aligned} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}^* \boldsymbol{M} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix} &=\boldsymbol{x}^*\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x} +\boldsymbol{x}^*\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{y} +\boldsymbol{y}^*\boldsymbol{B}^*\boldsymbol{x} +\boldsymbol{y}^*\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{y}\\ &=(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{y})^* \boldsymbol{A} (\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{y})\\ &\quad+\boldsymbol{y}^* (\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{B}^*\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}) \boldsymbol{y} \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is positive definite, the first term is nonnegative and vanishes exactly at

\[ \boldsymbol{x}=-\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{y} \]

The second term is \(\boldsymbol{y}^*(\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{y}\). Consequently,

\[ \min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{K}^m} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}^* \boldsymbol{M} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix} =\boldsymbol{y}^*(\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{y} \]

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Use positive definiteness, Lecture 8 §8.6.2, and the Schur complement, Lecture 9 §9.8. This minimization identity is derived here. The principal block \(A\) is positive definite by the zero-padding argument in 2(c). Put \(h=A^{-1}By\). Since \(A=A^*\),

\[ \begin{aligned} (A^{-1})^*&=(A^*)^{-1}=A^{-1},\\ Ah&=By,\qquad h^*A=y^*B^*,\\ h^*Ah&=y^*B^*A^{-1}By,\\ (x+h)^*A(x+h) &=x^*Ax+x^*Ah+h^*Ax+h^*Ah\\ &=x^*Ax+x^*By+y^*B^*x+y^*B^*A^{-1}By,\\ \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}^*M\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} &=(x+h)^*A(x+h)+y^*(D-B^*A^{-1}B)y\\ &=(x+h)^*A(x+h)+y^*Sy. \end{aligned} \]

For fixed \(y\), only the first term depends on \(x\):

\[ \begin{aligned} (x+h)^*A(x+h)&\ge0,\\ (x+h)^*A(x+h)=0&\iff x+h=0\iff x=-A^{-1}By,\\ \min_x\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}^*M\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} &=0+y^*Sy. \end{aligned} \]

The minimizer is unique because \(A\) is positive definite.

1.2.5 (e) [10 bonus pts]

By replacing the \(\min\) with a \(\min \max\) over suitable variables, derive a version of the results of (d) that is valid for general \(\boldsymbol{M}\).

1.2.5.1 Solution to 2(e)

Introduce an independent vector \(\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{K}^m\) in the left argument and define

\[ \begin{aligned} F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}) &=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{z}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}^* \boldsymbol{M} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}\\ &=\boldsymbol{z}^*(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{y}) +\boldsymbol{y}^*(\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{y}) \end{aligned} \]

First let \(\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}\). For fixed \(\boldsymbol{x}\), write \(\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{y}\). If \(\boldsymbol{r}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\), choosing \(\boldsymbol{z}=t\boldsymbol{r}\) and letting \(t\to+\infty\) makes \(F\) tend to \(+\infty\). If \(\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{0}\), the expression is independent of \(\boldsymbol{z}\). Thus

\[ \sup_{\boldsymbol{z}}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}) =\begin{cases} \boldsymbol{y}^*(\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{y}) &\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}\\ +\infty&\text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Since \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is invertible, the only finite case is \(\boldsymbol{x}=-\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{y}\). Substitution gives the general real-matrix version:

\[ \boldsymbol{y}^*(\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{y} =\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^m} \sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^m} F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}) \]

The supremum allows the value \(+\infty\) away from the constraint; at the minimizing \(\boldsymbol{x}\), every \(\boldsymbol{z}\) attains the inner maximum. No symmetry or p.d. is required. For a general complex matrix, the scalar on the left may be complex, so apply minimization and maximization to its real and imaginary parts:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Re}\!\left(\boldsymbol{y}^*(\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{y}\right) &=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^m} \sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^m} \operatorname{Re}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\\ \operatorname{Im}\!\left(\boldsymbol{y}^*(\boldsymbol{M}/\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{y}\right) &=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^m} \sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^m} \operatorname{Im}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}) \end{aligned} \]

The same proof uses \(\boldsymbol{z}=t\boldsymbol{r}\) for the real part and \(\boldsymbol{z}=-it\boldsymbol{r}\) for the imaginary part. For Hermitian \(\boldsymbol{M}\), the Schur-complement quadratic form is real, so the first identity alone gives its value.

Derivation details — added study explanation.

This is a homework min/sup construction, not a theorem stated in the lectures. Its ingredients are the Schur-complement definition, Lecture 9 §9.8 and the separation of real and imaginary parts, Lecture 8 §8.3. Fix \(y\) and assume only \(A\) invertible. Write \(r=Ax+By\) and \(c=y^*(Cx+Dy)\). Over \(\mathbb R\),

\[ \begin{aligned} F(x,z)&=z^Tr+c,\\ r\ne0,\quad z=tr &\Longrightarrow F(x,tr)=t\|r\|_2^2+c\longrightarrow+\infty,\\ r=0&\Longrightarrow F(x,z)=c\quad\text{for every }z,\\ r=0&\iff Ax=-By\iff x=x_*=-A^{-1}By,\\ c(x_*)&=y^*(C(-A^{-1}By)+Dy) =y^*(D-CA^{-1}B)y,\\ \min_x\sup_zF(x,z)&=c(x_*)=y^*(M/A)y. \end{aligned} \]

For \(\mathbb C\), a complex scalar has no order suitable for a minimum. Treat its two real components separately. For \(t\in\mathbb R\),

\[ \begin{aligned} z=tr&\Longrightarrow \operatorname{Re}F(x,z)=t\|r\|_2^2+\operatorname{Re}c,\\ z=-itr&\Longrightarrow z^*=it r^*,\\ &\hphantom{\Longrightarrow} \operatorname{Im}F(x,z)=t\|r\|_2^2+\operatorname{Im}c. \end{aligned} \]

When \(r\ne0\), both suprema are \(+\infty\); when \(r=0\), each equals the corresponding component of \(c(x_*)\). This proves both displayed complex identities above. The inner operation is a supremum: away from the constraint no finite maximum exists. At \(x_*\) every \(z\) is a maximizer. No interchange of minimum and supremum is used.

3 Cholesky and QR [25 pts]

1.3.1 (a) [5 pts]

Assume that the invertible matrix \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{K}^{m \times m}\) satisfies \(\boldsymbol{A} = \boldsymbol{L} \boldsymbol{U}\). Derive a way to write \(\boldsymbol{A}\) as the sum of rank-one matrices in outer product form.

1.3.1.1 Solution to 3(a)

Insert \(\boldsymbol{I}=\sum_{k=1}^m\boldsymbol{e}_k\boldsymbol{e}_k^*\) between the factors:

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{A} &=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{U}\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^m (\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{e}_k)(\boldsymbol{e}_k^*\boldsymbol{U})\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^m\boldsymbol{L}_{:,k}\boldsymbol{U}_{k,:} \end{aligned} \]

Each summand is the outer product of column \(k\) of \(\boldsymbol{L}\) and row \(k\) of \(\boldsymbol{U}\). Equivalently, set \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_k=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{e}_k\) and \(\boldsymbol{u}_k=\boldsymbol{U}^*\boldsymbol{e}_k\) to obtain \(\boldsymbol{A}=\sum_k\boldsymbol{\ell}_k\boldsymbol{u}_k^*\). Both factors are invertible, so these vectors are nonzero and every summand has rank one.

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Use the LU product, Lecture 3 §3.3; the outer-product organization is also used in §3.6. For the standard basis, \((e_ke_k^*)_{ij}=1\) only when \(i=j=k\), so \(\sum_ke_ke_k^*=I\). Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} A&=LIU=L\left(\sum_{k=1}^me_ke_k^*\right)U\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^m(Le_k)(e_k^*U) =\sum_{k=1}^m\ell_ku_k^*,\\ \ell_k&=Le_k=L_{:,k},\qquad u_k=U^*e_k,\qquad u_k^*=e_k^*U=U_{k,:},\\ (\ell_ku_k^*)_{ij}&=L_{ik}U_{kj},\\ \left(\sum_k\ell_ku_k^*\right)_{ij} &=\sum_kL_{ik}U_{kj}=(LU)_{ij}=A_{ij}. \end{aligned} \]

Here \(L,U\) are invertible because their square product is invertible. Thus \(\ell_k,u_k\ne0\). Every output of \(\ell_ku_k^*\) is a multiple of \(\ell_k\), and

\[ (\ell_ku_k^*)u_k=\ell_k\|u_k\|_2^2\ne0. \]

Its range is therefore exactly \(\operatorname{span}(\ell_k)\), which has dimension one.

1.3.2 (b) [5 pts]

Show that the LU factorization of an invertible matrix \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{K}^{m \times m}\), with \(\boldsymbol{L}\) unit lower triangular, is unique (when it exists).

1.3.2.1 Solution to 3(b)

Suppose there are two such factorizations:

\[ \boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{U} =\widetilde{\boldsymbol{L}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}} \]

All factors are invertible because \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is invertible. Rearranging gives

\[ \boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{L}} =\boldsymbol{U}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{-1} \]

The left-hand side is unit lower triangular; the right-hand side is upper triangular. Their common value is therefore diagonal, and its diagonal entries are all \(1\). Hence

\[ \boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{L}}=\boldsymbol{I} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \widetilde{\boldsymbol{L}}=\boldsymbol{L} \qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}=\boldsymbol{U} \]

Derivation details — added study explanation.

The closure and inverse facts are Lecture 3 §3.4, Theorem 2, with the inverse proof in §3.5.3. The upper-triangular versions follow by taking adjoints. The uniqueness argument is derived here:

\[ \begin{aligned} LU&=\widetilde L\widetilde U,\\ L^{-1}(LU)\widetilde U^{-1} &=L^{-1}(\widetilde L\widetilde U)\widetilde U^{-1},\\ U\widetilde U^{-1}&=L^{-1}\widetilde L=:H. \end{aligned} \]

\(H\) is lower triangular by its right expression and upper triangular by its left expression. Therefore \(H_{ij}=0\) whenever \(i\ne j\). Its diagonal is

\[ \begin{aligned} (L^{-1})_{ii}L_{ii}&=(L^{-1}L)_{ii}=1,\\ L_{ii}=\widetilde L_{ii}=1 &\Longrightarrow H_{ii}=(L^{-1})_{ii}\widetilde L_{ii}=1,\\ H&=I,\\ L^{-1}\widetilde L=I&\Longrightarrow\widetilde L=L,\\ U\widetilde U^{-1}=I&\Longrightarrow U=\widetilde U. \end{aligned} \]

The unit-diagonal normalization is essential: otherwise a nonsingular diagonal matrix can be moved between the two factors.

1.3.3 (c) [5 pts]

Assume that we factor \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{K}^{m \times m}\) as \(\boldsymbol{A} = \boldsymbol{L} \boldsymbol{L}^{*}\) for lower triangular \(\boldsymbol{L}\). Up to which choices is this factorization unique? Prove your results (as always) and make sure to treat the case \(\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}\).

1.3.3.1 Solution to 3(c)

Nonsingular case. If \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is invertible, then every \(L_{k,k}\ne0\). Define

\[ \boldsymbol{D}=\operatorname{diag}(L_{1,1},\ldots,L_{m,m}) \]

Normalize the lower factor to obtain a unit-lower-triangular LU factorization:

\[ \boldsymbol{A} =\underbrace{(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1})}_{\text{unit lower triangular}} \quad\underbrace{(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{L}^*)}_{\text{upper triangular}} \]

By part (b), these two normalized factors are unique. Moreover,

\[ (\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{L}^*)_{k,k}=|L_{k,k}|^2 \]

so the magnitudes of the diagonal entries of \(\boldsymbol{L}\) are fixed.

For another factor \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{L}}\), let \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}\) contain its diagonal entries. Uniqueness of the normalized lower factor gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1} &=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{L}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}^{-1}\\ \widetilde{\boldsymbol{L}} &=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}\\ &=\boldsymbol{L}\operatorname{diag}(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_m) \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\omega_k=\widetilde L_{k,k}/L_{k,k}\) and \(|\omega_k|=1\). Conversely, multiplying columns by such scalars preserves the factorization, since

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{W}&=\operatorname{diag}(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_m)\\ (\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W})(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W})^* &=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{W}^*\boldsymbol{L}^* =\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{L}^* \end{aligned} \]

Thus the factor is unique up to independent column signs over \(\mathbb{R}\) and independent column phases \(\omega_k=e^{i\theta_k}\) over \(\mathbb{C}\). Requiring every \(L_{k,k}\) to be positive real removes this freedom and gives the unique Cholesky factor.

If \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is singular, uniqueness up to column phases need not hold. For example,

\[ \boldsymbol{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \qquad \boldsymbol{L}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \qquad \widetilde{\boldsymbol{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix} \]

Both lower-triangular factors satisfy \(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{L}^*=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{L}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{L}}^*=\boldsymbol{A}\), but column phases cannot move a nonzero entry from one column to another. The sign/phase uniqueness conclusion therefore requires nonsingularity.

Derivation details — added study explanation.

The positive-diagonal Cholesky convention is Lecture 9 §9.3. The phase convention is analogous to Lecture 7 §7.6; the following Cholesky uniqueness proof uses homework 3(b).

If \(A\) is nonsingular and \(A=LL^*\), then \(L\) is nonsingular, so each diagonal \(L_{kk}\ne0\). Define \(D=\operatorname{diag}(L_{kk})\). Both factors in

\[ \begin{aligned} A&=(LD^{-1})(DL^*),\\ (LD^{-1})_{kk}&=L_{kk}/L_{kk}=1,\\ (DL^*)_{kk}&=L_{kk}\overline{L_{kk}}=|L_{kk}|^2 \end{aligned} \]

have the required triangular form. Applying 3(b) to a second factor \(\widetilde L\) yields

\[ \begin{aligned} LD^{-1}&=\widetilde L\widetilde D^{-1}, &|L_{kk}|^2&=|\widetilde L_{kk}|^2,\\ \widetilde L&=LD^{-1}\widetilde D=LW, &W&=\operatorname{diag}(\omega_k),\\ \omega_k&=\widetilde L_{kk}/L_{kk}, &|\omega_k|&=1,\\ (LW)(LW)^*&=LWW^*L^*=LIL^*=A. \end{aligned} \]

Over \(\mathbb R\), \(|\omega_k|=1\) means \(\omega_k=\pm1\). Over \(\mathbb C\), it means \(\omega_k=e^{i\theta_k}\). Positive real diagonals force \(\omega_k=1\) for every \(k\).

For the submitted singular counterexample, multiplication gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}^* &=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\\ \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}^* &=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying a zero column by a phase keeps it zero, so no diagonal phase matrix relates these two factors. Nonsingularity is necessary for the sign/phase-only uniqueness claim.

1.3.4 (d) [5 pts]

Using these results, prove and explain the relationship between the Cholesky factor of \(\boldsymbol{B}^* \boldsymbol{B}\) and the QR factorization of \(\boldsymbol{B}\), for \(\boldsymbol{B} \in \mathbb{K}^{m \times n}\) with \(m \geq n\) and full column rank.

1.3.4.1 Solution to 3(d)

Since \(\boldsymbol{B}\) has full column rank, \(\boldsymbol{B}^*\boldsymbol{B}\) is positive definite:

\[ \boldsymbol{x}^*\boldsymbol{B}^*\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x} =\|\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2>0 \qquad(\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}) \]

Take the reduced QR factorization \(\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}\) with \(\boldsymbol{Q}^*\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{I}_n\) and positive real diagonal entries in \(\boldsymbol{R}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{B}^*\boldsymbol{B} &=(\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R})^*(\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R})\\ &=\boldsymbol{R}^*\boldsymbol{Q}^*\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}\\ &=\boldsymbol{R}^*\boldsymbol{R} \end{aligned} \]

The matrix \(\boldsymbol{R}^*\) is lower triangular with positive real diagonal. By part (c), it is the unique lower Cholesky factor:

\[ \boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{R}^* \]

Conversely, given \(\boldsymbol{B}^*\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{L}^*\), set \(\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{L}^*\) and \(\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}\). Then \(\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}\) and

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{Q}^*\boldsymbol{Q} &=(\boldsymbol{R}^{-1})^*\boldsymbol{B}^*\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}\\ &=(\boldsymbol{R}^*)^{-1}\boldsymbol{R}^*\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}\\ &=\boldsymbol{I}_n \end{aligned} \]

Thus the upper Cholesky factor of the Gram matrix is exactly the triangular factor in reduced QR, with the same positive-diagonal convention.

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Use Lecture 8 §8.7.3’s full-rank Gram characterization, reduced QR in Lecture 7 §7.4.1, and the positive-diagonal convention in §7.6.2. Their connection is stated in Lecture 9 §9.10.

\[ \begin{aligned} x\ne0&\Longrightarrow Bx\ne0 \Longrightarrow x^*B^*Bx=\|Bx\|_2^2>0,\\ B&=QR,\qquad Q^*Q=I_n,\\ B^*B&=R^*Q^*QR=R^*I_nR=R^*R,\\ L&:=R^*\quad\Longrightarrow\quad LL^*=R^*R=B^*B. \end{aligned} \]

\(R^*\) is lower triangular and \((R^*)_{kk}=\overline{R_{kk}}=R_{kk}>0\), so 3(c) identifies it as the unique normalized Cholesky factor. Conversely, start from \(B^*B=LL^*\) and set \(R=L^*\):

\[ \begin{aligned} Q&=BR^{-1},\\ QR&=BR^{-1}R=B,\\ Q^*Q&=(R^{-1})^*B^*BR^{-1}\\ &=(R^*)^{-1}(R^*R)R^{-1}\\ &=((R^*)^{-1}R^*)(RR^{-1})=I_n. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(Q,R\) satisfy reduced QR with the same diagonal normalization.

1.3.5 (e) [5 pts]

For \(\boldsymbol{A} = \boldsymbol{B}^* \boldsymbol{B}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) as in (d), write \[ \boldsymbol{A} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}& \boldsymbol{A}_{1,2} \\ \boldsymbol{A}_{2,1}& \boldsymbol{A}_{2,2} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{B} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{B}_{1} & \boldsymbol{B}_{2} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{7} \] Relate the Schur complement \(\boldsymbol{A}_{2,2} - \boldsymbol{A}_{2,1} \left(\boldsymbol{A}_{1, 1}\right)^{-1} \boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}\) to the Gram-matrix of the columns of \(\boldsymbol{B}_2\), projected onto the orthogonal complement of the column space of \(\boldsymbol{B}_1\). Use this result to refine your comment in (d) on the relationship of Cholesky and QR factorization.

1.3.5.1 Solution to 3(e)

The blocks of the Gram matrix are

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}&=\boldsymbol{B}_1^*\boldsymbol{B}_1 &\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}&=\boldsymbol{B}_1^*\boldsymbol{B}_2\\ \boldsymbol{A}_{2,1}&=\boldsymbol{B}_2^*\boldsymbol{B}_1 &\boldsymbol{A}_{2,2}&=\boldsymbol{B}_2^*\boldsymbol{B}_2 \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\boldsymbol{B}_1\) has full column rank, the orthogonal projector onto its column space is

\[ \boldsymbol{P}_1 =\boldsymbol{B}_1(\boldsymbol{B}_1^*\boldsymbol{B}_1)^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}_1^* \qquad \boldsymbol{P}_1^*=\boldsymbol{P}_1 \qquad \boldsymbol{P}_1^2=\boldsymbol{P}_1 \]

Let \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_2=(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_1)\boldsymbol{B}_2\) be the projected columns. Since \(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_1\) is also Hermitian and idempotent,

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_2^*\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_2 &=\boldsymbol{B}_2^*(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_1)^*(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_1)\boldsymbol{B}_2\\ &=\boldsymbol{B}_2^*(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_1)\boldsymbol{B}_2\\ &=\boldsymbol{B}_2^*\boldsymbol{B}_2 -\boldsymbol{B}_2^*\boldsymbol{B}_1 (\boldsymbol{B}_1^*\boldsymbol{B}_1)^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}_1^*\boldsymbol{B}_2\\ &=\boldsymbol{A}_{2,2} -\boldsymbol{A}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2} \end{aligned} \]

Thus the Schur complement is the Gram matrix of the columns of \(\boldsymbol{B}_2\) after removing their projections onto \(\operatorname{range}(\boldsymbol{B}_1)\). To connect this with part (d), partition the reduced QR factorization in the same way:

\[ \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{B}_1&\boldsymbol{B}_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{Q}_1&\boldsymbol{Q}_2\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{R}_{1,1}&\boldsymbol{R}_{1,2}\\ \boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{R}_{2,2} \end{pmatrix} \]

The block equations give

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{B}_1&=\boldsymbol{Q}_1\boldsymbol{R}_{1,1}\\ \boldsymbol{B}_2&=\boldsymbol{Q}_1\boldsymbol{R}_{1,2} +\boldsymbol{Q}_2\boldsymbol{R}_{2,2} \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\boldsymbol{P}_1=\boldsymbol{Q}_1\boldsymbol{Q}_1^*\) and \(\boldsymbol{Q}_1^*\boldsymbol{Q}_2=\boldsymbol{0}\). Hence

\[ \widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_2 =(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{Q}_1\boldsymbol{Q}_1^*)\boldsymbol{B}_2 =\boldsymbol{Q}_2\boldsymbol{R}_{2,2} \]

Taking its Gram matrix yields

\[ \boldsymbol{A}/\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1} =\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_2^*\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_2 =\boldsymbol{R}_{2,2}^*\boldsymbol{R}_{2,2} \]

Therefore \(\boldsymbol{L}_{2,2}=\boldsymbol{R}_{2,2}^*\) under the positive-diagonal convention. Cholesky’s trailing Schur-complement update corresponds exactly to Gram-Schmidt’s projection step: the updated matrix stores the inner products of the remaining orthogonalized columns.

Derivation details — added study explanation.

The prerequisites are Gram-matrix block products, Lecture 8 §8.7, orthogonal projection, Lecture 5 §5.5, Gram–Schmidt, §5.7, and block Cholesky, Lecture 9 §9.8. This combines them to derive the requested identity.

A subset of the independent columns of \(B\) is independent, so \(G=B_1^*B_1\) is positive definite by Lecture 8 §8.7.3. For \(P=B_1G^{-1}B_1^*\),

\[ \begin{aligned} P^*&=B_1(G^{-1})^*B_1^*=B_1G^{-1}B_1^*=P,\\ P^2&=B_1G^{-1}(B_1^*B_1)G^{-1}B_1^*=P,\\ B_1^*(I-P)&=B_1^*-(B_1^*B_1)G^{-1}B_1^*=0,\\ (I-P)^*(I-P)&=(I-P)^2=I-2P+P^2=I-P. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \((I-P)B_2\) consists of columns orthogonal to \(B_1\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat B_2^*\widehat B_2 &=B_2^*(I-P)^*(I-P)B_2\\ &=B_2^*B_2-B_2^*B_1G^{-1}B_1^*B_2\\ &=A_{22}-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}=A/A_{11}. \end{aligned} \]

For the partitioned reduced QR, \(B_1=Q_1R_{11}\) and \(Q_1^*Q_1=I\). Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} G&=R_{11}^*R_{11},\qquad G^{-1}=R_{11}^{-1}(R_{11}^*)^{-1},\\ P&=Q_1R_{11}R_{11}^{-1}(R_{11}^*)^{-1}R_{11}^*Q_1^* =Q_1Q_1^*,\\ (I-P)B_2 &=(I-Q_1Q_1^*)(Q_1R_{12}+Q_2R_{22})\\ &=(Q_1-Q_1(Q_1^*Q_1))R_{12} +(Q_2-Q_1(Q_1^*Q_2))R_{22}\\ &=0+Q_2R_{22},\\ A/A_{11}&=R_{22}^*Q_2^*Q_2R_{22}=R_{22}^*R_{22}. \end{aligned} \]

By Lecture 9 §9.8, this same Schur complement is \(L_{22}L_{22}^*\). Both \(L_{22}\) and \(R_{22}^*\) have positive real diagonal, so 3(c) gives \(L_{22}=R_{22}^*\). Schur-complement elimination and orthogonal projection therefore produce the same trailing Gram matrix.
