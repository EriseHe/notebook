1 Less positive [12.5 pts]

Consider a symmetric positive definite matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}\) separated into subblocks according to \[ \boldsymbol{A} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{A}_{1, 1} & \boldsymbol{A}_{1, 2} \\ \boldsymbol{A}_{2, 1} & \boldsymbol{A}_{2, 2} \end{pmatrix} \] Show that the matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}_{2,2} - \boldsymbol{A}_{2,1} \boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}^{-1} \boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}\) is symmetric and positive definite.

Proof. For every \(\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\), \[ \boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}\boldsymbol{x} = \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}^{\!T} \boldsymbol{A} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}>0 \] Thus \(\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}\) is symmetric positive definite and invertible. This uses the quadratic-form definition in Lecture 8, §8.6.2 and the nullspace criterion in Lecture 2, §2.5: \[ \begin{aligned} A_{11}u=0&\Longrightarrow u^TA_{11}u=0\\ &\Longrightarrow u=0. \end{aligned} \] Let \(\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{A}_{2,2}-\boldsymbol{A}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}\). This is the Schur complement from Lecture 9, §9.8. Since \(\boldsymbol{A}_{2,1}=\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}^T\), then \[ \begin{aligned} (A_{11}^{-1})^T&=(A_{11}^T)^{-1}=A_{11}^{-1},\\ \boldsymbol{S}^T &=\boldsymbol{A}_{2,2}^T -\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}^T(\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}^{-1})^T\boldsymbol{A}_{2,1}^T\\ &=\boldsymbol{A}_{2,2} -\boldsymbol{A}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2} =\boldsymbol{S} \end{aligned} \] For \(\boldsymbol{y}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\), set \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{z} &=\begin{pmatrix} -\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}\boldsymbol{y}\\ \boldsymbol{y} \end{pmatrix}\ne\boldsymbol{0}, & \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z} &=\begin{pmatrix} -A_{11}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}y+A_{12}y\\ -A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}y+A_{22}y \end{pmatrix}\\ &&&=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}\\\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \] then we know \(0<\boldsymbol{z}^T\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{y}^T\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{y}\). Hence \(\boldsymbol{S}\) is symmetric positive definite.

2 Just one [12.5 pts]

Consider a matrix \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\), with (unpivoted) LU-factorization given by \(\boldsymbol{A} = \boldsymbol{L} \boldsymbol{U}\). Propose an algorithm for computing the \((i, j)\)-th entry of \(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\) using only \(\mathcal{O}\left(\left(m + 1 - j\right)^2 + \left(m + 1 - i\right)^2\right)\) floating point operations.

Proof. Using the given factors, we want to solve \(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{e}_j\) and \(\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{y}\), so then \[ \boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{U}^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_j =\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_j, \qquad x_i=(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})_{ij} \] Use the unit-lower LU convention from Lecture 3, §3.5, with \(A\) invertible. We know that \(L_{kk}=1\), so forward substitution starts at \(j\); and backward substitution stops at \(i\): see Lecture 2, §§2.6.1 and 2.6.2. \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{s=1}^{k-1}L_{ks}y_s+y_k&=(e_j)_k,\\ y_k&=0, &&k<j\\ y_j&=1,\\ y_k&=-\sum_{s=1}^{k-1}L_{ks}y_s =-\sum_{s=j}^{k-1}L_{ks}y_s, &&k=j+1,\ldots,m\\ U_{kk}x_k+\sum_{s=k+1}^mU_{ks}x_s&=y_k,\\ x_k&=\frac{y_k-\displaystyle\sum_{s=k+1}^{m}U_{ks}x_s}{U_{kk}}, &&k=m,m-1,\ldots,i \end{aligned} \] Return \(x_i\). Then the operation count is bounded by (counting multiplication and addition separately, as in Lecture 2, §2.3) \[ \begin{aligned} N_{\mathrm{flop}} &\le \underbrace{2\sum_{k=j+1}^{m}(k-j)}_{\text{forward}} +\underbrace{\sum_{k=i}^{m}\bigl(2(m-k)+1\bigr)}_{\text{backward}}\\ &=2\sum_{t=1}^{m-j}t+2\sum_{t=0}^{m-i}t+(m-i+1)\\ &=(m-j)(m-j+1)+(m-i)(m-i+1)+(m-i+1)\\ &=(m-j)(m-j+1)+(m-i+1)^2\\ &=\mathcal{O}\bigl((m+1-j)^2+(m+1-i)^2\bigr) \end{aligned} \]

For nonunit \(L\), forward substitution also divides by \(L_{kk}\); the asymptotic bound is unchanged.

\(\square\)

3 SVD [25 pts]

Use the recommended literature from the class syllabus to catch up on the SVD applied to non-square matrices, as well as the eigendecomposition of square matrices. The vector-two norm of a vector \(\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is defined as \[ \|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2}. \] The associated matrix-two norm of a matrix \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) is defined as \[ \|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2 = \sup \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}. \]

We denote as \(\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\) the smallest and largest singular value of a matrix and by \(\lambda_{i}\) its \(i\)-th eigenvalue. We consider general matrices \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times l}\). Show that the following results hold,

1.3.1 (a) [4 pts]

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{A}) \|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 \geq \|\boldsymbol{A} \boldsymbol{x}\|_2, \forall \boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n. \] Conclude that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{A}) = \|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2\).

Proof. We write \[ \boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^T, \qquad \sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_r\ge0, \qquad r=\min(m,n), \] with \(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{V}\) orthogonal. The SVD can be obtained from the Gram matrix (Lecture 8, §8.7.1) and the spectral theorem (Lecture 10, §10.2.1): \[ \begin{aligned} A^TA&=V\operatorname{diag}(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n)V^T,\qquad \mu_1\ge\cdots\ge\mu_n\ge0,\\ \mu_k&=v_k^TA^TAv_k=\|Av_k\|_2^2\ge0,\\ \sigma_k&=\sqrt{\mu_k},\qquad u_k=Av_k/\sigma_k\quad(\sigma_k>0),\\ u_i^Tu_j&=\frac{v_i^TA^TAv_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} =\frac{\mu_jv_i^Tv_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}=\delta_{ij},\\ \sigma_k=0&\Longrightarrow\|Av_k\|_2^2=\mu_k=0 \Longrightarrow Av_k=0. \end{aligned} \] Complete the nonzero-image \(u_k\) to an orthonormal basis (Lecture 7, §7.5.3). Then \(AV=U\Sigma\), so \(A=U\Sigma V^T\); when \(m<n\), omit the \(n-m\) additional zero eigenvalues from the list of \(r\) singular values. Let \(\boldsymbol{v}_k\) be the \(k\)-th column of \(\boldsymbol{V}\) and set \(\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{x}\), so \(\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_2=\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2\). \[ \begin{aligned} \|y\|_2^2&=x^TVV^Tx=x^Tx=\|x\|_2^2,\\ \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2 &=(U\Sigma y)^T(U\Sigma y)\\ &=y^T\Sigma^TU^TU\Sigma y =y^T\Sigma^T\Sigma y\\ &=\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{y}\|_2^2 =\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sigma_k^2y_k^2\\ &\le\sigma_1^2\sum_{k=1}^{r}y_k^2 \le\sigma_1^2\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_k^2 =\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})^2\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2 \end{aligned} \] Taking square roots gives the inequality. At the unit vector \(v_1\), \(Av_1=\sigma_1u_1\) and \(\|Av_1\|_2=\sigma_1\) (also when \(\sigma_1=0\)). Equality is attained at \(\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{v}_1\), hence \[ \sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A}) =\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}_1\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{v}_1\|_2} \le\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2 \le\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A}) \]

\(\square\)

1.3.2 (b) [4 pts]

\[ m \geq n \Rightarrow \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 \geq \sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) \|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2, \forall \boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \]

Proof. For \(m\ge n\), \(r=n\), and (using 3(a) and the spectral theorem in Lecture 10, §10.2.1) \[ \begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2 &=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_k^2y_k^2 =\sum_{k=1}^n\bigl(\sigma_n^2+(\sigma_k^2-\sigma_n^2)\bigr)y_k^2\\ &\ge\sigma_n^2\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_k^2 =\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})^2\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2\\ \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 &\ge\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 \end{aligned} \] Equality is attained for \(\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{v}_n\), since \[ \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}_n\|_2=\|\sigma_n\boldsymbol{u}_n\|_2=\sigma_n\|u_n\|_2=\sigma_n=\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}) \]

Consequently \(\inf_{x\ne0}\|Ax\|_2/\|x\|_2=\sigma_{\min}(A)\), with equality at \(v_n\). Full column rank is not required; if rank is deficient, both sides of this minimum are zero.

\(\square\)

1.3.3 (c) [4 pts]

\[ m = n \Rightarrow \sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) \leq \left|\lambda_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right)\right| \leq \sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right), \forall i \]

Proof. The same bounds hold for complex vectors, using \(|y_k|^2\) in the sums. Use the eigenpair definition and spectral theorem from Lecture 10, §§10.2–10.2.1. For an eigenvector \(\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^m\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\), we know that \[ \begin{aligned} y=V^*z&\Longrightarrow\sum_k|y_k|^2=z^*VV^*z=\|z\|_2^2,\\ \|Az\|_2^2&=\sum_k\sigma_k^2|y_k|^2,\\ \sigma_{\min}(A)^2\|z\|_2^2&\le\|Az\|_2^2\le\sigma_{\max}(A)^2\|z\|_2^2,\\ \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_2 &\le\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}\|_2 =\|\lambda_i(A)z\|_2=|\lambda_i(\boldsymbol{A})|\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_2 \le\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_2\\ \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}) &\le|\lambda_i(\boldsymbol{A})| \le\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A}) \end{aligned} \]

The last line divides by \(\|z\|_2>0\); it does not assume that \(A\) is diagonalizable.

\(\square\)

1.3.4 (d) [4 pts]

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) = 1 / \sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right)\text{, if $\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}$ exists} \]

Proof. Let \[ \boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^T,\qquad\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_m>0 \] Since \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is invertible, \(A^TA\) is positive definite (Lecture 8, §8.7.3), so every \(\sigma_k>0\). Using 3(a), \[ \begin{aligned} A^{-1}&=(U\Sigma V^T)^{-1} =(V^T)^{-1}\Sigma^{-1}U^{-1}\\ &=\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}^T,\\ (A^{-1})^TA^{-1} &=U\Sigma^{-1}V^TV\Sigma^{-1}U^T\\ &=U\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^{-2},\ldots,\sigma_m^{-2})U^T. \end{aligned} \] Thus the singular values of \(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\) are \[ \frac{1}{\sigma_1},\ldots,\frac{1}{\sigma_m} \] Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}) &=\min_{1\le k\le m}\frac{1}{\sigma_k(\boldsymbol{A})}\\ &=\frac1{\sigma_1(A)} \quad\text{since }\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_m>0\\ &=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})} \end{aligned} \] as desired.

\(\square\)

1.3.5 (e) [4 pts]

Find the most general conditions on \(m\), \(n\), and \(l\), under which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}\right) \geq \sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) \sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{B}\right)\).

Proof. We know that the necessary and sufficient dimensional condition for the inequality to hold for all \(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}\) is \[ \min(m,l)\le n\le\max(m,l) \] The singular-value identities follow from 3(a); the lower norm bound is 3(b), both based on Lecture 10, §10.2.1. In particular, \[ A^T=(U\Sigma V^T)^T=V\Sigma^TU^T. \] SO transposing replaces \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}\) by \(\boldsymbol{B}^T\boldsymbol{A}^T\) without changing singular values, so it suffices to consider \(m\ge l\). If \(m\ge n\ge l\), part (b) would give \[ \begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 &\ge\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\|\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2\\ &\ge\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{B})\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2,\\ \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}) &=\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} \ge\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{B}) \end{aligned} \] To show this to be true, we retain \(m\ge l\). If \(n<l\), choose \[ \boldsymbol{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_n\\0_{(m-n)\times n}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_n&0_{n\times(l-n)}\end{pmatrix} \] Then \(\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B})=n<l\). \[ \begin{aligned} A^TA&=I_n,\qquad BB^T=I_n,\\ AB&=\begin{pmatrix}I_n&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}_{m\times l},\\ (AB)^TAB&=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{n},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{l-n}). \end{aligned} \] If \(n>m\), choose, with \(\boldsymbol{e}_k\in\mathbb{R}^n\), \[ \boldsymbol{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_m&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{B}=\begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{e}_1&\cdots&\boldsymbol{e}_{l-1}&\boldsymbol{e}_{m+1} \end{pmatrix} \] Then \(\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B})=l-1\). \[ \begin{aligned} AA^T&=I_m,\qquad B^TB=I_l,\\ AB&=\begin{pmatrix}e_1^{(m)}&\cdots&e_{l-1}^{(m)}&0\end{pmatrix},\\ (AB)^TAB&=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{l-1},0). \end{aligned} \] Here \(e_k^{(m)}\) is an \(m\)-dimensional coordinate vector; for \(l=1\), the first \(l-1\) columns are absent. Both choices satisfy \[ \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})=\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{B})=1, \qquad \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B})=0, \] so the inequality fails outside of the stated range as demonstrated

\(\square\)

1.3.6 (f) [5 pts]

In class, we have defined the condition number of a scalar function \(f\) at a point \(x\) as \[ \kappa_f(x) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \sup_{0 < |\delta x| \leq \varepsilon} \frac{|f(x + \delta x) - f(x)|}{|f(x)|} \big/ \frac{|\delta x|}{|x|}. \] Similarly, we can define the two-norm condition number of a vector-valued function \(\boldsymbol{f}:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\) at a point \(\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) as \[ \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \sup_{0 < \|\delta \boldsymbol{x}\|_2 \leq \varepsilon} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x} + \delta \boldsymbol{x}) - \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_2} \big/ \frac{\|\delta \boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} . \] Show that the condition number of the function \(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\) is given by \[ \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2 \|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}. \] For \(m \geq n\) and \(\boldsymbol{A}\) having full column rank, conclude that the worst-case condition number of the function \(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\) is given by \[ \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}} = \sup_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right)}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right)} \eqcolon \kappa(\boldsymbol{A}). \] For invertible square \(\boldsymbol{A}\), find an expression of \(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\) only in terms of operator norms.

Proof. For \(\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\) with \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\), linearity and homogeneity give us (using the relative-perturbation definition in Lecture 1, §1.5) \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) &=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} \lim_{\varepsilon\to0} \sup_{0<\|\delta\boldsymbol{x}\|_2\le\varepsilon} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\delta\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\delta\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}\\ &=\frac{\|x\|_2}{\|Ax\|_2}\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\|A\|_2\\ &=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} \end{aligned} \] Indeed, \(f(x+\delta x)-f(x)=A\delta x\). For every \(\varepsilon>0\), scale any nonzero direction \(v\) to \(\delta x=\varepsilon v/(2\|v\|_2)\): \[ \begin{aligned} 0<\|\delta x\|_2&=\varepsilon/2\le\varepsilon,\\ \frac{\|A\delta x\|_2}{\|\delta x\|_2}&=\frac{\|Av\|_2}{\|v\|_2},\\ \sup_{0<\|\delta x\|_2\le\varepsilon}\frac{\|A\delta x\|_2}{\|\delta x\|_2} &=\sup_{v\ne0}\frac{\|Av\|_2}{\|v\|_2}=\|A\|_2. \end{aligned} \] If \(m\ge n\) and \(\boldsymbol{A}\) has full column rank, then \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) &=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)\|x\|_2}{\|Ax\|_2} \le\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)\|x\|_2}{\sigma_{\min}(A)\|x\|_2}\\ &\le\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})}{\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})}\\ \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{v}_n) &=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)\|v_n\|_2}{\|Av_n\|_2} =\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_n}\quad\text{by 3(b)}\\ &=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})}{\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})}\\ \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}} &=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}}\kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) =\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})}{\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})} =\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}) \end{aligned} \] Use 3(a) and 3(b) for these norm bounds. For invertible square \(\boldsymbol{A}\), \[ \kappa(\boldsymbol{A}) =\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)} =\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}) =\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2 \]

The last equality uses 3(d) and agrees with the definition in Lecture 6, §6.3.1.

\(\square\)

4 Inverses [25 pts]

We consider \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\) and assume that \(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\) exists. You may use without proof that if \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is symmetric, its largest and smallest eigenvalues are characterized as \[ \lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) = \sup \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} \] and \[ \lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) = \inf \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} \]

1.4.1 (a) [5 pts]

Show that \[ \left\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right\|_2^{-1} = \inf \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} \tag{1} \]

Proof. Since \(\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\) bijects the nonzero vectors, by invertibility (Lecture 2, §2.5), \[ \begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2^{-1} &=\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_2}\right)^{-1}\\ &=\left(\sup_{x\ne0}\frac{\|A^{-1}Ax\|_2}{\|Ax\|_2}\right)^{-1}\\ &=\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}\right)^{-1} =\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} \end{aligned} \]

For the reciprocal step, 3(b) gives \(a=\inf_{x\ne0}\|Ax\|_2/\|x\|_2=\sigma_{\min}(A)>0\). Writing this ratio as \(r(x)\), for every \(\eta>0\) some \(x\) has \(a\le r(x)<a+\eta\), so \[ \frac1{a+\eta}<\sup_{x\ne0}\frac1{r(x)}\le\frac1a \quad\xrightarrow{\eta\downarrow0}\quad \sup_{x\ne0}\frac1{r(x)}=\frac1a. \]

\(\square\)

1.4.2 (b) [5 pts]

Show that \[ \left\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right\|_2^{-1} \geq \inf \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{|\boldsymbol{x}^T \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} \tag{2} \]

Proof. By Cauchy–Schwarz and part (a), using Lecture 9, §9.6 and 4(a), \[ \begin{aligned} t&=\frac{x^TAx}{\|x\|_2^2},\\ 0\le\|Ax-tx\|_2^2 &=\|Ax\|_2^2-2t\,x^TAx+t^2\|x\|_2^2\\ &=\|Ax\|_2^2-\frac{(x^TAx)^2}{\|x\|_2^2},\\ |x^TAx|&\le\|x\|_2\|Ax\|_2,\\ \frac{|\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} &\le\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} =\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}\\ \inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{|\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} &\le\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} =\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2^{-1} \end{aligned} \]

The pointwise inequality holds for every \(x\ne0\), so taking the infimum on both sides preserves it.

\(\square\)

1.4.3 (c) [5 pts]

Show that for symmetric matrices \(\boldsymbol{M}, \boldsymbol{N} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\), \[ \lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\boldsymbol{M} + \boldsymbol{N}) \geq \lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{M}\right) + \lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{N}\right) \tag{3} \]

Proof. By the given Rayleigh-quotient characterization, which follows from the spectral theorem in Lecture 10, §10.2.1: for symmetric \(H=Q\Lambda Q^T\), put \(z=Q^Tx\) and write \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{x^THx}{x^Tx} &=\frac{z^T\Lambda z}{z^Tz} =\sum_k\lambda_k\frac{z_k^2}{\sum_jz_j^2},\\ \sum_k\frac{z_k^2}{\sum_jz_j^2}&=1,\qquad \frac{z_k^2}{\sum_jz_j^2}\ge0,\\ \frac{x^THx}{x^Tx}&\ge\lambda_{\min}(H). \end{aligned} \] A minimum-eigenvalue eigenvector attains equality. Applying this to \(M\), \(N\), and \(M+N\), \[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{N}) &=\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} +\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2}\right)\\ &\ge\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} +\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2}\\ &=\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{M})+\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{N}) \end{aligned} \]

Indeed, each Rayleigh quotient is at least its own minimum; summing these pointwise lower bounds and then minimizing gives the displayed inequality. The minimizing vectors for \(M\) and \(N\) need not coincide.

\(\square\)

1.4.4 (d) [5 pts]

In the same setting as (c), show that \[ \lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{M} + \boldsymbol{N}) \leq \lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{M}\right) + \lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{N}\right) \tag{4} \]

Proof. Similarly, use the weights from 4(c), based on Lecture 10, §10.2.1: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{x^THx}{x^Tx} &=\sum_k\lambda_kw_k\le\lambda_{\max}(H)\sum_kw_k=\lambda_{\max}(H),\\ w_k&=\frac{z_k^2}{\sum_jz_j^2}\ge0,\qquad\sum_kw_k=1. \end{aligned} \] A maximum-eigenvalue eigenvector attains equality. Hence \[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{N}) &=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} +\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2}\right)\\ &\le\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} +\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2}\\ &=\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{M})+\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{N}) \end{aligned} \]

Each quotient is bounded above by its own maximum; taking the supremum after adding preserves that upper bound.

\(\square\)

1.4.5 (e) [5 pts]

For \(\boldsymbol{A}\) symmetric positive definite, show that \[ \|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_{2}^{-1} = \inf \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T \boldsymbol{A} \boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^2_2} \]

Proof. Let \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{q}=\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{q}\), \(\boldsymbol{q}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\). Since \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is positive definite, \(\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})>0\). This follows from the positivity definition (Lecture 8, §8.6.2): \[ 0<q^TAq=\lambda_{\min}(A)\|q\|_2^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lambda_{\min}(A)>0. \] The Rayleigh characterization follows from 4(c) and the spectral theorem (Lecture 10, §10.2.1). Parts (b) and (a), respectively, give \[ \begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2^{-1} &\ge\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} =\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}),\\ \|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2^{-1} &\le\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{q}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_2} =\frac{\|\lambda_{\min}(A)q\|_2}{\|q\|_2}\\ &=\frac{|\lambda_{\min}(A)|\|q\|_2}{\|q\|_2} =\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}). \end{aligned} \] Therefore \[ \|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2^{-1} =\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}) =\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} \]

\(\square\)
