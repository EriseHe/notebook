1 Less positive [12.5 pts]

Consider a symmetric positive definite matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}\) separated into subblocks according to \[ \boldsymbol{A} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{A}_{1, 1} & \boldsymbol{A}_{1, 2} \\ \boldsymbol{A}_{2, 1} & \boldsymbol{A}_{2, 2} \end{pmatrix} \] Show that the matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}_{2,2} - \boldsymbol{A}_{2,1} \boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}^{-1} \boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}\) is symmetric and positive definite.

Proof. For every \(\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\), \[ \boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}\boldsymbol{x} = \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}^{\!T} \boldsymbol{A} \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}>0 \] Thus \(\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}\) is symmetric positive definite and invertible. Let \(\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{A}_{2,2}-\boldsymbol{A}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}\). Since \(\boldsymbol{A}_{2,1}=\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}^T\), then \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{S}^T &=\boldsymbol{A}_{2,2}^T -\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}^T(\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}^{-1})^T\boldsymbol{A}_{2,1}^T\\ &=\boldsymbol{A}_{2,2} -\boldsymbol{A}_{2,1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2} =\boldsymbol{S} \end{aligned} \] For \(\boldsymbol{y}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\), set \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{z} &=\begin{pmatrix} -\boldsymbol{A}_{1,1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}\boldsymbol{y}\\ \boldsymbol{y} \end{pmatrix}\ne\boldsymbol{0}, & \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z} &=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}\\\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \] then we know \(0<\boldsymbol{z}^T\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{y}^T\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{y}\). Hence \(\boldsymbol{S}\) is symmetric positive definite.

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Use the positive-definite quadratic-form definition in Lecture 8, §8.6.2. The block \(S\) is the Schur complement defined in Lecture 9, §9.8.

First, \(A_{11}\) is symmetric. For \(u\ne0\), \((u,0)^T\ne0\) and \[ \begin{aligned} u^TA_{11}u &=\begin{pmatrix}u\\0\end{pmatrix}^TA\begin{pmatrix}u\\0\end{pmatrix}>0,\\ A_{11}u=0&\Longrightarrow u^TA_{11}u=0\Longrightarrow u=0. \end{aligned} \] Thus \(A_{11}\) is invertible by the nullspace criterion in Lecture 2, §2.5. Transposing \(A_{11}A_{11}^{-1}=I\) gives \((A_{11}^{-1})^T=(A_{11}^T)^{-1}=A_{11}^{-1}\). Hence \[ \begin{aligned} S^T &=A_{22}^T-A_{12}^T(A_{11}^{-1})^TA_{21}^T\\ &=A_{22}-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}=S. \end{aligned} \] For \(y\ne0\), set \(w=-A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}y\) and \(z=(w,y)^T\ne0\). Block multiplication gives \[ \begin{aligned} Az&=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}w+A_{12}y\\A_{21}w+A_{22}y\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}-A_{12}y+A_{12}y\\-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}y+A_{22}y\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0\\Sy\end{pmatrix},\\ 0<z^TAz&=\begin{pmatrix}w^T&y^T\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0\\Sy\end{pmatrix}=y^TSy. \end{aligned} \] This proves both required properties.

2 Just one [12.5 pts]

Consider a matrix \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\), with (unpivoted) LU-factorization given by \(\boldsymbol{A} = \boldsymbol{L} \boldsymbol{U}\). Propose an algorithm for computing the \((i, j)\)-th entry of \(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\) using only \(\mathcal{O}\left(\left(m + 1 - j\right)^2 + \left(m + 1 - i\right)^2\right)\) floating point operations.

Proof. Using the given factors, we want to solve \(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{e}_j\) and \(\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{y}\), so then \[ \boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{U}^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_j =\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_j, \qquad x_i=(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})_{ij} \] We know that \(L_{kk}=1\), so forward substitution starts at \(j\); and backward substitution stops at \(i\): \[ \begin{aligned} y_k&=0, &&k<j\\ y_j&=1,\\ y_k&=-\sum_{s=j}^{k-1}L_{ks}y_s, &&k=j+1,\ldots,m\\ x_k&=\frac{y_k-\displaystyle\sum_{s=k+1}^{m}U_{ks}x_s}{U_{kk}}, &&k=m,m-1,\ldots,i \end{aligned} \] Return \(x_i\). Then the operation count is bounded by \[ \begin{aligned} N_{\mathrm{flop}} &\le \underbrace{2\sum_{k=j+1}^{m}(k-j)}_{\text{forward}} +\underbrace{\sum_{k=i}^{m}\bigl(2(m-k)+1\bigr)}_{\text{backward}}\\ &=(m-j)(m-j+1)+(m-i+1)^2\\ &=\mathcal{O}\bigl((m+1-j)^2+(m+1-i)^2\bigr) \end{aligned} \]

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Use forward substitution, Lecture 2 §2.6.1, back substitution, §2.6.2 and the unit-lower elimination factors in Lecture 3, §3.5. We need \(A\) invertible; the submitted formulas use \(L_{kk}=1\).

The \(j\)th column of \(A^{-1}\) solves \(Ax=e_j\). Since \(A=LU\), \[ \begin{aligned} Ax=e_j&\Longleftrightarrow LUx=e_j,\\ y=L^{-1}e_j,\quad x=U^{-1}y &\Longrightarrow x=A^{-1}e_j,\qquad x_i=(A^{-1})_{ij}. \end{aligned} \] For \(k<j\), the lower-triangular equations successively give \(y_k=0\). Row \(j\) gives \(y_j=1\). Later rows give \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{s=1}^{k-1}L_{ks}y_s+y_k&=0,\\ y_k&=-\sum_{s=j}^{k-1}L_{ks}y_s\qquad(k>j),\\ \sum_{s=k+1}^mU_{ks}x_s+U_{kk}x_k&=y_k,\\ x_k&=\frac{y_k-\sum_{s=k+1}^mU_{ks}x_s}{U_{kk}}\qquad(k=m,\ldots,i). \end{aligned} \] Rows below \(i\) determine \(x_i\); rows above \(i\) need not be solved. Counting multiply/add operations separately, as in Lecture 2, §2.3, \[ \begin{aligned} N_{\mathrm{forward}}&\le2\sum_{t=1}^{m-j}t=(m-j)(m-j+1),\\ N_{\mathrm{backward}}&\le\sum_{t=0}^{m-i}(2t+1)=(m-i+1)^2,\\ N_{\mathrm{total}}&\le(m-j)(m-j+1)+(m-i+1)^2\\ &=O\bigl((m+1-j)^2+(m+1-i)^2\bigr). \end{aligned} \] For a nonunit \(L\), divide each forward-substitution row by \(L_{kk}\); the same asymptotic bound holds.

3 SVD [25 pts]

Use the recommended literature from the class syllabus to catch up on the SVD applied to non-square matrices, as well as the eigendecomposition of square matrices. The vector-two norm of a vector \(\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is defined as \[ \|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2}. \] The associated matrix-two norm of a matrix \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) is defined as \[ \|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2 = \sup \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}. \]

We denote as \(\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\) the smallest and largest singular value of a matrix and by \(\lambda_{i}\) its \(i\)-th eigenvalue. We consider general matrices \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times l}\). Show that the following results hold,

1.3.1 (a) [4 pts]

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{A}) \|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 \geq \|\boldsymbol{A} \boldsymbol{x}\|_2, \forall \boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n. \] Conclude that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{A}) = \|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2\).

Proof. We write \[ \boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^T, \qquad \sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_r\ge0, \qquad r=\min(m,n), \] with \(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{V}\) orthogonal. Let \(\boldsymbol{v}_k\) be the \(k\)-th column of \(\boldsymbol{V}\) and set \(\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{V}^T\boldsymbol{x}\), so \(\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_2=\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2\). \[ \begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2 &=\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{y}\|_2^2 =\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sigma_k^2y_k^2\\ &\le\sigma_1^2\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_k^2 =\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})^2\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2 \end{aligned} \] Taking square roots gives the inequality. Equality is attained at \(\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{v}_1\), hence \[ \sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A}) =\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}_1\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{v}_1\|_2} \le\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2 \le\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A}) \]

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

The rectangular SVD is assigned background in this problem, rather than a theorem proved in the available lecture notes. Its needed norm identity follows from the Gram matrix in Lecture 8, §8.7.1 and the Hermitian spectral theorem in Lecture 10, §10.2.1.

Write \(A^TA=V\operatorname{diag}(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n)V^T\), with \(\mu_1\ge\cdots\ge\mu_n\ge0\). Put \(y=V^Tx\). Orthogonality gives \[ \begin{aligned} \|y\|_2^2&=x^TVV^Tx=x^Tx=\|x\|_2^2,\\ \|Ax\|_2^2&=x^TA^TAx\\ &=y^T\operatorname{diag}(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n)y\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^n\mu_ky_k^2 \le\mu_1\sum_{k=1}^ny_k^2 =\mu_1\|x\|_2^2. \end{aligned} \] To recover the SVD, set \(\sigma_k=\sqrt{\mu_k}\) and \(u_k=Av_k/\sigma_k\) whenever \(\sigma_k>0\). Then \[ \begin{aligned} u_i^Tu_j&=\frac{v_i^TA^TAv_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} =\frac{\mu_jv_i^Tv_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} =\delta_{ij},\\ \sigma_k=0&\Longrightarrow\|Av_k\|_2^2=\mu_k=0 \Longrightarrow Av_k=0. \end{aligned} \] Complete the nonzero-image \(u_k\) to an orthonormal basis, as in Lecture 7, §7.5.3. With these columns forming \(U\) and the rectangular diagonal matrix \(\Sigma\), \[ \begin{aligned} Av_k&=\sigma_ku_k\quad(\sigma_k>0),\qquad Av_k=0\quad(\sigma_k=0),\\ AV&=U\Sigma,\\ A&=U\Sigma V^T. \end{aligned} \] Thus the singular values are the square roots of the Gram eigenvalues, with the additional structural zeros omitted when \(m<n\); \(\sigma_{\max}(A)=\sqrt{\mu_1}\). Taking nonnegative square roots in the norm bound and dividing by \(\|x\|_2\) for \(x\ne0\) gives \(\|A\|_2\le\sigma_{\max}(A)\). For the unit eigenvector \(v_1\), \[ \begin{aligned} \|Av_1\|_2^2&=v_1^TA^TAv_1=\mu_1v_1^Tv_1=\mu_1,\\ \|A\|_2&\ge\frac{\|Av_1\|_2}{\|v_1\|_2}=\sqrt{\mu_1}=\sigma_{\max}(A). \end{aligned} \] Both inequalities also hold when \(A=0\).

1.3.2 (b) [4 pts]

\[ m \geq n \Rightarrow \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 \geq \sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) \|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2, \forall \boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \]

Proof. For \(m\ge n\), \(r=n\), and \[ \begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2 &=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_k^2y_k^2 \ge\sigma_n^2\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_k^2 =\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})^2\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2\\ \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 &\ge\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 \end{aligned} \] Equality is attained for \(\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{v}_n\), since \[ \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}_n\|_2=\|\sigma_n\boldsymbol{u}_n\|_2=\sigma_n=\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}) \]

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Use the Gram-matrix spectral expansion derived in 3(a), based on Lecture 10, §10.2.1. When \(m\ge n\), all \(n\) singular values occur in \(A^TA\), so \(\mu_n=\sigma_{\min}(A)^2\). \[ \begin{aligned} \|Ax\|_2^2-\sigma_{\min}(A)^2\|x\|_2^2 &=\sum_{k=1}^n\bigl(\mu_k-\mu_n\bigr)y_k^2\ge0,\\ \|Ax\|_2&\ge\sigma_{\min}(A)\|x\|_2. \end{aligned} \] For \(x=v_n\), \(y=e_n\), so every term in the difference vanishes: \[ \begin{aligned} \|Av_n\|_2^2&=v_n^TA^TAv_n=\mu_n,\\ \|Av_n\|_2&=\sqrt{\mu_n}=\sigma_{\min}(A),\\ \inf_{x\ne0}\frac{\|Ax\|_2}{\|x\|_2}&=\sigma_{\min}(A). \end{aligned} \] Full column rank is unnecessary for the inequality; rank deficiency makes the lower bound zero. The condition \(m\ge n\) matters: a wide full-row-rank matrix has a nonzero null vector even when its listed smallest singular value is positive.

1.3.3 (c) [4 pts]

\[ m = n \Rightarrow \sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) \leq \left|\lambda_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right)\right| \leq \sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right), \forall i \]

Proof. The same bounds hold for complex vectors, using \(|y_k|^2\) in the sums. For an eigenvector \(\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^m\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\), we know that \[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_2 &\le\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}\|_2 =|\lambda_i(\boldsymbol{A})|\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_2 \le\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_2\\ \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}) &\le|\lambda_i(\boldsymbol{A})| \le\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A}) \end{aligned} \]

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Eigenvalues and complex eigenvectors are defined in Lecture 10, §10.2. No diagonalizability of \(A\) is needed. The Gram-matrix spectral theorem from §10.2.1 extends the previous bounds to complex vectors: \[ \begin{aligned} y=V^*z&\Longrightarrow\|y\|_2^2=\sum_k|y_k|^2=\|z\|_2^2,\\ \|Az\|_2^2&=\sum_k\sigma_k^2|y_k|^2,\\ \sigma_{\min}(A)^2\|z\|_2^2 &\le\|Az\|_2^2\le\sigma_{\max}(A)^2\|z\|_2^2. \end{aligned} \] For any eigenpair \(Az=\lambda_i z\) with \(z\ne0\), \[ \begin{aligned} \|Az\|_2^2&=\sum_k|\lambda_i z_k|^2 =|\lambda_i|^2\|z\|_2^2,\\ \sigma_{\min}(A)^2&\le|\lambda_i|^2\le\sigma_{\max}(A)^2,\\ \sigma_{\min}(A)&\le|\lambda_i|\le\sigma_{\max}(A). \end{aligned} \] Division is valid because \(\|z\|_2^2>0\).

1.3.4 (d) [4 pts]

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) = 1 / \sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right)\text{, if $\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}$ exists} \]

Proof. Let \[ \boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^T,\qquad\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_m>0 \] Since \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is invertible, \[ \boldsymbol{A}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}^T. \] Thus the singular values of \(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\) are \[ \frac{1}{\sigma_1},\ldots,\frac{1}{\sigma_m} \] Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}) &=\min_{1\le k\le m}\frac{1}{\sigma_k(\boldsymbol{A})}\\ &=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})} \end{aligned} \] as desired.

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Use the assigned SVD notation from 3(a). Invertibility makes \(A^TA\) positive definite: \(x\ne0\) implies \(Ax\ne0\), so \(x^TA^TAx=\|Ax\|_2^2>0\) (Lecture 8, §8.7.3). Thus each \(\sigma_k>0\). \[ \begin{aligned} A&=U\Sigma V^T,\qquad U^{-1}=U^T,\quad V^{-1}=V^T,\\ A^{-1}&=(U\Sigma V^T)^{-1} =(V^T)^{-1}\Sigma^{-1}U^{-1} =V\Sigma^{-1}U^T,\\ (A^{-1})^TA^{-1} &=U\Sigma^{-1}V^TV\Sigma^{-1}U^T =U\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^{-2},\ldots,\sigma_m^{-2})U^T. \end{aligned} \] Hence the singular values of \(A^{-1}\) are \(1/\sigma_k\), in reversed size order: \[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_m>0 &\Longrightarrow \frac1{\sigma_1}\le\cdots\le\frac1{\sigma_m},\\ \sigma_{\min}(A^{-1})&=\frac1{\sigma_1}=\frac1{\sigma_{\max}(A)},\\ \sigma_{\max}(A)&=\frac1{\sigma_{\min}(A^{-1})}. \end{aligned} \]

1.3.5 (e) [4 pts]

Find the most general conditions on \(m\), \(n\), and \(l\), under which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}\right) \geq \sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) \sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{B}\right)\).

Proof. We know that the necessary and sufficient dimensional condition for the inequality to hold for all \(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}\) is \[ \min(m,l)\le n\le\max(m,l) \] SO transposing replaces \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}\) by \(\boldsymbol{B}^T\boldsymbol{A}^T\) without changing singular values, so it suffices to consider \(m\ge l\). If \(m\ge n\ge l\), part (b) would give \[ \begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 &\ge\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\|\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2\\ &\ge\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{B})\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2,\\ \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}) &=\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} \ge\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{B}) \end{aligned} \] To show this to be true, we retain \(m\ge l\). If \(n<l\), choose \[ \boldsymbol{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_n\\\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_n&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix} \] Then \(\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B})=n<l\). If \(n>m\), choose, with \(\boldsymbol{e}_k\in\mathbb{R}^n\), \[ \boldsymbol{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_m&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{B}=\begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{e}_1&\cdots&\boldsymbol{e}_{l-1}&\boldsymbol{e}_{m+1} \end{pmatrix} \] Then \(\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B})=l-1\). Both choices satisfy \[ \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})=\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{B})=1, \qquad \sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B})=0, \] so the inequality fails outside of the stated range as demonstrated

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

The norm bounds are derived in 3(b) from the Hermitian spectral theorem (Lecture 10, §10.2.1). The dimensional criterion itself is proved here, rather than attributed to a lecture.

Transposing the assigned SVD gives \(A^T=V\Sigma^TU^T\), so transposition preserves its listed singular values. Since \((AB)^T=B^TA^T\), it suffices to treat \(m\ge l\). If \(m\ge n\ge l\), both \(A\) and \(B\) are tall, and for every \(x\ne0\), \[ \begin{aligned} \|ABx\|_2 &\ge\sigma_{\min}(A)\|Bx\|_2\\ &\ge\sigma_{\min}(A)\sigma_{\min}(B)\|x\|_2,\\ \sigma_{\min}(AB) &=\inf_{x\ne0}\frac{\|ABx\|_2}{\|x\|_2} \ge\sigma_{\min}(A)\sigma_{\min}(B). \end{aligned} \] For the opposite ordering \(m\le l\), transposition gives the same argument with \(l\ge n\ge m\).

For necessity, keep \(m\ge l\). If \(n<l\), choose \[ \begin{aligned} A&=\begin{pmatrix}I_n\\0_{(m-n)\times n}\end{pmatrix}, &B&=\begin{pmatrix}I_n&0_{n\times(l-n)}\end{pmatrix},\\ A^TA&=I_n,&BB^T&=I_n,\\ AB&=\begin{pmatrix}I_n&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}_{m\times l}. \end{aligned} \] Thus \(\sigma_{\min}(A)=\sigma_{\min}(B)=1\), but \(AB\) has \(l-n\) zero singular values. If \(n>m\), choose \[ \begin{aligned} A&=\begin{pmatrix}I_m&0\end{pmatrix}, &B&=\begin{pmatrix}e_1&\cdots&e_{l-1}&e_{m+1}\end{pmatrix},\\ AA^T&=I_m,&B^TB&=I_l,\\ AB&=\begin{pmatrix}e_1^{(m)}&\cdots&e_{l-1}^{(m)}&0\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \] Here \(e_k\in\mathbb R^n\); the last display uses \(m\)-dimensional vectors. Again the individual smallest singular values are \(1\), but the product’s is \(0\). When \(l=1\), the first \(l-1\) columns are absent. These two failures exclude exactly the dimensions outside \(\min(m,l)\le n\le\max(m,l)\).

1.3.6 (f) [5 pts]

In class, we have defined the condition number of a scalar function \(f\) at a point \(x\) as \[ \kappa_f(x) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \sup_{0 < |\delta x| \leq \varepsilon} \frac{|f(x + \delta x) - f(x)|}{|f(x)|} \big/ \frac{|\delta x|}{|x|}. \] Similarly, we can define the two-norm condition number of a vector-valued function \(\boldsymbol{f}:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\) at a point \(\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) as \[ \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \sup_{0 < \|\delta \boldsymbol{x}\|_2 \leq \varepsilon} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x} + \delta \boldsymbol{x}) - \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_2} \big/ \frac{\|\delta \boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} . \] Show that the condition number of the function \(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\) is given by \[ \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2 \|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}. \] For \(m \geq n\) and \(\boldsymbol{A}\) having full column rank, conclude that the worst-case condition number of the function \(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\) is given by \[ \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}} = \sup_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right)}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right)} \eqcolon \kappa(\boldsymbol{A}). \] For invertible square \(\boldsymbol{A}\), find an expression of \(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\) only in terms of operator norms.

Proof. For \(\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\) with \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\), linearity and homogeneity give us \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) &=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} \lim_{\varepsilon\to0} \sup_{0<\|\delta\boldsymbol{x}\|_2\le\varepsilon} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\delta\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\delta\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}\\ &=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} \end{aligned} \] If \(m\ge n\) and \(\boldsymbol{A}\) has full column rank, then \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) &\le\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})}{\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})}\\ \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{v}_n) &=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})}{\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})}\\ \kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}} &=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}}\kappa_{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) =\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})}{\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})} =\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}) \end{aligned} \] For invertible square \(\boldsymbol{A}\), \[ \kappa(\boldsymbol{A}) =\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A})\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}) =\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_2\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2 \]

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

The relative-perturbation definition comes from Lecture 1, §1.5. The vector version is specified in this problem. It requires \(x\ne0\) and \(Ax\ne0\); otherwise the relative error denominator vanishes. \[ \begin{aligned} f(x+\delta x)-f(x)&=A(x+\delta x)-Ax=A\delta x,\\ \frac{\|f(x+\delta x)-f(x)\|_2/\|f(x)\|_2} {\|\delta x\|_2/\|x\|_2} &=\frac{\|x\|_2}{\|Ax\|_2} \frac{\|A\delta x\|_2}{\|\delta x\|_2}. \end{aligned} \] For every \(\varepsilon>0\), every nonzero direction \(v\) can be scaled to \(\delta x=\varepsilon v/(2\|v\|_2)\). Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} 0<\|\delta x\|_2&=\varepsilon/2\le\varepsilon,\\ \frac{\|A\delta x\|_2}{\|\delta x\|_2} &=\frac{\|Av\|_2}{\|v\|_2},\\ \sup_{0<\|\delta x\|_2\le\varepsilon} \frac{\|A\delta x\|_2}{\|\delta x\|_2} &=\sup_{v\ne0}\frac{\|Av\|_2}{\|v\|_2}=\|A\|_2,\\ \kappa_f(x)&=\frac{\|A\|_2\|x\|_2}{\|Ax\|_2}. \end{aligned} \] The supremum is independent of \(\varepsilon\), so taking the limit changes nothing. For full column rank, 3(a)–3(b) give \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa_f(x)&\le\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)},\\ \kappa_f(v_n)&=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)\|v_n\|_2}{\|Av_n\|_2} =\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)},\\ \sup_{x\ne0}\kappa_f(x)&=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)}. \end{aligned} \] For square invertible \(A\), 3(d) yields \(\sigma_{\max}(A^{-1})=1/\sigma_{\min}(A)\), hence \(\kappa(A)=\|A\|_2\|A^{-1}\|_2\). This is also the definition used in Lecture 6, §6.3.1.

4 Inverses [25 pts]

We consider \(\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\) and assume that \(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\) exists. You may use without proof that if \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is symmetric, its largest and smallest eigenvalues are characterized as \[ \lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) = \sup \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} \] and \[ \lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right) = \inf \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} \]

1.4.1 (a) [5 pts]

Show that \[ \left\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right\|_2^{-1} = \inf \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} \tag{1} \]

Proof. Since \(\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\) bijects the nonzero vectors, \[ \begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2^{-1} &=\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_2}\right)^{-1}\\ &=\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}\right)^{-1} =\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} \end{aligned} \]

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Invertibility gives a bijection \(y=Ax\) between nonzero vectors; the equivalent characterization of nonsingularity appears in Lecture 2, §2.5. Set \(a=\inf_{x\ne0}\|Ax\|_2/\|x\|_2\). By 3(b), \(a=\sigma_{\min}(A)>0\). \[ \begin{aligned} \|A^{-1}\|_2 &=\sup_{y\ne0}\frac{\|A^{-1}y\|_2}{\|y\|_2}\\ &=\sup_{x\ne0}\frac{\|A^{-1}Ax\|_2}{\|Ax\|_2}\\ &=\sup_{x\ne0}\frac{\|x\|_2}{\|Ax\|_2} =\sup_{x\ne0}\frac1{\|Ax\|_2/\|x\|_2}. \end{aligned} \] Every denominator is at least \(a\), so this supremum is at most \(1/a\). Conversely, for every \(\eta>0\), the definition of infimum gives some \(x\ne0\) with ratio below \(a+\eta\); its reciprocal exceeds \(1/(a+\eta)\). Letting \(\eta\downarrow0\) gives \[ \begin{aligned} \|A^{-1}\|_2&=1/a,\\ \|A^{-1}\|_2^{-1}&=a=\inf_{x\ne0}\frac{\|Ax\|_2}{\|x\|_2}. \end{aligned} \] The positivity of \(a\) is essential when taking reciprocals.

1.4.2 (b) [5 pts]

Show that \[ \left\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right\|_2^{-1} \geq \inf \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{|\boldsymbol{x}^T \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} \tag{2} \]

Proof. By Cauchy–Schwarz and part (a), \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{|\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} &\le\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} =\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}\\ \inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{|\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} &\le\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2} =\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2^{-1} \end{aligned} \]

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Cauchy–Schwarz is used explicitly in Lecture 9, §9.6. For completeness, with \(v\ne0\) and \(t=(u^Tv)/(v^Tv)\), \[ \begin{aligned} 0\le\|u-tv\|_2^2 &=u^Tu-2t\,u^Tv+t^2v^Tv\\ &=\|u\|_2^2-\frac{(u^Tv)^2}{\|v\|_2^2},\\ |u^Tv|&\le\|u\|_2\|v\|_2. \end{aligned} \] For \(v=0\), the last inequality is immediate. Apply it to \(u=x\), \(v=Ax\): \[ \begin{aligned} |x^TAx|&\le\|x\|_2\|Ax\|_2,\\ \frac{|x^TAx|}{\|x\|_2^2} &\le\frac{\|Ax\|_2}{\|x\|_2}\qquad(x\ne0). \end{aligned} \] To justify taking infima, let \(c\) be the infimum of the left-hand ratio. For every \(x\ne0\), \(c\) is at most that ratio and hence at most the right-hand ratio. Thus \(c\) is a lower bound for all right-hand ratios: \[ \begin{aligned} \inf_{x\ne0}\frac{|x^TAx|}{\|x\|_2^2} &\le\inf_{x\ne0}\frac{\|Ax\|_2}{\|x\|_2} =\|A^{-1}\|_2^{-1}, \end{aligned} \] where the final equality is 4(a).

1.4.3 (c) [5 pts]

Show that for symmetric matrices \(\boldsymbol{M}, \boldsymbol{N} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\), \[ \lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\boldsymbol{M} + \boldsymbol{N}) \geq \lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{M}\right) + \lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{N}\right) \tag{3} \]

Proof. By the given Rayleigh-quotient characterization, \[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{N}) &=\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} +\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2}\right)\\ &\ge\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} +\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2}\\ &=\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{M})+\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{N}) \end{aligned} \]

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

The minimum Rayleigh-quotient characterization is supplied in Problem 4. It follows from the Hermitian spectral theorem in Lecture 10, §10.2.1: for symmetric \(H=Q\Lambda Q^T\) and \(z=Q^Tx\), \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{x^THx}{x^Tx} &=\frac{z^T\Lambda z}{z^Tz} =\sum_k\lambda_k\frac{z_k^2}{\sum_jz_j^2},\\ \frac{z_k^2}{\sum_jz_j^2}&\ge0, \qquad\sum_k\frac{z_k^2}{\sum_jz_j^2}=1,\\ \frac{x^THx}{x^Tx}&\ge\lambda_{\min}(H). \end{aligned} \] Equality holds at an eigenvector for \(\lambda_{\min}(H)\). Apply this bound separately to \(M\) and \(N\): \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{x^T(M+N)x}{\|x\|_2^2} &=\frac{x^TMx}{\|x\|_2^2}+\frac{x^TNx}{\|x\|_2^2}\\ &\ge\lambda_{\min}(M)+\lambda_{\min}(N)\qquad(x\ne0),\\ \lambda_{\min}(M+N) &=\inf_{x\ne0}\frac{x^T(M+N)x}{\|x\|_2^2}\\ &\ge\lambda_{\min}(M)+\lambda_{\min}(N). \end{aligned} \] The separate minimizing eigenvectors need not coincide; that is why this is an inequality.

1.4.4 (d) [5 pts]

In the same setting as (c), show that \[ \lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{M} + \boldsymbol{N}) \leq \lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{M}\right) + \lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{N}\right) \tag{4} \]

Proof. Similarly, \[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{N}) &=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} +\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2}\right)\\ &\le\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} +\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2}\\ &=\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{M})+\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{N}) \end{aligned} \]

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Use the spectral expansion from 4(c), derived from Lecture 10, §10.2.1. Its nonnegative weights also give \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{x^THx}{x^Tx} &=\sum_k\lambda_kw_k\le\lambda_{\max}(H)\sum_kw_k =\lambda_{\max}(H),\\ w_k&=\frac{z_k^2}{\sum_jz_j^2}\ge0,\qquad\sum_kw_k=1. \end{aligned} \] An eigenvector for \(\lambda_{\max}(H)\) attains equality, proving the maximum characterization supplied in the problem. Consequently, \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{x^T(M+N)x}{\|x\|_2^2} &=\frac{x^TMx}{\|x\|_2^2}+\frac{x^TNx}{\|x\|_2^2}\\ &\le\lambda_{\max}(M)+\lambda_{\max}(N)\qquad(x\ne0),\\ \lambda_{\max}(M+N) &=\sup_{x\ne0}\frac{x^T(M+N)x}{\|x\|_2^2}\\ &\le\lambda_{\max}(M)+\lambda_{\max}(N). \end{aligned} \] No common eigenbasis for \(M\) and \(N\) is assumed.

1.4.5 (e) [5 pts]

For \(\boldsymbol{A}\) symmetric positive definite, show that \[ \|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_{2}^{-1} = \inf \limits_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{\boldsymbol{0}\}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T \boldsymbol{A} \boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^2_2} \]

Proof. Let \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{q}=\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{q}\), \(\boldsymbol{q}\ne\boldsymbol{0}\). Since \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is positive definite, \(\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})>0\). Parts (b) and (a), respectively, give \[ \begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2^{-1} &\ge\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} =\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}),\\ \|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2^{-1} &\le\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{q}\|_2}{\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_2} =\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}). \end{aligned} \] Therefore \[ \|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_2^{-1} =\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}) =\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\ne\boldsymbol{0}} \frac{\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2^2} \]

\(\square\)

Derivation details — added study explanation.

Positive definiteness is defined in Lecture 8, §8.6.2. Its positive eigenvalues follow by applying that definition to each eigenvector, as in §8.7.2: \[ \begin{aligned} Aq_k&=\lambda_kq_k,\quad q_k\ne0,\\ 0<q_k^TAq_k&=\lambda_k\|q_k\|_2^2 \Longrightarrow\lambda_k>0. \end{aligned} \] Combine 4(a) and 4(c) with the orthonormal spectral theorem from Lecture 10, §10.2.1, write \(A=Q\Lambda Q^T\) with \(0<\lambda_{\min}\le\lambda_k\). For \(y=Q^Tx\), \[ \begin{aligned} \|Ax\|_2^2 &=\|Q\Lambda y\|_2^2 =y^T\Lambda^2y =\sum_k\lambda_k^2y_k^2\\ &\ge\lambda_{\min}^2\sum_ky_k^2 =\lambda_{\min}^2\|x\|_2^2,\\ \inf_{x\ne0}\frac{\|Ax\|_2}{\|x\|_2} &=\lambda_{\min}\qquad\text{(attained at a minimum-eigenvalue eigenvector)},\\ \inf_{x\ne0}\frac{x^TAx}{\|x\|_2^2} &=\lambda_{\min}\qquad\text{by the expansion in 4(c)},\\ \|A^{-1}\|_2^{-1} &=\lambda_{\min} =\inf_{x\ne0}\frac{x^TAx}{\|x\|_2^2}\qquad\text{by 4(a)}. \end{aligned} \] Symmetry alone would not suffice: positivity ensures the smallest eigenvalue equals the smallest singular value.
