## 1.2 Vectors and Matrices

<!-- Source: Lecture 01.pdf, p. 3. -->

Write $\mathbb{K}$ for either $\mathbb{R}$, the real numbers, or $\mathbb{C}$, the complex numbers.

> [!definition] Vectors and matrices
> A vector $v\in\mathbb{K}^m$ is an $m$-tuple of elements of $\mathbb{K}$. A matrix $A\in\mathbb{K}^{m\times n}$ is an $m\times n$ array of elements of $\mathbb{K}$.

A vector can represent a position or direction in an $m$-dimensional coordinate system. A matrix can collect $n$ such vectors as its columns. For example,

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
A_{11} & A_{12}\\
A_{21} & A_{22}\\
A_{31} & A_{32}
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}a_1 & a_2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad a_1,a_2\in\mathbb{K}^3.
$$

We identify an $m\times1$ matrix with a vector:

$$
\mathbb{K}^{m\times1}\cong\mathbb{K}^m.
$$

### 1.2.1 Scalar Multiplication and Addition

For $\delta\in\mathbb{K}$ and $A,B\in\mathbb{K}^{m\times n}$, define

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\delta A)_{ij}&=\delta A_{ij},\\
(A+B)_{ij}&=A_{ij}+B_{ij}.
\end{aligned}
$$

### 1.2.2 Matrix–Vector and Matrix–Matrix Products

For

$$
A\in\mathbb{K}^{m\times n},
\qquad v\in\mathbb{K}^n,
\qquad B\in\mathbb{K}^{n\times\ell},
$$

define $Av\in\mathbb{K}^m$ and $AB\in\mathbb{K}^{m\times\ell}$ by

$$
\begin{aligned}
(Av)_i&=\sum_{k=1}^n A_{ik}v_k,\\
(AB)_{ij}&=\sum_{k=1}^n A_{ik}B_{kj}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $A$ represents a linear map

$$
A:\mathbb{K}^n\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}^m,
\qquad v\longmapsto Av.
$$

Applied to a matrix, it acts on each column separately. This is the algebraic interpretation of the sketch in which a vector $x$ is mapped to $Ax$.

## 1.3 Representation on a Computer

<!-- Source: Lecture 01.pdf, p. 4. -->

To store $v\in\mathbb{K}^m$ or $A\in\mathbb{K}^{m\times n}$ in memory, we first need a representation of individual numbers. The underlying storage consists of bits:

$$
\mathbb{B}=\{0,1\}.
$$

### 1.3.1 Unsigned Integers

A bit string $d=(d_1,\ldots,d_b)\in\mathbb{B}^b$ represents an unsigned integer through

$$
\operatorname{UInt}(d)=\sum_{i=1}^b d_i2^{i-1}.
$$

The representable integers range from $0$ to $2^b-1$.

### 1.3.2 Signed Integers

The signed representation on the sheet is

$$
\operatorname{Int}(d)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{b-1}d_i2^{i-1}-d_b2^{b-1}.
$$

The last bit has a negative weight, giving the range

$$
-2^{b-1}\le\operatorname{Int}(d)\le2^{b-1}-1.
$$

### 1.3.3 Fixed-Point Representation

A fixed-point representation rescales the signed integer by a fixed power of two. The example in the lecture is

$$
\operatorname{Fixed}(d)
=
\operatorname{Int}(d)\,2^{-\lfloor b/2\rfloor}.
$$

The spacing between neighboring representable values is fixed. Covering magnitudes from a small $s>0$ to $s^{-1}$ requires a number of bits growing on the order of $\log_2(1/s)$.[^magnitude-range]

### 1.3.4 Floating-Point Representation

Instead of fixing the scale, store the significand and exponent separately. In the lecture's simplified model,

$$
(p,e)\in\mathbb{B}^{b_p}\times\mathbb{B}^{b_e},
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Float}(p,e)
=
\underbrace{(-1)^{p_1}}_{\text{sign}}
\underbrace{\left(1+\sum_{i=2}^{b_p}p_i2^{-(i-1)}\right)}_{\text{significand}}
\underbrace{2^{\operatorname{Int}(e)}}_{\text{scale}}.
}
$$

This is the binary analogue of scientific notation, such as

$$
\pm1.123\times10^{-4}.
$$

The formula describes the normalized numbers in the lecture's model; it is not a complete specification of a hardware floating-point format.

A complex number $z\in\mathbb{C}$ can be represented by two floating-point numbers, one for each of its real and imaginary parts. The sheet points to Mike Overton's book for further details, without giving a title or edition.

## 1.4 Floating-Point Arithmetic

<!-- Source: Lecture 01.pdf, p. 5. -->

The exponent encodes the scale economically: the number of exponent bits needed to cover magnitudes from $s$ to $s^{-1}$ grows on the order of $\log_2\log_2(1/s)$ as $s$ becomes small. The tradeoff is that floating-point numbers are **not equally spaced**.[^magnitude-range]

### 1.4.1 Spacing and Relative Accuracy

In the preceding representation, let

$$
\epsilon=2^{-(b_p-1)}.
$$

The spacing is $\epsilon$ just above $1$, $2\epsilon$ just above $2$, and $4\epsilon$ just above $4$. More generally, the spacing scales with the magnitude of the number.

> [!remark] Relative rather than absolute accuracy
> Floating-point representation targets relative accuracy: a comparable relative perturbation is allowed at different magnitudes, rather than a fixed absolute spacing everywhere.

### 1.4.2 Arithmetic Is Not Associative

Assume rounding to the nearest representable number, with ties rounded upward, as specified in the lecture. Write $\operatorname{fl}$ for this rounding operation. The expression $(1+\epsilon)+1+2$ can give different results depending on its grouping:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{fl}\!\left(
\operatorname{fl}\bigl((1+\epsilon)+1\bigr)+2
\right)
&=\operatorname{fl}\bigl((2+2\epsilon)+2\bigr)\\
&=4+4\epsilon,\\[4pt]
\operatorname{fl}\!\left(
(1+\epsilon)+\operatorname{fl}(1+2)
\right)
&=\operatorname{fl}(4+\epsilon)\\
&=4.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus changing parentheses can change the computed result, even though the corresponding real-arithmetic expressions are identical.

### 1.4.3 Cancellation

Cancellation can turn a small relative error in a large quantity into a large relative error in the result. The lecture's example is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(10^6+1)(1+\epsilon)-10^6
&=\underbrace{(10^6+1)-10^6}_{1}
  +(10^6+1)\epsilon\\
&=1+(10^6+1)\epsilon\\
&\approx1+10^6\epsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$

The exact unperturbed result is $1$, while the perturbation in the result is approximately $10^6\epsilon$. The last line is an approximation, not the exact equality written on the sheet.[^cancellation]

## 1.5 Conditioning

<!-- Source: Lecture 01.pdf, p. 6. -->

Consider the problem of computing

$$
y=f(x),\qquad f:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}.
$$

The input is represented by a perturbed value $\widehat{x}$, with a small relative error

$$
\frac{|\widehat{x}-x|}{|x|}\le\epsilon.
$$

Even exact evaluation at that perturbed input produces $\widehat{y}=f(\widehat{x})$ rather than necessarily $f(x)$. How much can the relative input error be amplified?

For $x\ne0$, $f(x)\ne0$, and $\widehat{x}\ne x$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\displaystyle\frac{|\widehat{y}-y|}{|y|}}
     {\displaystyle\frac{|\widehat{x}-x|}{|x|}}
&=
\frac{|f(\widehat{x})-f(x)|}{|\widehat{x}-x|}
\frac{|x|}{|f(x)|}\\
&\longrightarrow
\frac{|x|\,|f'(x)|}{|f(x)|}
\qquad\text{as }\widehat{x}\to x.
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!definition] Relative condition number
> For a differentiable scalar function $f$, at a point with $x\ne0$ and $f(x)\ne0$, the relative condition number used in the lecture is
> $$
> \mathcal{K}_f(x)=\frac{|x|\,|f'(x)|}{|f(x)|}.
> $$
> It describes the local amplification of relative input perturbations by the problem itself.

**Conditioning depends on the problem, not on the algorithm.**

### 1.5.1 Example: Subtracting a Large Constant

Let

$$
f(x)=x-10^6.
$$

At $x=10^6+1$, we have $f(x)=1$ and $f'(x)=1$, so

$$
\mathcal{K}_f(10^6+1)
=
\frac{\underbrace{|10^6+1|}_{\text{input magnitude}}\,|1|}
     {\underbrace{|1|}_{\text{output magnitude}}}
=10^6+1\gg1.
$$

The sensitivity associated with cancellation is already present in the mathematical problem at this input.

## 1.6 Stability

<!-- Source: Lecture 01.pdf, p. 6. -->

Now let $\widehat{f}$ denote the actual algorithm, and write

$$
\widehat{y}=\widehat{f}(\widehat{x}).
$$

The lecture describes an algorithm as stable when it does not amplify perturbations unnecessarily beyond the sensitivity of the underlying problem. Its informal comparison is

$$
\frac{\displaystyle\frac{|\widehat{y}-y|}{|y|}}
     {\displaystyle\frac{|\widehat{x}-x|}{|x|}}
\approx\mathcal{K}_f(x).
$$

This is the lecture's schematic forward-error description, rather than a general formal definition covering every rounding-error model.

> [!remark] Conditioning versus stability
> Conditioning asks how sensitive the problem is. Stability asks whether the chosen algorithm introduces avoidable additional sensitivity.

### 1.6.1 Example: An Unnecessarily Sensitive Algorithm

For the well-conditioned problem

$$
f(x)=x,
$$

consider evaluating it instead through

$$
\widehat{f}(x)=(x+10^6)-10^6.
$$

The two expressions agree in exact arithmetic. In floating-point arithmetic, the intermediate addition can lose information about a small $x$, and the final subtraction does not recover it. The algorithm has introduced a cancellation problem that was absent from $f(x)=x$ itself.
