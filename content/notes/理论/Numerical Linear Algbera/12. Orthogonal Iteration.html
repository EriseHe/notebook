12. Orthogonal Iteration

2026-10-19

Numerical Methods I — Florian Schaefer. Source: Lecture 12.pdf, pp. 1–16.

As in Lecture 10, \(\lambda_i\) denotes the eigenvalue written \(\delta_i\) on the source sheet. We use \(^*\) for conjugate transpose; on real matrices it is \(^T\).

12.1 Recap: Deflation and Schur Factorization

For a normalized eigenvector \(x_1\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\), rank-one deflation removes the known eigenpair:

\[ A-\lambda_1\frac{x_1x_1^*}{x_1^*x_1}. \]

The previous lecture motivated subspace iteration: multiplication of a single vector by \(A\) finds at most one dominant eigenvector, whereas several eigenvectors require an invariant subspace. For a diagonalizable matrix, \(A=X\Lambda X^{-1}\). A defective matrix may have too few eigenvectors; its Jordan blocks need not be diagonal. The source draws a Jordan block as a triangular matrix with an eigenvalue repeated on its diagonal and ones immediately above it. The smallest example is

\[ J=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}; \qquad J_0=J\big|_{\lambda=0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}, \quad J_0^2=0,\ J_0\ne0. \]

The Schur factorization is

\[ A=QTQ^*, \]

where \(Q\) is unitary and \(T\) is upper triangular over \(\mathbb C\), with the eigenvalues on its diagonal.

12.2 When Is the Schur Form Diagonal?

Definition. \(A\) is normal if \(AA^*=A^*A\).

Theorem. \(A\) is normal if and only if it has an orthonormal eigenbasis. Equivalently, its complex Schur factor \(T\) is diagonal. If \(A=QTQ^*\), then

\[ QTT^*Q^*=AA^*=A^*A=QT^*TQ^*, \qquad TT^*=T^*T. \]

Compare the \((1,1)\) entries. Because \(T\) is upper triangular,

\[ (TT^*)_{11}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}|T_{1j}|^2, \qquad (T^*T)_{11}=|T_{11}|^2. \]

Thus \(T_{1j}=0\) for every \(j>1\). Repeating on the trailing triangular block zeros all strict upper-triangular entries. Conversely, \(A=Q\Lambda Q^*\) gives \(AA^*=A^*A\).

12.3 Orthogonal Iteration for Two Eigenpairs

Suppose the eigenvalues are ordered by magnitude, \(|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|>\cdots\), and we want two eigenpairs at once. Applying power iteration independently to random vectors \(q_1,q_2\) generally sends both toward \(x_1\). Keep the second iterate orthogonal to the first. One step is

\[ \begin{aligned} q_1&\leftarrow Aq_1,&q_2&\leftarrow Aq_2,\\ q_2&\leftarrow\left(I-\frac{q_1q_1^*}{q_1^*q_1}\right)q_2,\\ q_1&\leftarrow\frac{q_1}{\|q_1\|},& q_2&\leftarrow\frac{q_2}{\|q_2\|}. \end{aligned} \]

The projection leaves \(q_1\) unchanged, so after \(k\) iterations,

\[ q_1^{(k)}=\frac{A^kq_1^{(0)}}{\|A^kq_1^{(0)}\|} \longrightarrow x_1 \]

up to a phase or sign, provided the initial vector has a nonzero \(x_1\) component. Once \(q_1\approx x_1\), the direction of the second update is approximately

\[ q_2^{(k)}\approx (I-x_1x_1^*)Aq_2^{(k-1)}. \]

For normal \(A\), \(x_1\) is also a left eigenvector, and hence

\[ \begin{aligned} (I-x_1x_1^*)A(I-x_1x_1^*) &=A-2\lambda_1x_1x_1^*+\lambda_1x_1x_1^*\\ &=A-\lambda_1x_1x_1^*. \end{aligned} \]

This is the deflation from the previous lecture. For any diagonalizable \(A\), let \(P=I-x_1x_1^*\) and \(Ax_i=\lambda_i x_i\). Since \(Px_1=0\) and \(x_1^*x_1=1\),

\[ \begin{aligned} PAPx_i &=P A(x_i-x_1x_1^*x_i)\\ &=P(\lambda_i x_i-\lambda_1x_1x_1^*x_i)\\ &=\lambda_i Px_i. \end{aligned} \]

Thus the eigenpairs of \(PAP\) include \((0,x_1)\) and \((\lambda_i,Px_i)\) for \(i\ge2\) when \(Px_i\ne0\). In particular, the second iterate tends to the normalized direction \(Px_2\), not generally to \(x_2\). Nevertheless \(\operatorname{span}(x_1,Px_2)=\operatorname{span}(x_1,x_2)\), so the two-dimensional subspace tends to \(\operatorname{span}(x_1,x_2)\) under the spectral-gap and nondegenerate-start assumptions.

To estimate eigenvalues, let \(Q_k=[q_1^{(k)},q_2^{(k)}]\) and form the \(2\times2\) matrix

\[ T_k=Q_k^*AQ_k= \begin{pmatrix} q_1^*Aq_1&q_1^*Aq_2\\ q_2^*Aq_1&q_2^*Aq_2 \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&*\\0&\lambda_2\end{pmatrix}. \]

As \(q_1\to x_1\), its lower-left entry satisfies

\[ q_2^*Aq_1\approx q_2^*Ax_1 =\lambda_1q_2^*x_1\approx0. \]

The eigenvalues of this nearly triangular \(T_k\) approximate \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\). If \(Q_kv_i\approx x_i\), then

\[ Q_k^*AQ_kv_i\approx Q_k^*Ax_i =\lambda_iQ_k^*x_i \approx\lambda_i Q_k^*Q_kv_i =\lambda_i v_i. \]

This is the Rayleigh–Ritz calculation on the iterated subspace.

12.4 Orthogonal Iteration in General

To compute \(r>2\) eigenpairs, start with an \(m\times r\) matrix \(Q_0\) with orthonormal columns. Multiply all columns by \(A\), then reorthogonalize by QR factorization:

for k in 1:max_iter
    Z = A * Q
    Q, R = qr(Z)
end

Here \(Q_k^*Q_k=I_r\). The QR step prevents all columns from collapsing onto one dominant eigenvector. The eigenvalues of \(Q_k^*AQ_k\) approximate the leading eigenvalues of \(A\).

12.5 Eigenvector Estimates and the Schur Form

For a Hermitian \(A\), distinct eigenvectors are orthogonal. Under magnitude gaps and initial-overlap assumptions, each column of orthogonal iteration tends to the corresponding eigenvector, up to a phase or sign. For a nonsymmetric matrix, eigenvectors need not be mutually orthogonal. Suppose

\[ A=X\Lambda X^{-1}, \qquad X=[x_1\mid\cdots\mid x_m]. \]

Take the QR factorization of this eigenvector matrix, \(X=Q^{X}R^{X}\). Orthogonal iteration tends to the orthogonalized eigenvector basis \(Q^{X}\), up to column phases. The first column satisfies \(q_1\to q_1^{X}=x_1/\|x_1\|\), while

\[ \operatorname{span}(q_1^{(k)},q_2^{(k)}) \longrightarrow \operatorname{span}(x_1,x_2) =\operatorname{span}(q_1^{X},q_2^{X}). \]

Both \(q_2^{(k)}\) and \(q_2^{X}\) lie in that two-dimensional space and are orthogonal to \(q_1^{X}\), so they agree up to phase in the limit. Column by column, the source repeats the same argument:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{span}(x_1,\ldots,x_i) &=\operatorname{span}(q_1^{(k)},\ldots,q_i^{(k)})\quad(k\to\infty)\\ &=\operatorname{span}(q_1^X,\ldots,q_i^X),\\ q_i^{(k)}&\perp\operatorname{span}(q_1^{(k)},\ldots,q_{i-1}^{(k)}),\\ q_i^X&\perp\operatorname{span}(q_1^X,\ldots,q_{i-1}^X). \end{aligned} \]

Hence the \(i\)th iterated column tends, up to phase, to the unique orthogonal direction added when the invariant subspace grows from dimension \(i-1\) to \(i\).

Since \(Q^{X}=X(R^{X})^{-1}\) and \((Q^{X})^*=R^{X}X^{-1}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} (Q^{X})^*AQ^{X} &=R^{X}X^{-1}(X\Lambda X^{-1})X(R^{X})^{-1}\\ &=R^{X}\Lambda(R^{X})^{-1}. \end{aligned} \]

This is upper triangular because \(R^{X}\) and \((R^{X})^{-1}\) are upper triangular. Its diagonal contains \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m\), and its eigenvectors are \(R^{X}e_i\). Thus \(Q_k\) and \(Q_k^*AQ_k\) approach a Schur factorization, up to phase conventions, and the eigenvalues can be read from the diagonal.

12.6 From Orthogonal Iteration to Basic QR Iteration

Forming \(T_k=Q_k^*AQ_k\) directly would require both \(Q_k\) and another multiplication with \(A\). The orthogonal iteration step supplies an update. For the full square case,

\[ AQ_k=Q_{k+1}R_{k+1}. \]

Let \(U_{k+1}=Q_k^*Q_{k+1}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} T_k=Q_k^*AQ_k &=(Q_k^*Q_{k+1})R_{k+1} =U_{k+1}R_{k+1},\\ T_{k+1}=Q_{k+1}^*AQ_{k+1} &=R_{k+1}U_{k+1}. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(T_k\) is factored as QR, and the next iterate is obtained by reversing those factors. The basic QR iteration does not need to store \(Q_k\):

T = copy(A)
for k in 1:max_iter
    U, R = qr(T)
    T = R * U
end
return diag(T)

To recover the Schur vectors, accumulate the orthogonal factors: \(Q_0=I\) and \(Q_{k+1}=Q_kU_{k+1}\). Because \(U_{k+1}=Q_k^*Q_{k+1}\),

\[ U_1U_2\cdots U_{k+1} =Q_0^*Q_1Q_1^*Q_2\cdots Q_k^*Q_{k+1} =Q_{k+1}. \]

The \(U_i\) are successive orthogonal coordinate changes that take the initial basis toward the Schur basis.

12.7 Why Preprocess to Hessenberg Form?

A dense QR factorization costs \(O(m^3)\). If \(O(m)\) QR steps are needed, unstructured iteration can cost \(O(m^4)\). First reduce \(A\) by orthogonal similarity to upper Hessenberg form \(H\), whose entries below the first subdiagonal are zero:

\[ H=Q^*AQ, \qquad H_{ij}=0\quad(i>j+1). \]

The source constructs this reduction with Householder reflections. Choose \(P_1\) to map the subvector \(A[2:m,1]\) to a multiple of \(e_1\), and embed it as

\[ Q_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&P_1\end{pmatrix}. \]

Apply \(Q_1^*AQ_1\): left multiplication zeros the entries below the first subdiagonal in column 1. Right multiplication performs the same similarity transformation while preserving those zeros. Continue on the trailing \((m-2)\times(m-2)\) block for column 2 and on later trailing blocks for subsequent columns. If \(Q=Q_1Q_2\cdots Q_{m-2}\), the result is \(H=Q^*AQ\).

For a four-row sketch, the two-sided transformations have the pattern

\[ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} *&*&*&*\\ *&*&*&*\\ *&*&*&*\\ *&*&*&* \end{pmatrix}}_{A} \xrightarrow{\ Q_1^*(\cdot)Q_1\ } \begin{pmatrix} *&*&*&*\\ *&*&*&*\\ 0&*&*&*\\ 0&*&*&* \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{\ Q_2^*(\cdot)Q_2\ } \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} *&*&*&*\\ *&*&*&*\\ 0&*&*&*\\ 0&0&*&* \end{pmatrix}}_{H}. \]

Here \(Q_2=\operatorname{diag}(I_2,P_2)\) acts only on the final two coordinates; larger matrices continue the staircase. The page asks why the same process cannot generally zero the first subdiagonal as well. It invokes Abel’s theorem: exact triangularization of arbitrary matrices by a fixed finite sequence of arithmetic and radical operations would expose characteristic roots, which in general have no radical formula from degree five onward. Numerical QR iteration can nevertheless converge to the Schur form. Hessenberg form is the inexpensive starting shape.

12.8 QR Iteration on a Hessenberg Matrix

In the first part of a QR step, factor the current upper Hessenberg \(T_{k-1}\) with Givens rotations \(G_1,\ldots,G_{m-1}\). Only one entry below the diagonal must be removed in each column, so

\[ R_k=G_{m-1}^*\cdots G_1^*T_{k-1}, \qquad U_k=G_1\cdots G_{m-1}, \qquad T_{k-1}=U_kR_k. \]

Each rotation acts on two rows. There are \(m-1\) rotations, and each costs \(O(m)\), so the QR factorization costs \(O(m^2)\) rather than \(O(m^3)\). Then form

\[ T_k=R_kU_k=R_kG_1\cdots G_{m-1} \]

in another \(O(m^2)\). Right multiplication by the rotations, in order, preserves the Hessenberg staircase shown on page 14. Thus \(O(m)\) QR steps cost \(O(m^3)\) after preprocessing.

The two halves of the step have these structural patterns; \(\bullet\) marks a possible nonzero entry and \(0\) an enforced zero:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \bullet&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ \bullet&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ 0&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ 0&0&\bullet&\bullet \end{pmatrix}}_{T_{k-1}\text{ upper Hessenberg}} \xrightarrow{\ G_{m-1}^*\cdots G_1^*\ } \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \bullet&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ 0&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ 0&0&\bullet&\bullet\\ 0&0&0&\bullet \end{pmatrix}}_{R_k} \xrightarrow{\ (\cdot)G_1\cdots G_{m-1}\ } \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \bullet&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ \bullet&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ 0&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ 0&0&\bullet&\bullet \end{pmatrix}}_{T_k}. \]

The lecture then asks whether the left and right rotations can be applied simultaneously. The first interleaved similarity, \(G_1^*T_{k-1}G_1\), has a small out-of-place entry near the upper-left of the Hessenberg staircase, called a bulge. The second rotation must be chosen from this bulged matrix, not merely from the unreduced \(R_k\); it removes that entry while moving the bulge one position down the off-diagonal. Denote these interleaved rotations by \(\widehat G_j\), with \(\widehat G_1=G_1\). The successive matrices are

\[ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \bullet&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ \bullet&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ \boxed{\bullet}&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ 0&0&\bullet&\bullet \end{pmatrix}}_{B_1:\,\text{bulge at }(3,1)} \xrightarrow{\ \widehat G_2^*(\cdot)\widehat G_2\ } \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \bullet&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ \bullet&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ 0&\bullet&\bullet&\bullet\\ 0&\boxed{\bullet}&\bullet&\bullet \end{pmatrix}}_{B_2:\,\text{bulge at }(4,2)}. \]

For larger matrices the boxed entry continues down the off-diagonal until the last rotation removes it. Algebraically,

\[ \begin{aligned} B_1&=\widehat G_1^*T_{k-1}\widehat G_1,\\ B_2&=\widehat G_2^*B_1\widehat G_2 =\widehat G_2^*\widehat G_1^*T_{k-1}\widehat G_1\widehat G_2,\\ &\ \vdots\\ B_{m-1}&=\widehat G_{m-1}^*\cdots\widehat G_1^* T_{k-1}\widehat G_1\cdots\widehat G_{m-1}. \end{aligned} \]

The final matrix has its Hessenberg structure restored. The source says one can show it equals the QR iterate \(T_k=R_kU_k\), and leaves the precise choice of each next bulge-removing rotation as an algorithmic question.

12.9 Corrections and Clarifications to the Source

  • Page 2: The upper-triangular normal-matrix argument uses conjugate transpose and absolute squares in general. For real matrices they reduce to the sheet’s transposes and squares.
  • Pages 3–5: The identity \(PAP=A-\lambda_1x_1x_1^*\) requires normality, but \(PAPx_i=\lambda_iPx_i\) holds for a general diagonalizable \(A\). The second vector tends to the projected direction \(Px_2\) rather than to \(x_2\) itself.
  • Page 7: Ordered-column convergence needs eigenvalue magnitude gaps and a nondegenerate initial subspace; column phases or signs may vary.
  • Page 9: From \((Q^{X})^*AQ^{X}=R^{X}\Lambda(R^{X})^{-1}\), its eigenvectors are \(R^{X}e_i\), not \((R^{X})^{-1}e_i\) as written on the sheet.
  • Page 10: The update is expressed with \(T_k=U_{k+1}R_{k+1}\) and \(T_{k+1}=R_{k+1}U_{k+1}\) to keep the factors’ iteration indices consistent.
  • Page 13: The Householder product order is explicit: \(H=Q^*AQ\) with \(Q=Q_1\cdots Q_{m-2}\).
  • Pages 15–16: The sheet poses the interleaved bulge chase as an open question. The displayed \(B_j\) recurrence spells out its product order; the equality with \(R_kU_k\) is stated as in the source, not assumed from the rotation symbols.
