9. Cholesky Factorization and Schur Complements

2026-10-05

Numerical Methods I — Florian Schaefer. Source: Lecture 09.pdf, pp. 1–8; p. 9 is blank.

9.1 Recap

For the overdetermined least-squares problem

\[ \min_x\|Ax-b\|_2^2, \]

the optimality condition is

\[ \langle A\varepsilon,Ax-b\rangle=0 \quad\text{for every }\varepsilon. \]

Thus the residual is orthogonal to the column space, and

\[ \langle A\varepsilon,Ax\rangle =\langle A\varepsilon,b\rangle \quad\forall\varepsilon \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A^*Ax=A^*b. \]

The coefficient matrix \(A^*A\) is a Gram matrix. The preceding lecture established that Hermitian positive semidefinite matrices are exactly the matrices of the form \(B^*B\), and that \(B^*B\) is positive definite when \(B\) has full column rank.

We now seek a triangular factorization that uses this symmetry and positivity. Throughout, \(*\) denotes conjugate transpose; for real matrices it is transpose.

9.2 Symmetry-Preserving Elimination

Let \(A\) be Hermitian positive definite. Can its Gram representation be chosen with a triangular factor? We will construct a lower-triangular \(L\) such that \(A=LL^*\); equivalently, \(L^*\) is an upper-triangular Gram factor.

Write the first row and column separately:

\[ A= \begin{pmatrix} a&w^*\\ w&C \end{pmatrix}, \qquad a=A_{11}>0. \]

9.2.1 Eliminate the First Column

The usual Gaussian elimination step uses

\[ E_1= \begin{pmatrix} 1&0\\ -w/a&I \end{pmatrix}. \]

Multiplying out the blocks gives

\[ \begin{aligned} E_1A &= \begin{pmatrix} a&w^*\\ (-w/a)a+w&(-w/a)w^*+C \end{pmatrix}\\[3pt] &= \begin{pmatrix} a&w^*\\ 0&C-ww^*/a \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

The first column is now triangular, but this row operation alone has destroyed the symmetry.

9.2.2 Add the Matching Column Operation

Multiply on the right by the conjugate transpose of the same elimination matrix:

\[ E_1^*= \begin{pmatrix} 1&-w^*/a\\ 0&I \end{pmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} E_1AE_1^* &= \begin{pmatrix} a&w^*\\ 0&C-ww^*/a \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1&-w^*/a\\ 0&I \end{pmatrix}\\[3pt] &= \begin{pmatrix} a&-w^*+w^*\\ 0&C-ww^*/a \end{pmatrix}\\[3pt] &= \boxed{\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\0&S\end{pmatrix}}, \qquad S=C-\frac{ww^*}{a}. \end{aligned} \]

The row and column operations eliminate both off-diagonal blocks and leave a Hermitian trailing matrix. In the lecture’s entry notation,

\[ S=A_{2:m,\,2:m} -\frac{A_{2:m,\,1}A_{1,\,2:m}}{A_{11}}. \]

9.3 Cholesky Factorization

9.3.1 Normalize the Pivot

Normalize the first coordinate of the transformed matrix by \(\sqrt a\). Set

\[ D_1=\begin{pmatrix}a^{-1/2}&0\\0&I\end{pmatrix}, \qquad F_1=D_1E_1. \]

The preceding calculation becomes

\[ \begin{aligned} F_1AF_1^* &=D_1(E_1AE_1^*)D_1^*\\ &= \begin{pmatrix}a^{-1/2}&0\\0&I\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}a&0\\0&S\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}a^{-1/2}&0\\0&I\end{pmatrix}\\ &=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&S\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

To recover a factor of \(A\), invert the normalized elimination matrix:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_1^{-1}&=E_1^{-1}D_1^{-1}\\ &=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\w/a&I\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt a&0\\0&I\end{pmatrix}\\ &=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt a&0\\w/\sqrt a&I\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

Apply the same procedure recursively to \(S\). If \(S=L_{22}L_{22}^*\), then

\[ \begin{aligned} A &=F_1^{-1} \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&L_{22}L_{22}^*\end{pmatrix} (F_1^{-1})^*\\[3pt] &= \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt a&0\\w/\sqrt a&L_{22}\end{pmatrix}}_L \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt a&w^*/\sqrt a\\0&L_{22}^*\end{pmatrix}}_{L^*}. \end{aligned} \]

DefinitionCholesky factorization

A factorization \[ A=LL^* \] with \(L\) lower triangular and positive real diagonal entries is the Cholesky factorization of a Hermitian positive definite matrix.

The differences from ordinary LU are the square-root normalization of each pivot and the need to retain only one triangular factor. The algorithm can operate entirely on the lower triangle.

9.3.2 Right-Looking Algorithm

At step \(k\), the current diagonal entry is the next pivot. Take its square root, divide the entries below it by that value, and subtract the resulting outer product from the trailing matrix.

The following Julia-style loops overwrite the lower triangle of a Hermitian positive definite floating-point array \(A\) with \(L\):

m = size(A, 1)

for k in 1:m
    d = sqrt(real(A[k, k]))
    A[k, k] = d

    for i in (k + 1):m
        A[i, k] /= d
    end

    for j in (k + 1):m
        for i in j:m
            A[i, j] -= A[i, k] * conj(A[j, k])
        end
    end
end

The update uses the newly computed column \(L_{:,k}\). Its contribution is subtracted from every still-active entry in the lower triangle. The upper triangle is not used.

9.4 Left-Looking and Up-Looking Cholesky

The same factorization can be organized around different memory-access patterns.

9.4.1 Left-Looking Version

To compute column \(j\), look at the completed columns \(1,\ldots,j-1\). Subtract their contributions from the current column, then normalize it:

m = size(A, 1)

for j in 1:m
    for k in 1:(j - 1)
        for i in j:m
            A[i, j] -= A[i, k] * conj(A[j, k])
        end
    end

    d = sqrt(real(A[j, j]))
    A[j, j] = d
    for i in (j + 1):m
        A[i, j] /= d
    end
end

Before normalization, the entries of this column are

\[ A_{ij}-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}L_{ik}\overline{L_{jk}}, \qquad i=j,\ldots,m, \]

where \(A\) in this formula denotes the original matrix. For \(i=j\), this quantity is the square of \(L_{jj}\); for \(i>j\), it is \(L_{ij}L_{jj}\).

9.4.2 Up-Looking Version

Compute row \(i\) from left to right. For \(j<i\), the entrywise identity \(A=LL^*\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} A_{ij} &=\sum_{k=1}^{j}L_{ik}\overline{L_{jk}}\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}L_{ik}\overline{L_{jk}}+L_{ij}L_{jj}, \end{aligned} \]

because \(L_{jj}\) is real and positive. Therefore

\[ L_{ij} =\frac{A_{ij}-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}L_{ik}\overline{L_{jk}}}{L_{jj}}, \qquad j<i. \]

For the diagonal,

\[ \begin{gathered} A_{ii}=\sum_{k=1}^{i}|L_{ik}|^2\\ \Longrightarrow\quad L_{ii}=\sqrt{A_{ii}-\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}|L_{ik}|^2}. \end{gathered} \]

These are the off-diagonal and diagonal cases of the row-oriented loop:

m = size(A, 1)

for i in 1:m
    for j in 1:i
        for k in 1:(j - 1)
            A[i, j] -= A[i, k] * conj(A[j, k])
        end

        if j < i
            A[i, j] /= A[j, j]
        else
            A[i, i] = sqrt(real(A[i, i]))
        end
    end
end

9.4.3 Storing the Upper Factor

A row of the lower factor becomes a column of the upper factor \(U=L^*\). Storing \(U\) therefore makes the entries involved in an up-looking row calculation available down a column.

The lecture illustrates packed upper-triangular storage by numbering the entries column by column. For a \(6\times6\) factor, the storage positions are

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 1&2&4&7&11&16\\ &3&5&8&12&17\\ & &6&9&13&18\\ & & &10&14&19\\ & & & &15&20\\ & & & & &21 \end{pmatrix}. \]

For example, the consecutive positions \(7,8,9,10\) hold \(U_{14},U_{24},U_{34},U_{44}\). The blank lower triangle is not stored. This changes the storage and traversal, while retaining the same Cholesky factorization.

9.5 Why the Pivots Stay Positive

Ordinary LU without pivoting can encounter a zero pivot. Cholesky needs more: each pivot must be real and strictly positive so that its square root can be used for normalization.

TheoremExistence of Cholesky factorization

If \(A\) is Hermitian positive definite, Cholesky factorization without pivoting encounters only real, strictly positive pivots.

We begin with the lemma used in the lecture.

LemmaCongruence preserves positive definiteness

If \(A\) is Hermitian positive definite and \(B\) is nonsingular, then \(B^*AB\) is Hermitian positive definite.

Proof. First,

\[ (B^*AB)^*=B^*A^*B=B^*AB. \]

For \(x\ne0\), nonsingularity gives \(Bx\ne0\). Hence

\[ x^*B^*ABx=(Bx)^*A(Bx)>0. \]

Proof of the theorem. Initially, \(A_{11}=e_1^*Ae_1>0\), so the first elimination step is defined. After \(k\) steps, let \(E_1,\ldots,E_k\) be the corresponding nonsingular elimination matrices and set

\[ \begin{gathered} T_k=E_k\cdots E_1,\\ S^{(k)}=T_kAT_k^* =E_k\cdots E_1AE_1^*\cdots E_k^*. \end{gathered} \]

Apply the lemma with \(B=T_k^*\). The matrix \(S^{(k)}\) remains Hermitian positive definite. Hermitian symmetry makes its next diagonal entry real; positive definiteness makes that entry strictly positive:

\[ \begin{aligned} S^{(k)}_{k+1,k+1} &=\overline{S^{(k)}_{k+1,k+1}},\\ S^{(k)}_{k+1,k+1} &=e_{k+1}^*S^{(k)}e_{k+1}>0. \end{aligned} \]

The next pivot is therefore real and positive, so elimination can continue. This proves the assertion at every step.

9.5.1 Quantitative Pivot Bound

The lecture also states a lower bound for the pivots. With the spectral norm and with \(s_{kk}\) denoting the pivot before taking its square root,

\[ \boxed{s_{kk}\ge\frac1{\|A^{-1}\|_2}.} \]

The corresponding entry of the Cholesky factor is \(L_{kk}=\sqrt{s_{kk}}\), so

\[ L_{kk}\ge\frac1{\sqrt{\|A^{-1}\|_2}}. \]

9.6 Roundoff and Element Growth

The LU error analysis involved the product of entrywise absolute values \(|L||U|\). For Cholesky, \(U=L^*\), so the relevant product is \(|L||L|^T\).

For each pair of indices \(i,j\), Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\[ \begin{aligned} (|L||L|^T)_{ij} &=\sum_k|L_{ik}|\,|L_{jk}|\\ &\le \underbrace{\left(\sum_k|L_{ik}|^2\right)^{1/2}}_{\text{norm of row }i} \underbrace{\left(\sum_k|L_{jk}|^2\right)^{1/2}}_{\text{norm of row }j}\\ &=\sqrt{(LL^*)_{ii}}\sqrt{(LL^*)_{jj}}\\ &=\sqrt{A_{ii}}\sqrt{A_{jj}}\\ &\le\max_\ell A_{\ell\ell}\\ &\le\max_{p,q}|A_{pq}|. \end{aligned} \]

Thus the product governing the error estimate is controlled by the entries of \(A\). This is the structural advantage over the large intermediate growth possible in general LU factorization.

9.7 Symmetric Indefinite Matrices

For a real symmetric matrix that is not positive definite, one can use

\[ PAP^T=LDL^T, \]

where \(P\) is a permutation matrix and \(D\) is block diagonal, with \(1\times1\) and \(2\times2\) blocks.

The example in the lecture is

\[ A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}. \]

Both diagonal entries are zero, so no scalar diagonal pivot is available. Treating the whole \(2\times2\) matrix as a pivot block avoids this obstruction.

9.8 Block Cholesky and Schur Complements

Let \(A=LL^*\) and partition the factors conformably:

\[ A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\A_{21}&A_{22}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad L=\begin{pmatrix}L_{11}&0\\L_{21}&L_{22}\end{pmatrix}. \]

Block multiplication yields

\[ \begin{aligned} LL^* &=\begin{pmatrix}L_{11}&0\\L_{21}&L_{22}\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}L_{11}^*&L_{21}^*\\0&L_{22}^*\end{pmatrix}\\ &=\begin{pmatrix} L_{11}L_{11}^*&L_{11}L_{21}^*\\ L_{21}L_{11}^*&L_{21}L_{21}^*+L_{22}L_{22}^* \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} A_{11}&=L_{11}L_{11}^*,\\ A_{21}&=L_{21}L_{11}^*,\\ A_{22}&=L_{21}L_{21}^*+L_{22}L_{22}^*. \end{aligned} \]

From the second identity,

\[ L_{21}=A_{21}(L_{11}^*)^{-1}. \]

Using \(A_{12}=A_{21}^*\) and \((L_{11}L_{11}^*)^{-1}=(L_{11}^*)^{-1}L_{11}^{-1}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} L_{21}L_{21}^* &=A_{21}(L_{11}^*)^{-1}L_{11}^{-1}A_{12}\\ &=A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}. \end{aligned} \]

Substituting into the third block identity gives

\[ \boxed{L_{22}L_{22}^*=A_{22}-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}.} \]

DefinitionSchur complement

The matrix \[ S=A_{22}-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12} \] is the Schur complement of \(A_{11}\) in \(A\).

It is the trailing matrix left after eliminating the first block. The scalar-pivot calculation in Section 9.2 is the same construction with \(A_{11}=a\).

9.9 The Block-Inverse Identity

Assume \(A\) and \(A_{11}\) are invertible, and write

\[ \widehat A=A^{-1} =\begin{pmatrix}\widehat A_{11}&\widehat A_{12}\\ \widehat A_{21}&\widehat A_{22}\end{pmatrix}. \]

LemmaInvert, take the submatrix, invert

For \(S=A_{22}-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}\), \[ \widehat A_{22}=S^{-1}, \qquad S=(\widehat A_{22})^{-1}. \]

Proof by block LU. The block factorization indicated in the lecture is

\[ A= \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}}_{\mathcal L} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\0&S\end{pmatrix}}_{\mathcal U}. \]

Since \(A\) and \(\mathcal L\) are invertible, \(\mathcal U\) is invertible, and so is its diagonal block \(S\). Invert the factors in reverse order:

\[ \begin{aligned} A^{-1} &=\mathcal U^{-1}\mathcal L^{-1}\\[2pt] &=\begin{pmatrix} A_{11}^{-1}&-A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}S^{-1}\\ 0&S^{-1} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}I&0\\-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

The lower-right block of this product is \(0\cdot0+S^{-1}I=S^{-1}\), which proves the identity.

9.10 Cholesky and QR

Let \(B=QR\) be a reduced QR factorization of a full-column-rank matrix. Since \(Q^*Q=I\),

\[ \begin{aligned} B^*B &=(QR)^*(QR)\\ &=R^*Q^*QR\\ &=R^*R. \end{aligned} \]

With the positive real diagonal convention, \(R\) is the upper Cholesky factor of \(B^*B\). In lower-triangular form, the Cholesky factor is \(L=R^*\).

9.11 Corrections to the Handwritten Notes

  • P. 2 — Elimination signs and factor orientation. The displayed row elimination requires \(-A_{i1}/A_{11}\), although the handwritten matrix shows positive multipliers. Also, a lower Cholesky factor satisfies \(A=LL^*\); in the Gram convention \(A=B^*B\), the corresponding factor is \(B=L^*\), which is upper triangular.
  • P. 3 — Normalizing the transformation. To obtain \(\operatorname{diag}(1,S)\) from \(E_1AE_1^*\), use \(F_1=D_1E_1\) and \(F_1AF_1^*\). The handwritten instruction to divide a column of the elimination matrix is replaced by this explicit congruence; the recovered Cholesky column is \((\sqrt a,w/\sqrt a)^T\).
  • Pp. 3–4 — Algorithm indices and stored diagonals. Only the active part of a column is normalized. Cache its square-root pivot before overwriting the diagonal. In the up-looking loop, the inner sum ends at \(j-1\), not \(i-1\); an off-diagonal entry is divided by the already computed \(L_{jj}\), not \(\sqrt{L_{jj}}\); the diagonal itself is obtained by a square root. Complex updates require \(L_{ik}\overline{L_{jk}}\). The use of real in the loops assumes Hermitian positive definite input.
  • P. 4 — Packed-storage numbering. The diagram above uses a complete \(6\times6\) upper triangle, with consecutive column-by-column positions \(1,\ldots,21\). The later numbers in the handwritten sketch do not consistently count that full triangular pattern.
  • Pp. 5–6 — Positive definiteness. The theorem is labeled “PSD,” but strictly positive pivots require positive definiteness. The congruence proof uses this strict hypothesis throughout.
  • P. 5 — Pivot bound. The stated lower bound needs the reciprocal \(\|A^{-1}\|_2^{-1}\). This is the bound for the elimination pivot before taking a square root; the Cholesky diagonal has the corresponding square-root bound.
  • P. 6 — Roundoff calculation. The matrix-product entry is a sum, \(\sum_k|L_{ik}||L_{jk}|\). Over \(\mathbb C\), the row-norm squares are \(\sum_k|L_{ik}|^2\).
  • P. 7 — Block-factor order. The introductory \(A=L^*L\) is changed to \(A=LL^*\), which is the convention used by all the subsequent lower-triangular block equations.
  • P. 8 — Inverse block. The inverse matrix’s lower-right block equals \(S^{-1}\), not \(S\). Thus the Schur complement is obtained by inverting that block, as stated in the lecture’s “invert, take submatrix, invert” description.
