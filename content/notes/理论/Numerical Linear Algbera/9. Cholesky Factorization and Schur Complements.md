## 9.1 Recap

*Source: Lecture 09.pdf, p. 1.*

[[8. Least Squares and Normal Equations|The preceding lecture]] derived least-squares optimality as orthogonality of $Ax-b$ to the column space of $A$, obtained the normal equations $A^*Ax=A^*b$, and characterized Hermitian positive semidefinite matrices as Gram matrices.

Now we exploit the structure of a Hermitian positive definite matrix to obtain a triangular factorization using only one triangular half.

## 9.2 Symmetry-Preserving Elimination

*Source: Lecture 09.pdf, p. 2.*

Partition a Hermitian positive definite matrix as

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}a&w^*\\w&C\end{pmatrix},\qquad a>0.
$$

Ordinary elimination removes the entries below $a$. Applying a matching column operation preserves Hermitian symmetry:

$$
E=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-w/a&I\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
EAE^*=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\0&C-ww^*/a\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The minus signs in $E$ are required to obtain the subtraction shown in the source's trailing-block update.[^elimination-sign]

The remaining matrix

$$
S=C-\frac{ww^*}{a}
$$

is the Schur complement of the pivot block.

## 9.3 Cholesky Factorization

*Source: Lecture 09.pdf, p. 3.*

Normalize the pivot by its positive square root. Recursively factoring $S=L_{22}L_{22}^*$ yields

$$
\boxed{A=LL^*,}\qquad
L=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt a&0\\w/\sqrt a&L_{22}\end{pmatrix}.
$$

This is the **Cholesky factorization**. In contrast with ordinary LU:

- The pivot normalization uses a square root
- Only one triangular factor needs to be stored
- Hermitian symmetry allows the work to be restricted to one triangle

### 9.3.1 Right-Looking In-Place Algorithm

The following is an implementation-oriented transcription of the sheet, with its indexing, diagonal update, and complex conjugation made explicit. The input is assumed to be Hermitian positive definite and stored in a floating-point array. The lower triangle is overwritten by $L$.

```julia
n = size(A, 1)

for k in 1:n
    d = sqrt(real(A[k, k]))
    A[k, k] = d

    for i in (k + 1):n
        A[i, k] /= d
    end

    for j in (k + 1):n
        for i in j:n
            A[i, j] -= A[i, k] * conj(A[j, k])
        end
    end
end
```

The positive-pivot and Hermitian assumptions must be checked if this is used as general-purpose code; taking the real part is not permission to silently accept a non-Hermitian input. The upper triangle remains unused/stale after this lower-triangle algorithm.

## 9.4 Left-Looking and Up-Looking Cholesky

*Source: Lecture 09.pdf, p. 4.*

Cholesky can be organized around different memory-access patterns.

### 9.4.1 Left-Looking Version

For column $j$, subtract contributions from the previously computed columns, then normalize:

```julia
n = size(A, 1)

for j in 1:n
    for k in 1:(j - 1)
        for i in j:n
            A[i, j] -= A[i, k] * conj(A[j, k])
        end
    end

    d = sqrt(real(A[j, j]))
    A[j, j] = d
    for i in (j + 1):n
        A[i, j] /= d
    end
end
```

Caching $d$ before overwriting the diagonal is necessary. Otherwise a literal scalar-loop reading of the handwritten code changes the denominator midway through the column.[^algorithm-clarifications]

### 9.4.2 Up-Looking Version

Computing row $i$ from previously available entries gives

$$
L_{ij}=\frac{A_{ij}-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}L_{ik}\overline{L_{jk}}}{L_{jj}},
\qquad j<i,
$$

followed by

$$
L_{ii}=\sqrt{A_{ii}-\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}|L_{ik}|^2}.
$$

An upper-triangular/transposed storage arrangement can make this traversal more convenient in column-oriented memory. The source also sketches packed upper-triangular storage. These storage choices change traversal, not the underlying factorization.

The separate diagonal and off-diagonal formulas resolve the sheet's compact denominator notation: an already computed diagonal entry is $L_{jj}$, so an off-diagonal entry is divided by $L_{jj}$, not by another square root of it.[^algorithm-clarifications]

## 9.5 Why the Pivots Stay Positive

*Source: Lecture 09.pdf, pp. 5–6.*

> [!lemma] Congruence preserves positive definiteness
> If $A$ is Hermitian positive definite and $B$ is nonsingular, then $B^*AB$ is Hermitian positive definite.

Indeed, for $x\ne0$, nonsingularity gives $Bx\ne0$, and

$$
x^*B^*ABx=(Bx)^*A(Bx)>0.
$$

Symmetry-preserving elimination is such a congruence. Its transformed matrix remains positive definite, so the next diagonal pivot is real and positive:

$$
s_{kk}=e_k^*Se_k>0.
$$

> [!theorem] Existence without pivoting
> A Hermitian positive definite matrix admits Cholesky factorization without pivoting, with strictly positive real diagonal entries in $L$.

The handwritten theorem says “PSD”; strict positive definiteness is needed for the stated positive-pivot division algorithm.[^spd]

### 9.5.1 Quantitative Pivot Bound

The intended reciprocal bound can be stated precisely as

$$
s_{kk}\ge\lambda_{\min}(A)=\frac1{\|A^{-1}\|_2},
\qquad
L_{kk}\ge\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(A)}.
$$

Here $s_{kk}$ is the unnormalized elimination pivot and $L_{kk}$ is its square root. The handwritten line lacks the reciprocal and does not distinguish the two quantities.[^pivot-bound]

An editorial completion using the block-inverse identity below proves the bound. The inverse of a trailing Schur complement $S$ is a principal block of $A^{-1}$, so

$$
\|S^{-1}\|_2\le\|A^{-1}\|_2.
$$

Therefore $\lambda_{\min}(S)\ge\lambda_{\min}(A)$, and any diagonal entry of $S$ is at least $\lambda_{\min}(S)$.

## 9.6 Roundoff and Element Growth

*Source: Lecture 09.pdf, p. 6.*

Earlier LU error analysis involved the product of entrywise absolute values $|L||U|$. For Cholesky, $U=L^*$, so the corresponding nonnegative product is $|L||L|^T$.

Cauchy–Schwarz gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
(|L||L|^T)_{ij}
&=\sum_k|L_{ik}||L_{jk}|\\
&\le\sqrt{\sum_k|L_{ik}|^2}\sqrt{\sum_k|L_{jk}|^2}\\
&=\sqrt{A_{ii}A_{jj}}\\
&\le\max_\ell A_{\ell\ell}
\le\max_{p,q}|A_{pq}|.
\end{aligned}
$$

The matrix-product expansion uses a sum, and the complex case requires squared absolute values.[^growth]

This explains why the large factor growth that can occur in general elimination is controlled for positive definite Cholesky. It is not a claim that every arbitrarily ill-conditioned positive definite matrix can be factored successfully in finite precision.

## 9.7 Symmetric Indefinite Matrices

*Source: Lecture 09.pdf, p. 7.*

For real symmetric matrices that are not positive definite, a factorization of the form

$$
PAP^T=LDL^T
$$

can use a block-diagonal $D$ with $1\times1$ and $2\times2$ blocks. The example

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

shows why a $2\times2$ pivot block is useful: there is no nonzero scalar diagonal pivot to start with. The lecture mentions this structure without specifying a complete pivot-selection algorithm.

## 9.8 Block Cholesky and Schur Complements

*Source: Lecture 09.pdf, p. 7.*

Let $A$ be Hermitian positive definite, partitioned as

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\A_{21}&A_{22}\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
L=\begin{pmatrix}L_{11}&0\\L_{21}&L_{22}\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Using $A=LL^*$,[^factor-order]

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{11}&=L_{11}L_{11}^*,\\
A_{21}&=L_{21}L_{11}^*,\\
A_{22}&=L_{21}L_{21}^*+L_{22}L_{22}^*.
\end{aligned}
$$

Writing $L_{11}^{-*}=(L_{11}^*)^{-1}$, we have $L_{21}=A_{21}L_{11}^{-*}$ and

$$
L_{21}L_{21}^*=A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12},
$$

so

$$
\boxed{S=A_{22}-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}=L_{22}L_{22}^*.}
$$

The matrix $S$ is the **Schur complement of $A_{11}$ in $A$**.

## 9.9 The Block-Inverse Identity

*Source: Lecture 09.pdf, p. 8.*

Assume $A$ and $A_{11}$ are invertible. Then the Schur complement is invertible and

$$
\boxed{(A^{-1})_{22}=S^{-1},\qquad S=((A^{-1})_{22})^{-1}.}
$$

This is the lecture's phrase **“invert, take submatrix, invert”** made explicit.[^block-inverse]

The sheet proposes a proof by block LU. Completing that calculation,

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\0&S\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Inverting the two factors in reverse order gives

$$
A^{-1}=
\begin{pmatrix}
A_{11}^{-1}&-A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}S^{-1}\\
0&S^{-1}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The lower-right block is $S^{-1}$, as claimed.

## 9.10 Cholesky and QR

*Source: Lecture 09.pdf, p. 8.*

Let $B=QR$ be a reduced QR factorization of a full-column-rank matrix, with $Q^*Q=I$. Then

$$
B^*B=R^*Q^*QR=R^*R.
$$

Thus, with positive real diagonal convention, $R$ is the upper Cholesky factor of $B^*B$. The lower-factor convention is $L=R^*$.

This is an algebraic connection, not a reason to ignore the squared-conditioning disadvantage of forming the Gram matrix.
