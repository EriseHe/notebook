14. Hessenberg QR and Bulge Chasing

Numerical Methods I — Florian Schaefer. Source: Lecture 14.pdf, pp. 1–7.

14.1 Recap: Schur Factorization and Orthogonal Iteration

The preceding lecture connected the eigenvalue decomposition, orthogonal iteration, and Schur factorization. If \(A=X\Lambda X^{-1}\) and \(X=QR\), then

\[ A=Q(R\Lambda R^{-1})Q^*, \]

where \(R\Lambda R^{-1}\) is upper triangular. For orthogonal iteration, \(A^kQ_0=Q_k(R_k\cdots R_1)\), so the columns of \(Q_k\) span the columns of \(A^kQ_0\). For the first \(p\) columns, denoted \(\bar Q_k\),

\[ \operatorname{span}(\bar Q_{k+1}) =\operatorname{span}(A\bar Q_k) =\operatorname{span}(A^{k+1}\bar Q_0). \]

In eigenvector coordinates,

\[ A^i\bar Q_0 =X\Lambda^iX^{-1}\bar Q_0 =\lambda_p^iX\operatorname{diag}\!\left( (\lambda_1/\lambda_p)^i,\ldots,(\lambda_m/\lambda_p)^i \right)X^{-1}\bar Q_0. \]

Assuming \(|\lambda_1|\ge\cdots\ge|\lambda_p|>|\lambda_{p+1}|\ge\cdots\), the ratios for \(j>p\) tend to zero. With the nonsingular starting overlap from Lecture 13, \(\operatorname{span}(\bar Q_k)\) approaches \(\operatorname{span}(x_1,\ldots,x_p)\). Writing the orthogonal basis as \(Q_k=(\bar Q_k,\widetilde Q_k)\) gives the block picture

\[ Q_k^*AQ_k= \begin{pmatrix} \bar Q_k^*A\bar Q_k&\bar Q_k^*A\widetilde Q_k\\ \widetilde Q_k^*A\bar Q_k&\widetilde Q_k^*A\widetilde Q_k \end{pmatrix}. \]

As \(\operatorname{span}(\bar Q_k)\) approaches an invariant eigenspace, \(\operatorname{span}(A\bar Q_k)\) approaches that same space. The lower-left block therefore approaches zero, giving the upper-triangular form of a Schur factorization.

The basic QR iteration starts from \(T_0=A\) and repeats

\[ T_{k-1}=U_kR_k, \qquad T_k=R_kU_k. \]

The QR factors are denoted by \(U_k,R_k\) to distinguish them from the accumulated orthogonal basis. After enough iterations, the diagonal of \(T_k\) approximates the eigenvalues.

T = A
for k in 1:max_iter
    U, R = qr(T)
    T = R * U
end
return diag(T)

14.2 Why a Direct QR Step Is Expensive

Computing a dense QR factorization afresh at every step can cost \(O(m^3)\) per iteration. Up to \(m\) successive eigenvalue deflations may be needed, so even if each takes only \(O(1)\) iterations, this can total \(O(m^4)\). The idea is to preprocess \(A\) so that each later QR factorization is cheaper.

Find an orthogonal \(Q\) such that

\[ H=Q^*AQ \]

is upper Hessenberg: \(H_{ij}=0\) whenever \(i>j+1\). Only its first subdiagonal can be nonzero below the diagonal. We now study QR iteration on this \(H\).

14.3 Reduction to Upper Hessenberg Form

Use Householder reflections to reduce one column at a time. First choose \(P_1\in\mathbb K^{(m-1)\times(m-1)}\) mapping the lower part of the first column to a multiple of its first coordinate vector:

\[ P_1A[2\!:\!m,1]=\alpha_1e_1, \qquad Q_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&P_1\end{pmatrix}. \]

The source takes \(\alpha_1=\|v\|\) for \(v=A[2\!:\!m,1]\); a sign or phase choice gives the same zero pattern. With a self-adjoint Householder reflector \(P_1\), the left-then-right matrix pictures are

\[ \begin{aligned} \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&P_1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}a&r\\v&B\end{pmatrix} &=\begin{pmatrix}a&r\\\alpha_1e_1&P_1B\end{pmatrix},\\ \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&P_1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}a&r\\v&B\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&P_1^*\end{pmatrix} &=\begin{pmatrix}a&rP_1^*\\\alpha_1e_1&P_1BP_1^*\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

Thus the similarity zeros entries \(3,\ldots,m\) of column 1. The multiplication on the right preserves those zeros because \(Q_1\) fixes the first coordinate. Repeat on the trailing submatrix, acting on coordinates \(3,\ldots,m\) to zero entries below the first subdiagonal of column 2, then continue. With \(Q=Q_1Q_2\cdots Q_{m-2}\),

\[ Q^*AQ=H, \qquad H_{ij}=0\quad(i>j+1). \]

The source asks why the same construction does not make the matrix upper triangular directly, citing Abel’s theorem. Exact triangularization by a fixed finite sequence of arithmetic and radical operations would expose arbitrary polynomial roots; a general radical formula does not exist from degree five onward. Here the reflector preserves the norm of \(v\) and cannot also zero its remaining subdiagonal entry unless \(v=0\). Hessenberg reduction leaves the eigenvalue computation to the subsequent numerical QR iteration.

14.4 QR Iteration on a Hessenberg Matrix

For a Hessenberg iterate \(T_{k-1}\), compute its QR factorization with \(m-1\) adjacent Givens rotations:

\[ T_{k-1}=U_kR_k, \qquad U_k=G_1G_2\cdots G_{m-1}, \qquad R_k=G_{m-1}^*\cdots G_2^*G_1^*T_{k-1}. \]

Each rotation zeros the next subdiagonal entry. Its action on the matrix costs \(O(m)\), so this factorization costs \(O(m^2)\). The second half of the QR step is

\[ T_k=R_kU_k=R_kG_1G_2\cdots G_{m-1}. \]

It costs another \(O(m^2)\). Thus \(m\) steps cost \(O(m^3)\) after the initial Hessenberg reduction. The matrix \(T_k\) is again upper Hessenberg; the next two pages explain this through the movement of a small bulge below the first subdiagonal.

14.5 Bulge Chasing

If all the left rotations are applied first, they make \(R_k\) upper triangular. The remaining product \(R_kG_1\cdots G_{m-1}\) is upper Hessenberg: successive right rotations of an upper-triangular matrix introduce only the allowed first-subdiagonal entries. The bulge arises when the left and right rotations are interleaved on the Hessenberg matrix \(T_{k-1}\).

The first similarity uses \(G_1\) on the right and \(G_1^*\) on the left:

\[ G_1^*T_{k-1}G_1. \]

The right multiplication mixes columns 1 and 2, so the nonzero at position \((3,2)\) can enter position \((3,1)\); the left multiplication of rows 1 and 2 does not remove it. This is the first bulge below the Hessenberg band. Schematically, with \(\ast\) marking a possible nonzero,

\[ T_{k-1}\sim \begin{pmatrix} \ast&\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ \ast&\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ 0&\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ 0&0&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ 0&0&0&\ast&\ast \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{\ G_1^*(\cdot)G_1\ } \begin{pmatrix} \ast&\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ \ast&\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ \boxed{\ast}&\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ 0&0&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ 0&0&0&\ast&\ast \end{pmatrix}. \]

Choose \(G_2\) to remove this bulge by mixing rows 2 and 3 on the left. Its matching right multiplication mixes columns 2 and 3 and produces the next bulge at \((4,2)\). The next stage has the pattern

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \ast&\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ \ast&\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ 0&\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ 0&\boxed{\ast}&\ast&\ast&\ast\\ 0&0&0&\ast&\ast \end{pmatrix}. \]

Repeating with \(G_3,G_4,\ldots\) chases the bulge down and right until it leaves the matrix, restoring upper Hessenberg form. Each paired operation is a unitary similarity, so the eigenvalues are preserved.

The point left open in the lecture is how to select the next rotation from the current bulged matrix, rather than requiring the complete \(R_k\) in advance. The implicit \(Q\) theorem supplies the needed uniqueness principle.

14.6 The Implicit \(Q\) Theorem

Theorem. Assume \(Q\) is orthogonal and

\[ Q^*AQ=H \]

is unreduced upper Hessenberg, meaning every first-subdiagonal entry \(H_{i+1,i}\) is nonzero. Then columns \(2,\ldots,m\) of \(Q\) are uniquely determined by its first column up to signs.

The proof is given at the start of Lecture 15. This is why the first column is enough to identify the orthogonal similarity that an implicit QR step must reproduce.

14.7 Corrections and Clarifications to the Source

  • Page 3: Made the left-then-right block products and accumulated factor order explicit. The Abel remark concerns exact finite radical formulas, not numerical convergence.
  • Pages 4–6: Made the Givens factor order explicit and distinguished the complete \(RQ\) product from interleaved similarities. The source’s diagrams show that a bulge appears in the interleaved construction; ordinary postmultiplication of triangular \(R_k\) does not create an entry below the Hessenberg band.
  • Page 7: “Unreduced” means every first-subdiagonal entry is nonzero. The theorem statement in the source says this as “not block upper triangular.”
