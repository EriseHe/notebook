“How do proposals for US medical interventions into China in the mid-twentieth century enact/materialize gender?”

Goal: Position your research (question/archival object) in existing scholarly conversations

Chose 5 peer-reviewed sources

  • Describe how you searched for these sources
  • Write a paragraph for each source that includes
  1. Why you chose this source vs. others
  2. BRIEF summary of this source (no more than 3 sentences)
  3. Discussion of how this source is useful in helping you to
  • Situate your work in context of existing scholarly debates, and/or
  • Answer/reframe your research question, and/or
  • Better understanding your archival object

Should be between 1000-1500 words, MS word, Chicago Style Citation, Due March 18 on Canvas

  1. Record citation
  2. Read actively

Research Question

Literature Engagement Paper (Draft)

The archival object—the 1945 “Conference on Medical and Health Work in China” recommendations—may suggest that four overlapping issues require careful examination: missionary medicine in China, the professionalization of nursing, the medicalization of childbirth and midwifery, and the relationship between public health and colonial or modernizing power. Moreover, the significant evidence could indicate that peer-reviewed monographs from university presses and peer-reviewed journal articles provide the most relevant foundation, given that these works appear substantial enough to frame a final project rather than short encyclopedia-style summaries. Furthermore, the key results might demonstrate that combinations of keywords such as “missionary medicine China nursing,” “midwifery Republican China,” “public health China modernity,” “women missionaries nursing China,” and “medical modernization China” across scholarly search tools and publisher or journal pages could support this approach. In light of these findings, the five sources below may indicate that together they move from broad context to narrow relevance: from medical modernity in China generally, to hygiene and public health, to childbirth and midwifery, to missionary nursing, and finally to a Chinese woman medical missionary whose work could demonstrate important questions of gender and professionalization. Sources show movement broad to narrow. (De Gruyter Brill)

  1. Bridie Andrews, The Making of Modern Chinese Medicine, 1850–1960

The findings from Andrews’s book may suggest that it provides the broadest historical map of the medical world that produced this object. However, the significant evidence could indicate that rather than treating “Western” medicine as simply replacing “Chinese” medicine, Andrews appears to demonstrate that medicine in China remained highly pluralistic well into the modern period. Additionally, the key results might suggest that both missionaries and Chinese reformers adapted and translated medical knowledge rather than simply importing or rejecting it, which could demonstrate that the argument proves useful. Given that the conference document might otherwise appear too linear—as if missionary planners simply designed a system that China then absorbed—the findings may indicate that Andrews helps resist that oversimplification. Andrews shows medicine emerged through negotiation, reform, selective appropriation—not one-way transfer. (De Gruyter Brill)

This source supports my project in two important ways. Moreover, it situates my object within a longer debate over whether missionary medicine could indicate colonial imposition, collaborative reform, or a hybrid process. Furthermore, the evidence may suggest that the recommendations on nursing schools, medical colleges, and public health centers demonstrate that these institutional forms appear to reflect a larger struggle over what would count as “modern” medicine in China. Given that Andrews directly addresses the relationship between missionaries, modernizing states, and institutional medical reform, the findings might indicate that this same triangle structures my 1945 conference document. Source supports project clearly.

  1. Ruth Rogaski, Hygienic Modernity: Meanings of Health and Disease in Treaty-Port China

However, the significant evidence may suggest that my object is not only about hospitals and personnel; it could also demonstrate a new language of public health, prevention, and social medicine. In light of Rogaski’s key findings, the results might indicate that weisheng (hygiene/health) changed meaning under imperial pressure, shifting from older Chinese regimens of self-cultivation and bodily care toward modern public health, sanitary regulation, laboratory knowledge, and racial/national fitness. Nevertheless, the important evidence may suggest that this conceptual history appears extremely helpful for my project because the conference recommendations repeatedly use seemingly neutral terms such as “preventive,” “social,” and “public health.” Thus, the findings could indicate that Rogaski demonstrates that this vocabulary was historically loaded and deeply entangled with modernity, sovereignty, and imperial hierarchy. Vocabulary shows colonial weight.

Additionally, the significant source may suggest that it reframes my question from “How did missionaries improve health?” to “What kind of health order did missionaries help normalize?” Notwithstanding the administrative tone of the object, the evidence could demonstrate that the conference’s recommendations appear to be part of a broader hygienic project that made populations legible through standards and institutions. Moreover, the key findings might indicate that Rogaski’s work could help connect medicine to colonial discourse and state-making, which appears important if the results are to demonstrate that missionary health planning had political consequences beyond hospital walls. Furthermore, the evidence may suggest that this source provides important context for understanding how seemingly neutral institutional recommendations could indicate deeper assumptions about modernity and sovereignty. Rogaski links medicine to power.

  1. Tina Johnson, Childbirth in Republican China: Delivering Modernity

Johnson’s book may suggest that it stands as the most significant source for the midwifery side of my project, given that my object explicitly calls for “trained midwives” and proposes building midwifery teaching and administrative capacity across China’s regions. Moreover, the findings from Johnson’s work could indicate that the medicalization of childbirth in modern China began under Euro-American missionary influence and was then pursued more systematically by both Nationalist and Communist governments. Furthermore, the evidence may demonstrate that her work shows how reproduction became tied to modern state-building, maternal and child health, and changing definitions of women’s status. In light of these significant findings, the results might indicate that this makes Johnson an ideal companion to my object, which only gestures toward midwifery in a few lines but clearly treats it as a field requiring training, administration, and standardization. Johnson helps answer research question by showing why midwifery matters conceptually. (Bloomsbury Publishing)

The evidence may suggest that Johnson helps me answer my research question by showing why midwifery matters conceptually. However, the significant results could indicate that the conference recommendations are not only about professional labor but are also about who gets to manage childbirth and under what authority. Additionally, the key findings might demonstrate that I chose Johnson over broader women’s history books because she directly links gender, medicine, and state power. Given that her work could indicate that overclaiming remains a risk, the evidence may suggest that instead of saying my 1945 document “caused” later reproductive governance, I can use Johnson to argue more carefully that missionary planning was part of a longer medicalization of childbirth that states later expanded. Work links gender, medicine, state power directly. (Bloomsbury Publishing)

Notwithstanding the Canadian rather than U.S. focus, the significant evidence may suggest that I chose Grypma’s book because it keeps missionary work, nursing, gender, and wartime China in the same frame. Moreover, the key findings could indicate that the book explicitly argues that scholarship on Protestant missions in China has often neglected nurses and nursing, and it reconstructs how missionary nurses shaped healthcare in Henan over six decades. Furthermore, the results might demonstrate that this perspective is exactly what my object needs, given that without a source like Grypma the conference recommendations risk looking abstract. Therefore, the evidence may suggest that Grypma helps me see what was at stake in making nursing schools separate institutions, raising standards, and defining what counted as “modern nursing.” Grypma shows nursing, gender, wartime China connected. (UBC Press)

This source demonstrates that feminist dimensions of the project could indicate significant gaps in conventional analysis. Moreover, the object may suggest that technical language carefully reorganizes feminized care labor in ways the evidence might indicate are deliberate. Furthermore, Grypma could demonstrate that nursing as missionary work, nursing as modern profession, and nursing as a site where gender, religion, and empire intersect provides the historical and gendered flesh behind those recommendations. In light of these significant findings, the study may suggest that Grypma offers a richer missionary context extending to 1947, close to the moment of the 1945 object, rather than short surveys of Chinese nursing history. Source extends to 1947. Connie Shemo’s article could indicate that a simple ‘Western missionaries imposed nursing on China’ story may suggest important oversimplification. However, the article centers Shi Meiyu, a Chinese woman medical missionary who ran a nursing school in Jiujiang from 1896 to 1920, and the evidence might indicate that this focus reveals significant complexity. Moreover, the findings may suggest that Shi reframed Chinese women not as passive victims needing rescue but as potential skilled healers, even as the results could demonstrate that she sought funding to align her school with increasingly scientific medicine. Given that the evidence demonstrates this article brings together gendered labor and institutional medical modernization, the study may suggest that Shemo’s work appears particularly valuable for the key question. Shemo links agency to professionalization. Nevertheless, the object’s conference language may suggest that nurses and midwives could demonstrate vulnerability to administrative flattening into categories, and the findings might indicate that Shemo’s article helps reinsert female agency. Thus, the results could suggest that pressures toward professionalization and standardization remain important to the evidence, and the study may indicate that Shemo appears more directly relevant than general mission biographies. In light of significant findings on nursing schools and public health work, the evidence could demonstrate that the tension between women’s empowerment and institutional control may suggest a key analytical framework. Article shows roles contested.

These five sources may suggest that a usable scholarly framework emerges for the archival object under consideration. Moreover, the significant findings from Andrews and Rogaski could indicate that the conference may be situated within broader debates about medical modernity, hygiene, and colonial public-health power. Furthermore, the evidence from Johnson might demonstrate that the conference’s brief references to midwifery appear critical for understanding reproduction and state-building in this period. In light of these results, the data from Grypma could suggest that nursing becomes visible as missionary and gendered labor within the key institutional frameworks examined here. Nursing labor links missionary work and gender. However, the important findings from Shemo may indicate that the project should avoid reducing that labor to a one-way imposition from the West, given that the evidence supports a more nuanced interpretive approach. Additionally, the significant results from these sources together could demonstrate that the 1945 conference recommendations appear to reflect not neutral administrative planning but a historical struggle over legitimate knowledge and women’s work. Thus, the key evidence may suggest that health and reproduction became governable in modern China through institutions shaped by these contested forces. Evidence shows knowledge, labor, institutions interconnected. Notwithstanding these findings, the evidence could indicate that the framework established here appears to support further analysis of how women’s work and health governance developed across this critical period in Chinese medical history.
